## Trigonometric Ratios

## Quadrantal Angles

In a coordinate plane the two axes divide the plane in four equivalent parts called quadrants. If terminal ray of an angle in standard position coincides with any axis, then it is called quadrantal angle. The measure of a quadrantal angle is multiple of $90^{\circ}$.
For example; $0^{\circ}, 90^{\circ}, 180^{\circ}, 270^{\circ}, 360^{\circ}, 450^{\circ}, \ldots$ are quadrantal angles.
In radian measure quadrantal angle is a multiple of $\frac{\pi}{2}$.
For example; $0, \frac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \frac{3 \pi}{2}, 2 \pi, \frac{5 \pi}{2}, 3 \pi, \ldots$ are quadrantal angles.

## Trigonometric Ratios

The ratio between any two sides of a right-angled triangle is called trigonometric ratio.

## Kevisaction

Two angles having same terminal ray in standard position, are called coterminal angles.

In triangle $A B C, \angle C=90^{\circ}$ and $\angle A=\theta$.
With respect to acute angle $\theta, \mathrm{BC}=a$ is length of perpendicular, $\mathrm{AC}=b$ is length of base and $\mathrm{AB}=c$ is length of hypotenuse.
Various trigonometric ratios are defined as:

- The ratio of perpendicular and hypotenuse is sine of $\theta$ denoted by $\sin \theta$.

$$
\therefore \sin \theta=\frac{\text { Perpendicular }}{\text { Hypotenuse }}=\frac{a}{c}
$$

- The ratio of base and hypotenuse is cosine of $\theta$ denoted by $\cos \theta$.

$$
\therefore \cos \theta=\frac{\text { Base }}{\text { Hypotenuse }}=\frac{b}{c}
$$

- The ratio of perpendicular and base is tangent of $\theta$ denoted by $\tan \theta$.

$$
\therefore \tan \theta=\frac{\text { Perpendicular }}{\text { Base }}=\frac{a}{b}
$$

- Furthermore, $\operatorname{cosec} \theta=\frac{\text { Hypotenuse }}{\text { Perpendicular }}=\frac{c}{a}=\frac{1}{\sin \theta}, \quad \sec \theta=\frac{\text { Hypotenuse }}{\text { Base }}=\frac{c}{b}=\frac{1}{\cos \theta}$ and

$$
\cot \theta=\frac{\text { Base }}{\text { Perpendicular }}=\frac{b}{a}=\frac{1}{\tan \theta}
$$

## Trigonometric Ratios with the help of Unit Circle

A circle whose radius is 1 unit is called a unit circle.
Consider a unit circle with centre O .
Take a point $\mathrm{P}(x, y)$ on the circle. Join P with O .
Draw PC perpendicular to x-axis.
Let $\mathrm{OC}=x, \mathrm{PC}=y$ and $\mathrm{OP}=1$ (unit circle).
If $\angle \mathrm{COP}=\theta$ where $0<\theta<180^{\circ}$, then:

$$
\begin{equation*}
\cos \theta=\frac{x}{1} \text { and } \sin \theta=\frac{y}{1} \tag{i}
\end{equation*}
$$


Other trigonometric ratios can be defined as:
$\tan \theta=\frac{y}{x}, \cot \theta=\frac{x}{y}, \sec \theta=\frac{1}{x}$ and $\operatorname{cosec} \theta=\frac{1}{y}$
Now from (i), we have:

$$
x=\cos \theta \text { and } y=\sin \theta
$$

So, $\mathrm{P}(x, y)=\mathrm{P}(\cos \theta, \sin \theta)$.
∴ P is a point on a unit circle with centre O and $\angle C O P=\theta$, then coordinates of P are:
$(\cos \theta, \sin \theta)$.

## Trigonometric Ratios between $\mathbf{9 0}^{\circ}$ and $\mathbf{1 8 0}^{\circ}$

We have discussed finding the sine and cosine for angles in the first quadrant, but what if our angle is in second quadrant $\left(90^{\circ}<\theta<180^{\circ}\right)$ ?
In the diagram, $R(1,0)$ and $P(a, b)$ are points on the unit circle. Under a reflection in the y -axis, the image of $P(a, b)$ is $P^{\prime}(-a, b)$ and the image of $R(1,0)$ is $R^{\prime}(-1,0)$.
Since angle measure is preserved under a line reflection,
$\therefore \quad \angle R O P=\angle R^{\prime} O P^{\prime}$
Also $\angle R O P^{\prime}$ and $\angle R^{\prime} O P^{\prime}$ are supplementary angles.

$$
\begin{equation*}
\therefore \quad \angle R O P^{\prime}+\angle R^{\prime} O P^{\prime}=180^{\circ} \tag{i}
\end{equation*}
$$

But, $\angle R^{\prime} O P^{\prime}=\angle R O P$

Therefore, (i) becomes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \angle R O P^{\prime}+\angle R O P=180^{\circ} \\
& \angle R O P=180^{\circ}-\angle R O P^{\prime}=180^{\circ}-\theta
\end{aligned}
$$

Case 1: If $\angle R O P^{\prime}=\theta$, then:
$\cos \theta=-a$ and $\sin \theta=b \ldots$. (ii) (using the properties of unit circle)
Case 2: If $\angle R O P=180^{\circ}-\theta$, then:

$$
\begin{equation*}
\cos \left(180^{\circ}-\theta\right)=a \text { and } \sin \left(180^{\circ}-\theta\right)=b \tag{iii}
\end{equation*}
$$

Comparing (ii) and (iii), we get:
$\cos \left(180^{\circ}-\theta\right)=-\cos \theta$ and $\sin \left(180^{\circ}-\theta\right)=\sin \theta$
As, $\frac{\sin \left(180^{\circ}-\theta\right)}{\cos \left(180^{\circ}-\theta\right)}=\tan \left(180^{\circ}-\theta\right)$ and $\frac{\sin \theta}{-\cos \theta}=-\tan \theta$
$\therefore \tan \left(180^{\circ}-\theta\right)=-\tan \theta$

To find sine and cosine of angle lying between $90^{\circ}$ and $180^{\circ}$, proceed as follows:
a. Measure the angle between the terminal side of the given angle and the horizontal axis. This is the reference angle.
b. Determine the values of the cosine and sine of the reference angle.
c. Give the cosine the same sign as the $x$-values in the quadrant of the original angle.
d. Give the sine the same sign as the $y$-values in the quadrant of the original angle.

## Oblique Triangles

An oblique triangle is any triangle that is not a right triangle. It could be an acute triangle or it could be an obtuse triangle.

Acute Triangle

Obtuse Triangle

The Pythagoras Theorem along with trigonometric ratios allow us to easily handle any given right triangle problem, but what if the triangle is not a right triangle i.e. the triangle is oblique?
To solve oblique triangles, we use certain laws known as "law of cosines", "law of sines" and "law of tangents".

## Law of Cosines

When we know the measures of two sides and the included angle of a triangle (SAS), the size and shape of the triangle are determined. Therefore, we should be able to find the measure of the third side of the triangle. Sometimes three sides of oblique triangles are given (SSS) and we have to find the three angles of triangle. Such cases are easily handled by using law of cosines.

## Statement:

If $a, b, c$ are three sides and $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are three angles of an oblique triangle with usual notation, then law of cosines is stated as follows:

$$
\begin{align*}
& a^{2}=b^{2}+c^{2}-2 b c \cos \alpha  \tag{1}\\
& b^{2}=a^{2}+c^{2}-2 a c \cos \beta  \tag{2}\\
& c^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}-2 a b \cos \gamma \tag{3}
\end{align*}
$$

Proof: Consider an acute angled triangle ABC with usual notations as shown in the figure.
Draw $\overline{\mathrm{AD}} \perp \overline{\mathrm{BC}}$.
In right $\triangle \mathrm{ABD}$,

$$
\begin{align*}
& \frac{\mathrm{BD}}{\mathrm{AB}}=\cos \beta \\
& \Rightarrow \mathrm{BD}=\mathrm{AB} \cos \beta=c \cos \beta \tag{i}
\end{align*}
$$

Again, $\frac{\mathrm{AD}}{\mathrm{AB}}=\sin \beta$

$$
\begin{equation*}
\Rightarrow \mathrm{AD}=\mathrm{AB} \sin \beta=c \sin \beta . \tag{ii}
\end{equation*}
$$

Now, $\mathrm{CD}=\mathrm{BC}-\mathrm{BD}=a-c \cos \beta$
In right $\triangle A C D$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mathrm{AC})^{2} & =(\mathrm{AD})^{2}+(\mathrm{CD})^{2} \quad \ldots \ldots(\text { Pythagoras Theorem }) \\
b^{2} & =(c \sin \beta)^{2}+(a-c \cos \beta)^{2} \\
b^{2} & =c^{2} \sin ^{2} \beta+a^{2}+c^{2} \cos ^{2} \beta-2 a c \cos \beta \\
b^{2} & =a^{2}+c^{2}\left(\sin ^{2} \beta+\cos ^{2} \beta\right)-2 a c \cos \beta \\
b^{2} & =a^{2}+c^{2}-2 a c \cos \beta
\end{aligned}
$$

Law of cosines is applicable when:

- Two sides and included angle are given.
* Three sides are given.

Similarly, we can prove that:
$a^{2}=b^{2}+c^{2}-2 b c \cos \alpha \quad$ and $\quad c^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}-2 a b \cos \gamma$
From laws of cosines,

$$
\cos \alpha=\frac{b^{2}+c^{2}-a^{2}}{2 b c}, \cos \beta=\frac{a^{2}+c^{2}-b^{2}}{2 a c}, \cos \gamma=\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2}}{2 a b}
$$

These laws are useful when three sides of oblique triangles are given and we have to find their angles.

## Law of Sines

Law of cosines is not applicable when two angles and one side (AAS or ASA) or one angle and two sides (angle not included (SSA)) of an oblique triangle are given. In order to handle such situation, we use another law known as "law of sines".

## Statement:

If $a, b, c$ are three sides and $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are three angles of an oblique triangle with usual notation, then law of sines is stated as follows:

$$
\frac{a}{\sin \alpha}=\frac{b}{\sin \beta}=\frac{c}{\sin \gamma}
$$

Proof: Consider an obtuse triangle ABC as shown in the figure below.
Let $h$ be the height of triangle ABC with respect to base $\overline{\mathrm{AC}}$. In right $\triangle \mathrm{ABD}$,

$$
\begin{align*}
& \frac{\mathrm{BD}}{\mathrm{AB}}=\sin \alpha \\
& \Rightarrow \mathrm{BD}=\mathrm{AB} \sin \alpha=c \sin \alpha \\
& \Rightarrow h=c \sin \alpha \ldots \ldots(i) \tag{i}
\end{align*}
$$

In right $\triangle \mathrm{CBD}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{\mathrm{BD}}{\mathrm{CB}}=\sin (\pi-\gamma) \\
& \Rightarrow \mathrm{BD}=\mathrm{CB} \sin (\pi-\gamma) \\
& \Rightarrow h=a \sin \gamma \ldots \ldots(i i)
\end{aligned}
$$

[as $\sin (\pi-\gamma)=\sin \gamma$ ]

From (i) and (ii), we get:

$$
a \sin \gamma=c \sin c^{c}
$$

Or

$$
\frac{a}{\sin \alpha}=\frac{c}{\sin \gamma}
$$


Similarly, by drawing perpendiculars on other two sides of $\triangle \mathrm{ABC}$, we get:

$$
\frac{a}{\sin \alpha}=\frac{b}{\sin \beta} \text { and } \frac{c}{\sin \gamma}=\frac{b}{\sin \beta}
$$

Combining above results, we obtain the following law known as "law of sines".

$$
\frac{a}{\sin \alpha}=\frac{b}{\sin \beta}=\frac{c}{\sin \gamma} \quad \text { or } \quad \frac{\sin \alpha}{a}=\frac{\sin \beta}{b}=\frac{\sin \gamma}{c}
$$

## To Determine the Number of Solutions in $\mathbf{\triangle} \mathbf{A B C}$

Task: Given measure of sides $a, b$ and angle $\alpha$. Use the law of sines to solve the $\Delta \mathrm{ABC}$ for $\sin \beta$.

## Case-1:

(i) When $\sin \beta>1$, there is no triangle, so no solution.
(ii) When $\sin \beta=1$, there is one right triangle (one solution) if $\alpha$ is acute and no triangle (no solution) if $\alpha$ is obtuse.
Case-2:
When $\alpha$ is acute and $\sin \beta<1$, there are two possible values of $\beta$, acute or obtuse.

$$
0<\beta<90^{\circ} \text { and } \beta^{\prime}=180^{\circ}-\beta
$$

(i) If $\alpha+\beta^{\prime}<180^{\circ}$, there are two possible triangles $\triangle \mathrm{ABC}$ and $\triangle \mathrm{AB}^{\prime} C$.
(ii) If $\alpha+\beta^{\prime} \geq 180^{\circ}$, there is only one $\triangle \mathrm{ABC}$.

Case-3:
When $\alpha$ is obtuse and $\sin \beta<1$, then $\beta$ must be acute.
(i) If $\alpha+\beta<180^{\circ}$, there is only one $\triangle \mathrm{ABC}$.
(ii) If $\alpha+\beta^{\prime} \geq 180^{\circ}$, there is no triangle, so no solution.

$$
\begin{aligned}

& c=\frac{25 \times \sin 44^{\circ} 12^{\prime}}{\sin 66^{\circ} 51^{\prime}}=18.96 \mathrm{~cm}
\end{aligned}
$$

Again, by law of sines,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{b}{\sin \beta}=\frac{a}{\sin \alpha} & \Rightarrow \frac{b}{\sin 68^{\circ} 57^{\prime}}=\frac{25}{\sin 66^{\circ} 51^{\prime}} \\
& \Rightarrow b=\frac{25}{\sin 66^{\circ} 51^{\prime}} \times \sin 68^{\circ} 57^{\prime}=25.37 \mathrm{~cm}

## Law of Tangents

Law of tangents is also used to solve oblique triangles. This law handles the same situations for which the law of cosines is used.

## Statement:

If $a, b, c$ are three sides and $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are three angles of an oblique triangle with usual notation, then law of tangents is stated as follows:

$$
\frac{a+b}{a-b} \cdot \frac{\tan \left(\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\right)}{\tan \left(\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}\right)}, \frac{b+c}{b-c}=\frac{\tan \left(\frac{\beta+\gamma}{2}\right)}{\tan \left(\frac{\beta-\gamma}{2}\right)}, \frac{c+a}{c-a}=\frac{\tan \left(\frac{\gamma+\alpha}{2}\right)}{\tan \left(\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{2}\right)}
$$

## Proof:

For any oblique triangle ABC , the law of sines is given as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{a b}{\sin \alpha}=\frac{}{\sin \beta} \\
& \frac{a}{b}=\frac{\sin \alpha}{\sin \beta}
\end{aligned}
$$

Using componendo and dividendo property, we get:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{a+b}{a-b}=\frac{\sin \alpha+\sin \beta}{\sin \alpha-\sin \beta} \\
& \frac{a+b}{a-b}=\frac{2 \sin \frac{\alpha+\beta}{2} \cos \frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}}{2 \cos \frac{\alpha+\beta}{2} \sin \frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}} \\
& \frac{a+b}{a-b}=\frac{\tan \frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}{\tan \frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

## When Law of Sines and Law of Cosines are Used

The Law of Cosines and the Law of Sines can be used to find the remaining three measures of any triangle when we know the measure of a side and the measures of any two other components (two sides, two angles, one side one angle) of the triangle.

1. Given: Two sides and the included angle.

- Use the Law of Cosines to find the measure of the third side.
- Use the Law of Sines or the Law of Cosines to find the measure of another angle.
- Use the sum of the angles of a triangle to find the measure of the third angle.

2. Given: Three sides.

- Use the Law of Cosines to find the measure of an angle.
- Use the Law of Sines or the Law of Cosines to find the measure of another angle.
- Use the sum of the angles of a triangle to find the measure of the third angle.

3. Given: Two angles and a side.

- Use the sum of the angles of a triangle to find the measure of the third angle.
- Use the Law of Sines to find the remaining sides.

4. Given: Two sides and an angle opposite to one of them

- Use the Law of Sines to find the possible measure(s) of another angle.
- Determine if there are two, one, or no possible triangles.
- If there is a triangle, use the sum of the angles of a triangle to find the measure(s) of the third angle.
- Use the Law of Sines or the Law of Cosines to find the measure(s) of the third side.

## Circhles Connected with Triangles

## (a) Circumcircle

A circle passing through the vertices of any triangle (polygon) is called circumcircle.
The centre of this circle is called the circumcentre and its radius is called the circumradius.
The radius of circumcircle is denoted by $R$.

## (b) Incircle

A circle drawn inside and touching the sides of a triangle (polygon) is called incircle or inscribed circle. It is the largest circle contained in the triangle. The centre of this circle is called the incentre usually denoted by I and its radius is called the inradius denoted by $r$.

## (c) Escribed Circle

A circle touching one of the sides of a triangle externally and the extensions of its two other sides internally, is called an escribed circle (e-circle or ex-circle). The centre of this circle is called the e-centre and its radius is called the e-radius.
If a circle touches extended arms of angle $A$ (circle is drawn opposite to vertex $A$ ) of a triangle ABC , then its centre and radius are respectively denoted by $\mathrm{I}_{1}$ and $r_{1}$. Similarly,
(i) Centre and radius of e-circle opposite to vertex B are denoted by $\mathrm{I}_{2}$ and $r_{2}$.
(ii) Centre and radius of e-circle opposite to vertex C are denoted by $\mathrm{I}_{3}$ and $r_{3}$.

$$
