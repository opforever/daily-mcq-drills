# 15.1 Drug Discovery and Development Process

Pharmacology is the scientific study of the effects of drugs and chemicals on living organisms. A drug is any chemical substance, natural or synthetic, that affects a biological system and is used to diagnose, cure, treat, or prevent disease. Drug therapy relies on pharmacology for continual advancement and on pharmacy for appropriate management.

The drug discovery and development process is a complex, multiphase process that can take several years, or even up to a decade, before a compound reaches patients.

## Drug Discovery

- **Target identification:** the process begins with identifying a new target molecule, a protein or other molecule involved in the disease process.
- **Target validation:** experiments confirm that modulating the target can produce a therapeutic effect.
- **Hit identification:** screening large libraries of compounds to find "hits" that interact with the target.
- **Hit-to-lead optimization:** refining the hits to improve their potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic properties.

## Preclinical Development

- **In vitro studies:** testing the lead compounds in a laboratory setting to evaluate their effects on cells and biological systems.
- **In vivo studies:** conducting tests in animal models to assess the safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetics of the compounds.
- **Toxicology studies:** evaluating potential toxicity and side effects through rigorous testing.
- **Formulation development:** developing the drug formulation for stability, delivery, and dosage.

## Clinical Development

An investigational new drug application submits data from preclinical studies to regulatory authorities (such as the FDA) to obtain permission to begin clinical trials. Clinical trials are composed of four phases:

- **Phase 1 trials:** testing the drug in a small group of healthy volunteers (20-100) to assess safety, dosage, and pharmacokinetics.
- **Phase 2 trials:** expanding the study to a larger group (100-500) of patients to evaluate efficacy and further assess safety.
- **Phase 3 trials:** conducting large-scale studies (1,000-3,000 participants) to confirm efficacy, monitor side effects, and compare with standard treatment.
- **Phase 4 trials:** post-marketing surveillance that monitors the drug's long-term effects after approval.

## Regulatory Review

- **New drug application:** submitting comprehensive data from all studies to regulatory authorities for approval to market the drug.
- **Review process:** regulatory agencies evaluate the data for safety, efficacy, and quality before granting approval.

## Post-Marketing Surveillance (Phase 4)

- **Monitoring:** after approval, the drug is continuously monitored for long-term effects, rare side effects, and overall effectiveness in the general population.
- **Additional studies:** conducting further studies to explore new uses, dosage adjustments, or combinations with other drugs.

Throughout this process, collaboration across disciplines such as biology, chemistry, pharmacology, and regulatory affairs is essential. Drug discovery and development is expensive and time-consuming, with a high rate of failure at each stage, but it ultimately aims to bring safe and effective medications to patients.

# 15.2 Antibiotics and Their Mode of Action

Antibiotics are medicines used to treat or prevent bacterial infections. They work by killing bacteria or stopping them from multiplying, for example by destroying crucial parts they need to survive, like their cell walls or DNA, or by preventing them from making certain proteins they need to multiply. Antibiotics do not work against viral infections such as the common cold or flu.

## Antibiotic Resistance

Antibiotics are powerful germ-fighting tools when used carefully and safely, but up to half of all antibiotic use is unnecessary. Overuse has led to antibiotic resistance: bacteria adapt over time and become "superbugs" that cannot be killed by standard antibiotics, having developed resistance through genetic mutation and natural selection that lets them survive and thrive despite the presence of drugs that would normally kill them.

## Classes of Antibiotics

There are hundreds of different antibiotics, but most fall into four groups:

- **Penicillins:** widely used to treat skin, chest, and urinary tract infections (examples: penicillin, amoxicillin, flucloxacillin). Mode of action: inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to penicillin-binding proteins, leading to cell lysis.
- **Tetracyclines:** used to treat a wide range of infections (examples: tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline). Mode of action: inhibit protein synthesis by binding to the 30S ribosomal subunit, blocking attachment of aminoacyl-tRNA to the ribosome.
- **Fluoroquinolones:** broad-spectrum antibiotics once used widely for respiratory and urinary tract infections, now used more cautiously because of the risk of serious side effects (examples: ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin). Mode of action: inhibit bacterial DNA replication by targeting enzymes like DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV.
- **Sulfonamides:** synthetic bacteriostatic antibiotics (examples: mafenide, sulfacetamide, sulfadiazine, sulfanilamide). Mode of action: competitively inhibit conversion of p-aminobenzoic acid to dihydropteroate, which bacteria need for folate synthesis and ultimately purine and DNA synthesis. Humans acquire folate from their diet rather than synthesizing it, so human DNA synthesis is largely unaffected.

# 15.3 Antivirals and Antiretrovirals

Antivirals and antiretrovirals are classes of medication used to treat viral and retroviral infections, caused by viruses such as HIV, herpes viruses, and hepatitis B and C.

## Antivirals

Antivirals are drugs used to treat viral infections. They work by inhibiting the ability of viruses to multiply and spread within the body, targeting a diverse group of viruses such as herpes, hepatitis, and influenza viruses.

## Antiretrovirals

Antiretrovirals are a subset of antivirals specifically used to fight retrovirus infections, mainly HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus). Retroviruses use RNA as their genetic material and reverse transcriptase to replicate. Different classes of antiretroviral drugs act on different stages of the HIV life cycle, inhibiting the replication of retroviruses to help control HIV infection and reduce the risk of progression to AIDS.

# 15.4 Advantages of Monoclonal Antibodies

Monoclonal antibodies (moAbs or mAbs) are laboratory-made proteins that act like the antibodies produced naturally by the immune system. Antibodies seek out antigens (foreign materials) and bind to them so they can be destroyed; laboratory-made monoclonal antibodies help stimulate the body's own immune system. "Monoclonal" refers to the fact that these antibodies are clones, exact copies of one original antibody. They are used for diagnosis, disease treatment, and research.

Monoclonal antibodies offer several advantages over traditional antibody therapies:

- **High specificity:** engineered to bind to a single, specific antigen or epitope, allowing precise targeting and reducing off-target effects.
- **Consistent quality:** can be produced in large quantities with consistent quality across batches, ensuring reproducibility and reliability.
- **Renewable production:** once a hybridoma or recombinant cell line is established, monoclonal antibodies can be produced indefinitely.
- **Versatile applications:** used in diagnostics (pregnancy tests, HIV testing), therapy (cancer treatment, autoimmune disease), and research (molecular biology, biochemistry).
- **Engineering possibilities:** can be engineered in the lab to enhance binding affinity or target specific molecules for tailored applications.
- **Humanization:** can be humanized to minimize the risk of immune reactions in patients.
- **Large-scale production:** recombinant DNA technology allows production in large quantities, making them accessible for various applications.

## Monoclonal Antibodies Compared to Other Drug Classes

- Monoclonal antibodies are clones of a single antibody and bind to one antigen only; polyclonal antibodies come from several different immune cells and bind to more than one antigen.
- Because monoclonal antibodies target specific cells, they allow smaller doses of chemotherapy drugs to be used alongside them, which can reduce side effects and risk to healthy cells while improving immune function.
- Compared with antibiotics, monoclonal antibodies offer high target-specificity, are less susceptible to resistance mechanisms, and have longer half-lives (around 21 days for IgG).
- Compared with small-molecule drugs, monoclonal antibodies have greater surface area for binding to an antigen, giving exquisite specificity and fewer off-target effects than most small-molecule drugs.
