# 7.1 Human Skeleton

The human skeletal system, comprised of *bones* and *cartilage*, serves as the body's primary framework. It provides support for soft tissues and enables movement through the interaction of muscles and joints. The study of bones and cartilage is known as ***osteology***.

## Structure of Bone

A long bone is a complex organ made of various tissues.

### Gross Anatomy

- ***Epiphysis:*** The broad, terminal ends of the bone. Contains most of the spongy bone.
- ***Diaphysis (Shaft):*** The long, middle section of the bone, containing a central cavity filled with *yellow bone marrow*.

### Coverings and Linings

- ***Periosteum:*** A fibrous connective tissue membrane covering the outer surface of the bone.
- ***Endosteum:*** A membrane lining the inner cavity of the bone.

### Types of Bone Tissue

- ***Compact Bone:*** The dense, hard outer layer of the bone, found peripherally under the endosteum.
- ***Spongy Bone:*** The inner, porous bone mass, whose spaces contain *red bone marrow*.

<CaptionedImage src="kg2avpg7sqvdmx3sefhqyswbkx8dghmq" alt="Structure of a long bone" caption="Figure 7.1: Structure of a long bone showing the epiphysis, diaphysis, and different types of bone tissue and marrow." />

## Cells Associated with Bone

Bone is a dynamic tissue with three main cell types derived from osteogenic cells.

- ***Osteoblasts:*** These are *bone-forming cells*. They synthesize and secrete the unmineralized ground substance of the bone matrix.
- ***Osteocytes:*** Mature bone cells that form when osteoblasts become trapped in the matrix they have secreted. They maintain healthy bone tissue, regulate mineral content, and control calcium release into the blood.
- ***Osteoclasts:*** These are *bone-destroying cells*. They perform *bone resorption*-the process of breaking down bone tissue to release calcium and phosphate into the blood. This is crucial for bone growth, repair, and remodeling.

<CaptionedImage src="kg25eqvs3vc82jgswdh7s1erm18dh3yg" alt="Main types of bone cells" caption="Figure 7.2: The main types of bone cells and their functions." />

## Structure of Cartilage

Cartilage is a flexible connective tissue that is not as strong as bone.

### Composition
The matrix is gel-like and contains numerous *collagenous* and *elastic fibres*, making it flexible.

### Key Structures

- ***Perichondrium:*** A dense layer of collagen fibres covering the cartilage.
- ***Lacunae:*** Small cavities within the matrix that contain cartilage cells.
- ***Chondrocytes:*** The living cells of cartilage, located within the lacunae.

### Vascularity
Cartilage is *avascular* (lacks blood vessels). Chondrocytes receive nutrients via diffusion, which results in very slow healing.

### Types of Cartilage

- ***Hyaline Cartilage:*** Found at the ends of long bones, in the nose, larynx, and trachea.
- ***Fibrocartilage:*** Contains thick rows of collagenous fibres. Found in intervertebral disks and the knee joint.
- ***Elastic Cartilage:*** Highly flexible. Found in the ear flaps and epiglottis.

<CaptionedImage src="kg204znxcszh2hcmxhqtxwfkbh8dg7b1" alt="Structure of cartilage" caption="Figure 7.3: Structure of cartilage, highlighting the chondrocytes within lacunae and the surrounding matrix." />

## Comparison between Bone and Cartilage

| Feature | Bone | Cartilage |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Collagen** | Densely packed | Loosely packed |
| **Cell Types** | Osteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts | Chondrocytes |
| **Blood Vessels** | Present (vascular) | Absent (avascular) |
| **Minerals** | Deposits minerals such as calcium, carbonates, phosphates, etc. | No deposition of minerals |
| **External Covering** | Covered by periosteum | Covered by perichondrium |

## Axial and Appendicular Skeleton

The adult human skeleton consists of 206 bones, divided into two main parts:

### Axial Skeleton
Consists of 80 bones including the skull (cranium and facial bones), vertebral column, ribs, and sternum.

### Appendicular Skeleton
Consists of 126 bones including the pectoral girdle (scapula and clavicle), pelvic girdle, and the bones of the upper and lower limbs.

# 7.2 Divisions of Human Skeleton

The human skeletal system is composed of **206 bones**, which are categorized into two primary divisions: the **Axial Skeleton** and the **Appendicular Skeleton**.

## Axial Skeleton

*The axial skeleton forms the central axis of the body.* It consists of the bones of the skull, vertebral column, and rib cage.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920045132.webp" alt="Human skeleton" caption="Figure 7.4: Human skeleton" />

### Head Bones (Skull)

The head contains a total of **29 bones**, organized into four groups.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920045158.webp" alt="Human skull (side view)" caption="Figure 7.5: Human skull (side view)" />

| **Skull Division** | **Number of Bones** | **Details** |
| :----------------- | :------------------ | :---------- |
| **Cranial Bones**  | 8                   | Form the cranium (brain box). Paired: Parietal, Temporal (2 pairs). Unpaired: Frontal, Occipital, Ethmoid, Sphenoid (4). |
| **Facial Bones**   | 14                  | Form the structure of the face. Paired: Lacrimal, Zygomatic, Nasal, Inferior Nasal Concha, Maxilla, Palatine (6 pairs). Unpaired: Mandible (jaw), Vomer (2). |
| **Ear Ossicles**   | 6 (3 pairs)         | Located in the middle ear: Malleus, Incus, Stapes. |
| **Hyoid Bone**     | 1                   | A single U-shaped bone at the base of the skull, below the tongue. *It is unique because it does not articulate with any other bone.* |

### Vertebral Column

The vertebral column consists of **33 vertebrae** organized into five regions. It protects the spinal cord and supports the head and body.

<InlineNoteTag label="Neuron" notePath="biology-12/5.3-neuron" />

| **Region**            | **Number of Vertebrae** | **Description** |
| :-------------------- | :---------------------- | :-------------- |
| **Cervical**          | 7                       | Neck vertebrae. The first is the atlas (C1) and the second is the axis (C2). |
| **Thoracic**          | 12                      | Chest region vertebrae that articulate with the ribs. |
| **Lumbar**            | 5                       | Abdominal region vertebrae. |
| **Sacral (Sacrum)**   | 5 (fused)               | Fused vertebrae that articulate with the hip bones to form the back of the pelvis. |
| **Coccygeal (Coccyx)**| 4 (fused)               | Fused vertebrae forming the tailbone. |

*Note: The sacral and coccygeal vertebrae are sometimes referred to as the pelvic vertebrae.*

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920045230.webp" alt="Human vertebral column" caption="Figure 7.6: Human vertebral column" />

### Rib Cage

The rib cage consists of **12 pairs of ribs** that articulate posteriorly with the thoracic vertebrae and the sternum (breastbone) anteriorly. It protects the heart and lungs.

<InlineNoteTag label="Mechanism of Ventilation" notePath="biology-12/3.3-mechanism-of-ventilation--breathing-" />

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920045256.webp" alt="Human rib cage" caption="Figure 7.7: Human rib cage" />

| **Type of Ribs** | **Pairs**   | **Description** |
| :--------------- | :---------- | :-------------- |
| **True Ribs**    | 1-7         | Attach directly to the sternum via their own costal cartilage. |
| **False Ribs**   | 8-10        | Attach to the sternum indirectly via the costal cartilage of the 7th rib. |
| **Floating Ribs**| 11-12       | Do not attach to the sternum at all. |

## Appendicular Skeleton

*The appendicular skeleton includes the bones of the limbs (appendages) and the girdles that attach them to the axial skeleton.* It consists of **126 bones**.

### Pectoral Girdle and Upper Limb

The **pectoral girdle** connects the upper limb to the axial skeleton and is made up of the **clavicle** (collar bone), which articulates with the sternum and scapula, and the **scapula** (shoulder blade), a flat, triangular bone.

The **upper limb (forelimb)** continues from there: the **humerus** is the long bone of the upper arm, and the **radius and ulna** are the bones of the forearm, with the radius on the thumb side. The hand is made up of the **carpals**, 8 short bones forming the wrist, the **metacarpals**, 5 bones forming the palm, and the **phalanges**, 14 bones of the fingers (3 in each finger, 2 in the thumb).

### Pelvic Girdle and Lower Limb

The **pelvic girdle** connects the lower limb to the axial skeleton. It is composed of two hip bones (coxa), each formed by the fusion of three bones, the **ileum, ischium, and pubis**; the two halves join at the **pubic symphysis**, and the **acetabulum** is the socket that articulates with the head of the femur.

The **lower limb (hind limb)** consists of the **femur**, the thigh bone and the longest and strongest bone in the body, the **patella** (kneecap), and the **tibia and fibula**, the bones of the lower leg, of which the tibia (shin bone) is the larger. The foot is made up of the **tarsals**, 7 bones forming the ankle, the **metatarsals**, 5 bones forming the sole of the foot, and the **phalanges**, 14 bones of the toes (3 in each toe, 2 in the big toe).

## Possible Questions and Answers

**Q:** What are the two main divisions of the human skeleton?
**A:** The axial skeleton (central axis: skull, vertebral column, rib cage) and the appendicular skeleton (limbs and girdles).

**Q:** Which bone is unique because it does not articulate with any other bone?
**A:** The hyoid bone, located at the base of the skull below the tongue.

**Q:** What is the difference between true, false, and floating ribs?
**A:** True ribs (1-7) attach directly to the sternum. False ribs (8-10) attach indirectly to the sternum via shared cartilage. Floating ribs (11-12) do not attach to the sternum at all.

**Q:** What three bones fuse to form the coxa (hip bone) of the pelvic girdle?
**A:** The ileum, ischium, and pubis.

## Summary

The human skeleton consists of 206 bones, divided into the **axial** and **appendicular** skeletons. The **axial skeleton** forms the body's central axis and includes the skull, vertebral column, and rib cage. The **appendicular skeleton** consists of the pectoral and pelvic girdles, and the bones of the upper and lower limbs, facilitating movement.

| Skeleton Division       | Major Components                                            | Primary Function                      |
| :---------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------ |
| **Axial Skeleton**      | Skull (29), Vertebral Column (33), Rib Cage (12 pairs), Sternum | Protection of vital organs, support   |
| **Appendicular Skeleton** | Pectoral Girdle, Upper Limbs, Pelvic Girdle, Lower Limbs  | Movement, manipulation of objects     |

**Biological Significance:** The skeletal system provides a rigid framework for support, protects internal organs (such as the brain, heart, and lungs), allows for movement through articulation with muscles, stores minerals, and is the site of blood cell formation (hematopoiesis).

# 7.3 Joints

A joint (articulation) is a location where two bones, or a bone and cartilage, connect. The scientific study of the structure and function of joints is called **Arthrology**.

Joints are classified based on their structure and the degree of movement they permit.

| Joint Classification | Connecting Material | Movement | Presence of Joint Cavity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Fibrous Joint** | Fibrous connective tissue (collagen) | Immoveable | No |
| **Cartilaginous Joint** | Hyaline or fibrocartilage | Slightly moveable | No |
| **Synovial Joint** | Joint capsule with synovial fluid | Freely moveable | Yes |

## 7.3.1 Fibrous Joints

Fibrous joints are connections where adjacent bones are directly linked by dense, collagen-rich fibrous connective tissue. They lack a joint cavity and permit no movement.

- **Examples:**
  - **Sutures:** Found between most bones of the skull.
  - Joints between the shaft regions of long bones (e.g., in the forearm and leg).
  - The connection between the root of a tooth and its socket in the mandible or maxilla.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920045337.webp" alt="Fibrous joints" caption="Figure 7.8: Examples of fibrous joints." />

## 7.3.2 Cartilaginous Joints

In cartilaginous joints, adjacent bones are united by either hyaline cartilage or fibrocartilage. These joints also lack a cavity and allow for only a small amount of movement.

- **Examples:**
  - **Costal cartilages:** Attach the ribs to the sternum.
  - **Pubic symphysis:** Connects the two pubic bones of the pelvis.
  - **Intervertebral discs:** Found between the vertebrae of the spine.

For more on the vertebral column, see <InlineNoteTag label="7.1 Human Skeleton" notePath="biology-12/7-1-human-skeleton" />.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920045359.webp" alt="Cartilaginous joint" caption="Figure 7.9: Cartilaginous joint of pubic symphysis." />

## 7.3.3 Synovial Joints

Synovial joints are the most common type of joint and are characterized by their free mobility. The ends of the bones are covered by **articular cartilage**, a layer of smooth hyaline cartilage, and the whole joint is enclosed by a **joint capsule**, a tube-like structure of dense fibrous tissue. Its outer layer is composed of ligaments for stability, while its inner layer, the **synovial membrane**, secretes **synovial fluid**, a viscous fluid that lubricates the joint, reduces friction, and nourishes the articular cartilage. This fluid fills the **joint cavity**, the space between the bones.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920045424.webp" alt="Synovial joint" caption="Figure 7.10: Synovial joint." />

Synovial joints come in several types. **Hinge joints** allow movement in one plane, as in the elbow, knee, and finger joints. **Ball-and-socket joints** allow for a wide range of motion, as in the shoulder and hip joints. **Pivot joints** allow for rotational movement, as between the first two vertebrae in the neck. **Gliding joints** allow for sliding or gliding movements, as in the carpal joints of the wrist and tarsal joints of the ankle.

# 7.4 Disorders of Skeleton

This section outlines common disorders affecting the skeletal system, categorizing them based on their causes: hereditary, hormonal, or nutritional. The disorders detailed include their specific pathologies and clinical manifestations.

## Slipped Disc (Herniated Disc)

A slipped disc, also known as a herniated disc, is a condition affecting the intervertebral discs of the spine.

### Structure of Intervertebral Discs

Intervertebral discs are cushion-like pads located between adjacent vertebrae. Each disc consists of two main components:

- **Nucleus Pulposus:** The inner, semifluid core that provides elasticity and compressibility. It functions like a rubber ball, allowing the spine to absorb shocks and undergo movement.
- **Annulus Fibrosus:** A strong outer ring of fibrocartilage that holds successive vertebrae together and contains the nucleus pulposus.

The primary function of intervertebral discs is to act as shock absorbers for the spine.

### Herniation Process

1. Severe or sudden trauma causes the annulus fibrosus to rupture.
2. The inner nucleus pulposus protrudes (herniates) outwards through the tear.
3. This protrusion can press on the spinal cord or spinal nerves, causing pain and potential neurological damage.

The term "slipped disc" is misleading, as the entire disc does not slide out of position. Instead, the inner material bulges through the damaged outer ring.

### Symptoms

Severe pain and potential damage to nervous structures result from nerve compression.

## Spondylosis

Spondylosis is a degenerative condition characterized by the immobility and fusion of vertebral joints.

- **Cervical Spondylosis:** A common form resulting from chronic degeneration of the cervical (neck) vertebrae, often linked to aging and disc herniation.

---

## Sciatica

Sciatica refers to nerve pain originating from the sciatic nerve.

- **Symptoms:** Pain, weakness, numbness, or tingling in the leg.
- **Cause:** Injury to or pressure on the sciatic nerve.
- **Common Triggers:**
  - Slipped disc
  - Pelvic injury or fracture
  - Tumors

---

## Arthritis

Arthritis is a general term for the inflammation of one or more joints.

### General Symptoms

- Pain, especially after walking, which can later occur at rest
- Creaking sounds in the joint
- Difficulty getting up from a chair
- Pain on walking up and down stairs

### Types of Arthritis

| Type | Cause | Description | Common Joints Affected |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Osteoarthritis** | Degenerative ("wear and tear") | Progressive disease where the articular cartilages gradually soften and disintegrate. | Knee, hip, intervertebral joints |
| **Rheumatoid Arthritis** | Autoimmune Disorder | The body's immune system mistakenly attacks the synovial membrane, causing inflammation. | Hands, feet |
| **Gouty Arthritis (Gout)** | Metabolic Disorder | High levels of uric acid in the blood lead to the deposition of sodium urate crystals in the joints. | Joint of the big toe is most common. |

---

## Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is a disease characterized by reduced bone mass (density), leading to an increased risk of fractures.

- **Underlying Mechanism:** The rate of bone resorption (breakdown) exceeds the rate of bone deposition (formation).
- **Bone Mass Lifecycle:**
  - Bone mass increases during growth, reaching a peak between ages 20 and 25.
  - After this peak, bone mass gradually falls in both sexes.
- **Prevention:** Ingesting adequate amounts of calcium, especially for women in their 20s and 30s, helps build a higher peak bone mass. A higher peak mass provides a greater tolerance for bone loss later in life.

## Osteomalacia and Rickets

While not detailed in the original summary table, these nutritional disorders are critical skeletal conditions.

## Possible Questions and Answers

**Q:** What happens during a "slipped disc"?
**A:** The outer ring of the intervertebral disc (annulus fibrosus) ruptures, allowing the soft inner core (nucleus pulposus) to protrude and press on nearby nerves or the spinal cord.

**Q:** What is the fundamental difference between Osteoarthritis and Rheumatoid Arthritis?
**A:** Osteoarthritis is a degenerative "wear-and-tear" disease where cartilage breaks down. Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disorder where the body's immune system attacks the joint's synovial membrane.

**Q:** Why is osteoporosis more than just "weak bones"?
**A:** It is a systemic disease where the balance between bone formation and bone breakdown is disrupted. Specifically, bone resorption (breakdown) outpaces bone deposition (formation), leading to a progressive loss of bone density and structural integrity.

# 7.5 Bone Fractures

A **fracture** is a medical term for a broken bone, occurring when the physical force exerted on the bone is stronger than the bone's structural integrity.

## Common Types of Fractures

- **Simple (Closed) Fracture:** The bone is broken, but the overlying skin remains intact.
- **Compound (Open) Fracture:** The broken bone ends penetrate the skin, creating an open wound. This carries a higher risk of infection.
- **Complicated Fracture:** A fracture that causes damage to adjacent organs or tissues.

<CaptionedImage src="kg2ebk2sf8xde7nc5s427hc65s8dgefa" alt="Bone fractures" caption="Figure 7.12: Bone fractures" />

## Bone Repair

Bone is a living tissue capable of self-repair. The healing of a simple fracture occurs in four main stages:

### 1. Haematoma (Clot) Formation

- Immediately after the fracture, blood vessels within the bone and surrounding tissues are torn, leading to haemorrhage (bleeding).
- A **haematoma** (a mass of clotted blood) forms at the fracture site.
- Bone cells deprived of their nutrient supply die.
- The area becomes swollen, painful, and inflamed.

### 2. Fibrocartilaginous (Soft) Callus Formation

- Occurs within a few days.
- New capillaries grow into the haematoma.
- Phagocytic cells (like macrophages) invade the area to clean up cellular debris.
- The ruptured periosteum stimulates **osteoblasts** (bone-building cells) and cartilage-forming cells.
- These cells secrete a porous mass of bone and cartilage called a **callus** (or soft callus), which replaces the blood clot and splints the broken ends together.
- This stage takes approximately three to four weeks.

### 3. Bony Callus Formation (Callus Ossification)

- Beginning about a week after soft callus formation, it is gradually converted into a **bony callus** of spongy bone.
- **Osteoclasts** (bone-resorbing cells) break down the cartilage.
- **Osteoblasts** replace the cartilage with bone.
- A firm union of the bone ends is typically formed after about two months.

### 4. Bone Remodelling

- This is the final phase, where the bony callus is remodelled.
- Compact bone is formed across the fracture line to connect both sides.
- **Osteoclasts** remove the excess bone material produced during healing.
- The final structure of the repaired area resembles the original unbroken bone, as it adapts and responds to mechanical stressors.

<CaptionedImage src="kg2egbzhnprj8k152shkn52fyh8dgke6" alt="The stages of bone repair" caption="Figure 7.13: The stages of bone repair" />

## Science, Technology and Society Connections

- **Improper Posture and Bone/Joint Health:** Maintaining improper posture increases stress on joints and their supporting structures. This can lead to injury, chronic pain, and premature degeneration of bones and joints.

# 7.6 Injuries to Joints

Joint injuries occur due to forces like torsion (twisting) or sudden impact, damaging the structures that make up and support a joint.

## Dislocation of Joints

**Definition**: A dislocation occurs when the ends of bones in a joint are forced from their normal anatomical positions, causing the joint to slip out of place.

**Causes**:
*   **Trauma**: Most common cause, resulting from a blow, fall, or other impact.
*   **Disease**: Conditions like Rheumatoid arthritis can weaken joint structures, leading to dislocation.
*   **Defective Ligaments**: Inherently weak or damaged ligaments may not be able to hold the joint in place.

**Symptoms**:
*   Intense pain
*   Swelling
*   Immobility of the affected joint

**Consequences**: Severe dislocations can tear surrounding muscles, ligaments, and tendons.

**Treatment**:
*   Must be "reduced" (put back into place) by a trained medical professional.
*   Surgery may be required to repair or tighten stretched ligaments.

## Sprain

**Definition**: A sprain is an injury to a ligament, which occurs when it is stretched beyond its normal range of motion.

**Common Locations**: Ankle, knee, and wrist.

**Treatment**:
*   **Rest**: The injured area should be rested to allow for healing.
*   **Conservative Treatment**: Often includes icing to reduce swelling and physical therapy to restore function.
*   **Immobilization**: Using dressings, bandages, or wraps (e.g., ace-wraps) to provide support and limit movement.

## Comparison of Dislocation and Sprain

| Feature | **Dislocation** | **Sprain** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Affected Structure** | Bones are forced out of their normal position in a joint. | Ligament is stretched or torn. |
| **Primary Cause** | Trauma (blow, fall), disease (e.g., arthritis). | Stretching a ligament too far. |
| **Key Symptoms** | Intense pain, swelling, immobility of the joint. | Pain, swelling, bruising. |
| **Treatment** | Medical professional must "reduce" the joint; surgery may be needed. | Rest, icing, immobilization, physical therapy. |

## Other Related Injuries

While dislocations and sprains are primary joint injuries, inflammation of associated structures is also common:

*   **Bursitis**: Inflammation of the bursa (fluid-filled sac) usually caused by a blow or friction.
*   **Tendonitis**: Inflammation of tendon sheaths, typically caused by overuse.

## First Aid for Skeletal Disorders (Fractures & Dislocations)

**Goal**: To prevent further injury and manage symptoms until professional medical help arrives.

1.  **Immobilize**: Do not attempt to move, pull, or realign the injured joint or bone. Use a sling or splint to support the affected area.
2.  **Apply Ice**: If possible, apply a cold pack to the injured area to reduce swelling and pain.
3.  **Position for Comfort**: Assist the victim into a comfortable position without moving the injured part.
4.  **Seek Professional Help**: For severe injuries like hip/leg dislocations or compound fractures, an ambulance is required.

# 7.7 Muscles

This section outlines the structure, function, and types of muscle tissue in the body. Muscles are specialized tissues that contract and relax to produce movement, maintain posture, move body fluids, and generate heat. The study of muscles is known as *myology*.

## General Characteristics of Muscle Tissue

- **Contractility:** The ability to shorten forcefully.
- **Relaxation:** The ability to return to a resting state.
- **Functions:**
  - Movement of body parts and locomotion (whole-body movement).
  - Maintaining posture and holding body parts in position.
  - Movement of body fluids (e.g., blood, contents of the digestive tract).
  - Heat production.

## Types of Muscle Tissues

There are three distinct types of muscle tissue: *smooth*, *cardiac*, and *skeletal*.

### Smooth Muscle

- **Appearance:** Non-striated (lacks a striped pattern) because its myofilaments are not organized into sarcomeres.
- **Cell Structure:** Spindle-shaped cells, each with a single, centrally located nucleus. Contains non-contractile intermediate filaments.
- **Control:** *Involuntary* (not under conscious control).
- **Location:** Found in the walls of hollow organs such as the digestive tract, reproductive and urinary tracts, and blood vessels.
- **Function:** Controls the movement of substances through these hollow organs.

<CaptionedImage src="kg28pvz4ymqk4yjjvckv7s9d418dhbvp" alt="Smooth muscle cells" caption="Figure 7.7a: Smooth muscle cells (non-striated)" />

### Cardiac Muscle

- **Appearance:** Striated (striped), similar to skeletal muscle.
- **Cell Structure:** Branched cells that join together. Each cell typically contains one centrally located nucleus.
- **Control:** *Involuntary*.
- **Location:** Found exclusively in the walls of the heart.
- **Function:** To pump blood throughout the body.
- **Special Features:** Adjacent cells are connected by ***intercalated discs***, which are specialized junctions containing gap junctions. These allow action potentials (electrical signals) to pass rapidly from cell to cell, enabling the heart to contract as a coordinated unit.

<CaptionedImage src="kg20ndepmhbr439bq28nk36h7d8dgk14" alt="Cardiac muscle cells" caption="Figure 7.7b: Cardiac muscle cells (striated)" />

### Skeletal Muscle

- **Appearance:** Striated with regular, parallel stripes.
- **Cell Structure:** Long, cylindrical cells (fibers) that are *multinucleated* (contain many nuclei per cell).
- **Control:** *Voluntary* (under conscious control).
- **Location:** Attached to bones of the skeleton.
- **Function:** Responsible for the movement of body parts and locomotion.

For more details on how skeletal muscles work together, refer to <InlineNoteTag label="Antagonistic Arrangement of Skeletal Muscles" notePath="biology-12/710-antagonistic-arrangement-of-skeletal-muscles" />. For information on common skeletal muscle disorders, see <InlineNoteTag label="Muscle Disorders" notePath="biology-12/711-muscle-disorders" />.

## Possible Questions/Answers

- **Q:** What are intercalated discs and why are they important?
  **A:** Intercalated discs are specialized junctions that connect adjacent cardiac muscle cells. They contain gap junctions which allow electrical signals to pass quickly between cells, ensuring the heart muscle contracts in a coordinated, wave-like manner to pump blood effectively.

- **Q:** Why is smooth muscle not striated?
  **A:** Smooth muscle lacks striations because its contractile filaments (myofilaments) are not arranged in the highly organized, repeating units called sarcomeres that are found in skeletal and cardiac muscle.

- **Q:** What is the key difference between voluntary and involuntary muscle control?
  **A:** *Voluntary* control means the muscle can be consciously controlled (e.g., deciding to lift your arm using skeletal muscles). *Involuntary* control means the muscle functions automatically without conscious thought (e.g., the beating of the heart or the movement of food through the intestines).

# 7.8 Structure of Skeletal Muscles

The main biological concepts cover the hierarchical organization of skeletal muscle, from the whole muscle down to the molecular proteins responsible for its function. This includes the macroscopic and microscopic anatomy, with a focus on the sarcomere as the fundamental contractile unit.

## Macroscopic and Microscopic Structure of Skeletal Muscle

Skeletal muscles, also known as *striated muscles*, have a complex, bundled structure. Three layers of connective tissue cover the muscle at different scales: the ***epimysium*** is a dense connective tissue layer that wraps the entire muscle, the ***perimysium*** surrounds bundles of muscle cells called *fascicles*, and the ***endomysium*** is a delicate connective tissue layer that covers each individual *muscle fibre* (muscle cell). At the ends of the muscle, these connective tissue layers merge to form a strong tendon, which attaches the muscle to bone.

Each muscle fibre is a single, cylindrical, multinucleated cell with several specialized components. The ***sarcolemma*** is the plasma membrane of the muscle fibre. ***Transverse tubules (T-tubules)*** are deep invaginations of the sarcolemma that penetrate into the cell, allowing nerve impulses to travel deep into the muscle fibre. The ***sarcoplasm*** is the cytoplasm of the muscle fibre, and the ***sarcoplasmic reticulum*** is the specialized endoplasmic reticulum of a muscle cell, which stores and releases calcium ions. ***Myofibrils*** are numerous thin, rod-like structures that run parallel to the length of the muscle fibre and contain the contractile elements.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920050000.webp" alt="Hierarchical structure of skeletal muscle" caption="Fig. 7.15: Hierarchical structure of skeletal muscle, from the whole muscle down to the myofibril." />

## The Sarcomere: The Contractile Unit

Myofibrils exhibit a repeating pattern of light and dark bands, giving skeletal muscle its striated appearance. This pattern is created by the arrangement of *myofilaments* within a unit called the **sarcomere**.

- **Sarcomere:** The fundamental contractile unit of a myofibril, defined as the region between two successive **Z-lines**.
- The arrangement of thick and thin myofilaments creates distinct bands and zones within the sarcomere.

| Band / Line | Description                                                                 | Composition                                         |
| :---------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------- |
| **A-Band**  | The dark band (*anisotropic*). Represents the full length of the thick filaments. | Thick filaments and overlapping thin filaments      |
| **I-Band**  | The light band (*isotropic*).                                               | Only thin filaments                                 |
| **Z-Line**  | A dark line that bisects the I-band.                                        | Serves as the boundary of the sarcomere.            |
| **H-Zone**  | A lighter region in the middle of the A-band.                               | Only thick filaments (in a relaxed muscle).         |
| **M-Line**  | A dark line in the center of the H-zone.                                    | Proteins that hold adjacent thick filaments together. |

## Myofilaments: The Contractile Proteins

Myofibrils are composed of two types of protein filaments: thick and thin myofilaments. Thick myofilaments are primarily composed of the protein **myosin**. Each myosin molecule has a long tail and two globular heads, and the heads contain binding sites for actin and ATP. About 300 myosin molecules are bundled together, with their tails forming the core and their heads pointing outwards.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920050159.webp" alt="Myosin molecule structure" caption="Structure of a myosin molecule and its arrangement in a thick filament." />

Thin myofilaments are composed of three proteins: **actin**, **tropomyosin**, and **troponin**. ***Actin*** is the primary structural protein: globular actin (G-actin) subunits polymerize to form two intertwined strands of fibrous actin (F-actin), and each G-actin has a myosin-binding site. ***Tropomyosin*** is a rod-shaped protein that spirals around the actin core; in a relaxed muscle, it blocks the myosin-binding sites on actin. ***Troponin*** is a complex of three regulatory proteins bound to tropomyosin: **TnI (inhibitory)** binds to actin, **TnT (tropomyosin-binding)** binds to tropomyosin and helps position it on actin, and **TnC (calcium-binding)** binds to calcium ions (Ca²⁺), which triggers the contraction process.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920050216.webp" alt="Troponin complex" caption="Fig. 7.16: The troponin complex on the thin filament." />

## Possible Questions/Answers

- **Q:** What gives skeletal muscle its striated (striped) appearance?
  **A:** The regular, repeating arrangement of dark A-bands and light I-bands within the myofibrils.
- **Q:** What is a sarcomere?
  **A:** It is the basic contractile unit of a muscle fibre, extending from one Z-line to the next.
- **Q:** What is the role of tropomyosin in a relaxed muscle?
  **A:** Tropomyosin physically blocks the myosin-binding sites on the actin filaments, preventing the myosin heads from attaching and thus preventing contraction.
- **Q:** How are thick and thin filaments arranged relative to each other?
  **A:** In the regions of overlap, each thick filament is surrounded by six thin filaments in a hexagonal pattern.

## Summary

- Skeletal muscle is organized hierarchically: **Muscle → Fascicles → Muscle Fibres → Myofibrils → Myofilaments**.
- Connective tissue layers (*epimysium, perimysium, endomysium*) provide structure and support.
- The **sarcomere** is the functional unit of contraction, located between two Z-lines. Its structure includes A-bands, I-bands, H-zones, and an M-line.
- Myofilaments are the proteins responsible for contraction.

| Filament Type | Primary Protein(s)                      | Function                                                                 |
| :------------ | :-------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Thick**     | Myosin                                  | Forms cross-bridges with actin to generate force.                        |
| **Thin**      | Actin, Tropomyosin, Troponin            | Actin provides binding sites; Tropomyosin and Troponin regulate contraction. |

- **Biological Significance:** This highly organized, intricate structure of proteins and membranes allows for the precise control and powerful force generation required for voluntary movement, powered by the sliding of myofilaments past one another.

# 7.9 Muscle Contraction: Sliding Filament Model

The following notes detail the molecular mechanism of muscle contraction, known as the sliding filament model, which explains how muscle fibers shorten to generate force.

## The Sliding Filament Theory

The fundamental principle of muscle contraction is the *sliding filament theory*. This theory states that muscle contraction occurs not because the myofilaments themselves shorten, but because the *thin (actin) myofilaments slide past the thick (myosin) myofilaments*. This sliding action increases the degree of overlap between them, shortening the entire muscle cell.

- **Relaxed State:** In a relaxed muscle, the thin and thick filaments overlap only partially, at the ends of the A band.
- **Contracted State:** Upon stimulation, myosin heads on the thick filaments attach to binding sites on the actin of the thin filaments, forming **cross-bridges**. These cross-bridges repeatedly form and break, acting like ratchets to pull the thin filaments toward the center of the sarcomere.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920050255.webp" alt="Sliding filament model" caption="Figure 7.17: Sliding of thin (actin) filaments over thick (myosin) filaments resulting in sarcomere shortening." />

## Changes in the Sarcomere During Contraction

As the thin filaments slide, the structure of the sarcomere changes measurably:

| Sarcomere Zone/Band | Change During Contraction | Reason |
|---------------------|---------------------------|--------|
| **I Band** | Shortens | Contains only thin filaments, which are pulled towards the M-line. |
| **H Zone** | Disappears | The central region of the A band where thin filaments do not overlap at rest vanishes as thin filaments slide inward and overlap. |
| **Z Discs** | Move closer together | The boundaries of the sarcomere are pulled inward as the unit shortens. |
| **A Band** | Remains the same length | Represents the full length of the thick (myosin) filaments, which do not change their length. |

## Control of Cross-Bridge Formation

The formation of cross-bridges is a tightly regulated process initiated by the nervous system. First, a nerve impulse arrives at the **neuromuscular junction**. <InlineNoteTag label="Neuron" notePath="biology-12/5.3-neuron" /> The electrical signal (action potential) then travels along the muscle cell membrane (*sarcolemma*), down the **T-tubules**, until it reaches the **sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)**, triggering the opening of its calcium gates; **calcium ions ($Ca^{2+}$)** are released from the SR into the cytosol surrounding the myofilaments.

Regulatory proteins then determine whether contraction can proceed: at rest, the protein **tropomyosin** is positioned over the myosin-binding sites on the actin filaments, blocking them, but upon $Ca^{2+}$ release, the released ions bind to another protein, **troponin**, causing it to change shape. This shape change in troponin pulls the attached tropomyosin strand away, exposing the myosin-binding sites on the actin filament. Myosin heads can then attach to the exposed binding sites on actin, forming a cross-bridge; **ATP is hydrolyzed** (broken down to ADP and Pi), providing the energy for the myosin head to pivot (the "power stroke"), pulling the actin filament. The cross-bridge then breaks, the myosin head re-energizes, and the cycle of attachment, pivoting, and detachment repeats as long as $Ca^{2+}$ and ATP are present.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/biology/Pasted image 20250920050344.webp" alt="Molecular interactions" caption="Figure 7.18: Molecular interactions during the sliding filament process." />

## Possible Questions/Answers

**Q:** What is the sliding filament theory?
**A:** It is the theory that muscle contraction results from the sliding of thin (actin) filaments past thick (myosin) filaments, increasing their overlap and shortening the sarcomere. The filaments themselves do not change length.

**Q:** What is the role of calcium ions ($Ca^{2+}$) in muscle contraction?
**A:** Calcium ions are the trigger for contraction. They bind to troponin, which causes tropomyosin to move away from the myosin-binding sites on actin, allowing cross-bridges to form.

**Q:** Which band of the sarcomere does not change length during contraction?
**A:** The **A band** does not change length because it corresponds to the length of the thick (myosin) filaments, which do not shorten.

**Q:** What is a cross-bridge?
**A:** A cross-bridge is the physical link formed when a myosin head binds to an active site on an actin filament. The repeated formation and breaking of these bridges generates the force for muscle contraction.

## Biological Significance

The sliding filament model is the universal mechanism for muscle contraction in animals, converting chemical energy (ATP) into mechanical force. This process is essential for all forms of movement, from locomotion to the beating of the heart. <InlineNoteTag label="Phases of Heartbeat" notePath="biology-12/2.4-phases-of-heartbeat" />

# 7.10 Antagonistic Arrangement of Skeletal Muscles

Skeletal muscles work in opposing pairs to produce controlled movement at joints, using the knee joint as a specific example.

## Muscle Attachment to Bone

*   **Origin:** The end of a muscle attached to a bone that remains stationary during contraction.
*   **Insertion:** The end of a muscle attached to a bone that moves when the muscle contracts. The insertion is typically pulled toward the origin.
*   **Belly:** The thick, fleshy, central part of a muscle between the origin and insertion that performs the contraction.

## Antagonistic Muscle Arrangement

*   Muscles can only pull (contract); they cannot push (extend).
*   To allow for movement in two opposite directions (e.g., bending and straightening a limb), muscles are arranged in opposing pairs.
*   When one muscle in the pair contracts (the agonist), the other muscle relaxes (the antagonist). This coordinated action allows for smooth and controlled movement.

### Types of Muscles in an Antagonistic Pair

*   **Flexor muscle:** A muscle that, upon contraction, bends a joint, decreasing the angle between the bones.
*   **Extensor muscle:** A muscle that, upon contraction, straightens a joint, increasing the angle between the bones.

## Movement in the Knee Joint

The knee joint (tibio-femoral joint) is a complex hinge joint between the femur (thigh bone) and the tibia (shin bone). Its primary movements are flexion and extension.

<CaptionedImage src="kg255bn172khcne51x5kpm3d498dhv22" alt="Movement at knee joint" caption="Figure 7.19: Movement at knee joint" />

<CaptionedImage src="kg2d5ccy0qnkphafac7kwdj4p98dgx67" alt="Biceps femoris" caption="Figure 7.20: Biceps femoris" />

| Action | Muscle Group | Primary Muscle | Location | Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Flexion** (Bending the knee) | Hamstrings (Flexors) | **Biceps femoris**, Semitendinosus, Semimembranosus | Posterior (back) of the thigh | Contracts to pull the lower leg backward, bending the knee. |
| **Extension** (Straightening the knee) | Quadriceps (Extensors) | **Quadriceps femoris** | Anterior (front) of the thigh | Contracts to pull the lower leg forward, straightening the knee. Essential for standing, walking, and running. |

### Details of Knee Muscles

*   **Biceps Femoris (Hamstring):**
    *   *Origin:* Pelvic girdle and the top of the femur.
    *   *Insertion:* Upper parts of the tibia and fibula.
*   **Quadriceps Femoris (Extensor):**
    *   *Origin:* Ilium (part of the pelvis) and femur.
    *   *Insertion:* Tibia, via a common tendon that surrounds the patella (kneecap).

# 7.11 Muscle Disorders

This section outlines common physiological issues affecting muscle function, including fatigue, cramps, and tetany, and explores related physiological concepts like rigor mortis.

## Muscle Fatigue

**Definition**: A physiological state where muscles lose their ability to contract.

**Causes**:
*   Accumulation of lactic acid, which lowers the muscle pH and causes an aching sensation.
*   Ionic imbalance within the muscle cells.

---

## Cramp

**Definition**: A sustained, involuntary, and painful tetanic contraction of an entire muscle.

**Characteristics**:
*   Lasts from a few seconds to several hours.
*   Causes the muscle to become taut (tightly drawn).

**Common Locations**: Thigh and hip muscles.

**Triggers**:
*   Often occurs at night or after strenuous exercise.
*   Can be a sign of low blood sugar, electrolyte depletion, dehydration, or irritability of spinal cord neurons.

<InlineNoteTag label="Neuron" notePath="biology-12/5.3-neuron" />

---

## Tetany

**Definition**: A condition characterized by continuous muscle contractions and convulsions due to electrolyte imbalance, specifically low calcium levels.

**Mechanism**: Low calcium increases the excitability of neurons, leading to spontaneous muscle stimulation.

**Symptoms**:
*   Body shakes from continuous muscle contractions.
*   Loss of sensation.
*   In severe, untreated cases: spasm of the larynx, paralysis, and death.

---

### Comparison: Tetany vs. Tetanus

| Feature | Tetany | Tetanus |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cause** | Electrolyte imbalance (e.g., low calcium) | Bacterial infection (*Clostridium tetani*) |
| **Mechanism** | Overly stimulated peripheral nerves and muscles | Toxin from the bacteria blocks inhibitory nerve signals |
| **Symptoms** | Muscle spasms, cramps, potential larynx spasms | Muscle stiffness and spasms, especially in the jaw ("lockjaw") |
| **Treatment** | Address the underlying electrolyte imbalance | Tetanus immune globulin, antibiotics, and supportive care |

---

## Related Physiological Concepts

### Muscle Action: Pull vs. Push

Muscles work by contracting, which causes them to shorten. This shortening action pulls on bones, which act as levers. Muscles cannot actively lengthen or push; they can only pull and then relax. Antagonistic muscle pairs are required to produce movement in opposite directions. For example, biceps pulls to bend the arm, triceps pulls to straighten it.

<InlineNoteTag label="Antagonistic Arrangement" notePath="biology-12/710-antagonistic-arrangement-of-skeletal-muscles" />

---

### Rigor Mortis

**Definition**: The stiffening of joints and muscles of a body after death (literally "rigidity of death").

**Cause**: After death, ATP production ceases. ATP is required to detach the myosin heads from the actin filaments (break the cross-bridges) during muscle relaxation.

**Mechanism**: Without ATP, the cross-bridges that were formed just before death remain locked, causing the muscles to become fixed in a state of partial contraction.

**Timeline**:
*   Appears about 4 hours after death.
*   Lasts for approximately 34 hours.
*   Disappears as muscle proteins are broken down by cellular enzymes.
