# 1.1 Cells: The Basic Unit of Life

A cell is defined as the *basic structural and functional unit* of all living organisms. It is the smallest unit and fundamental building block of life. All organisms are composed of one or more cells. The shape and size of cells are often related to the specific function they perform.

---

## Classification by Cell Number

Organisms can be classified based on the number of cells they contain.

| **Type** | **Definition** | **Examples** |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |
| **Unicellular** | Organisms made of a single cell. | *Amoeba*, *Paramecium*, *Euglena* |
| **Multicellular** | Organisms made of many cells. | Plants, Animals |

<InlineNoteTag label="Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells" notePath="biology-11/9-prokaryotic-and-eukaryotic-cells" />

---

## Properties of Life

A single cell exhibits all the basic properties necessary for life. These are often remembered by the acronym **MR. H GREN**.

*   **Movement**: Living things show movement, which can be external (changing location) or internal (movement of substances within the organism).

*   **Respiration**: The chemical processes in which living things use substances from their environment (like glucose) to release energy for life processes.

*   **Homeostasis**: The ability of living things to maintain a stable and constant internal environment, despite changes in the external environment.

*   **Growth**: The ability of living things to increase in size or change shape.

*   **Reproduction**: The process by which living things produce offspring, either sexually or asexually, to ensure the continuation of the species.

*   **Excretion**: The removal of metabolic waste products from the body to prevent them from becoming toxic.

*   **Nutrition**: The intake and use of materials and gases from the environment for energy, growth, and repair.

---

## Cell Theory

The development of the cell theory was a collective effort of many scientists. It provides the foundation for understanding biological systems.

<InlineNoteTag label="Cell Theory" notePath="biology-11/2-cell-theory" />

*   **Robert Hooke (1665):** First observed "cells" in cork.
*   **Theodor Schwann (1839):** Concluded that all animals are made of cells.

*   **Rudolf Virchow (1855):** Proposed *Omnis cellula e cellula*, meaning all cells arise from pre-existing cells.

### Modern Cell Theory Tenets
Modern biology has added further depth to the classical theory:
1. Energy flow (metabolism and biochemistry) occurs within cells.
2. Cells contain hereditary information (DNA) which is passed from cell to cell during cell division.
3. All cells are basically the same in chemical composition in organisms of similar species.

---

## Exceptions to Cell Theory

While the cell theory applies to the vast majority of life, there are notable exceptions:
*   **Viruses:** They are acellular (not made of cells) and cannot reproduce outside a host cell.
*   **Atypical Cells:** Mature mammalian Red Blood Cells (RBCs) lack a nucleus, and sieve tube cells in plants also lose their nuclei at maturity. Fungal hyphae can be coenocytic (multinucleated without cell walls).

---

## Structure-Function Relationship

The diverse shapes and sizes of cells are directly related to the specific roles they play within an organism. For example, nerve cells are long to transmit signals over distances, while RBCs are biconcave to maximize surface area for oxygen transport.

# 1.2 Cell Theory

The Cell Theory is a foundational principle in biology, outlining the basic properties of all known living organisms. It was developed over time through the work of several key scientists.

## Historical Development

**Robert Hooke (1665):** An English scientist who was the first to observe and name "cells".

- He examined a thin slice of cork under a simple microscope.
- He saw tiny, empty, honeycomb-like spaces which he called *cells*, reminding him of the small rooms in a monastery.
- What he actually observed were the non-living, thick cell walls of dead cork tree tissue.

<CaptionedImage src="kg26q9excc2kbsdftp5t13ws7d8dgjex" alt="Cork cells" caption="Figure 1.1: Cork cells as observed by Robert Hooke." />

---

**Matthias Schleiden (1838):** A German botanist. He examined numerous plant tissues and concluded that all plants are composed of cells, and that the cell is the ultimate structural unit of plant tissue.

---

**Theodor Schwann (1839):** A German zoologist who studied various animal cells and found they lacked a cell wall, being covered only by a membrane. He recognised the structural similarity between the inside of plant and animal cells and concluded that all animals are also composed of cells.

**Schleiden and Schwann (1839):** They combined their findings to formulate the initial Cell Theory.

---

**Rudolf Virchow (1855):** A German doctor who observed that new cells develop from the division of existing cells. He famously stated, "*Omnis cellula e cellula*" (all cells arise from cells).

For more details on how cells divide, refer to <InlineNoteTag label="Cell Division" notePath="biology-11/12-cell-division" />.

## The Tenets of Cell Theory

The classical cell theory is based on three fundamental principles:

1. The cell is the **fundamental unit of structure and function** in all living things.
2. All organisms are composed of **one or more cells**.
3. All cells arise from **pre-existing cells** through cellular division.

For more information about the basic unit of life, refer to <InlineNoteTag label="Cells The Basic Unit Of Life" notePath="biology-11/1-cells---the-basic-unit-of-life" />.

## Modern Cell Theory and Validation

The modern version of the cell theory incorporates newer discoveries and includes these concepts:

- Energy flow (metabolism) occurs within cells.
- Hereditary information (DNA) is passed from cell to cell during division.
- All cells share the same basic chemical composition.

**Experimental evidence that validates the theory includes:**

- Cells can be removed from tissues and survive independently for short periods in culture.
- No entity smaller than a cell has been found to be able to live independently.
- Experiments by Francesco Redi and Louis Pasteur demonstrated that life (cells) does not arise spontaneously from non-living matter.

For microscopy techniques used to observe cells, refer to <InlineNoteTag label="Microscopy" notePath="biology-11/3-microscopy" />.

## Exceptions to Cell Theory

Some biological entities and cell types do not perfectly fit all the tenets of the cell theory.

**Viruses, Viroids, and Prions:** These are considered *acellular* (not made of cells). They lack their own cellular machinery and can only replicate inside a living host cell, behaving as non-living particles when outside a host.

**Red Blood Cells (RBCs) and Sieve Tube Cells:** These mature cells are highly specialised and lack a nucleus and other organelles. Consequently, they cannot divide to form new cells.

**Bacteria and Cyanobacteria (Prokaryotes):** They lack a well-organised nucleus. Their genetic material (DNA) lies directly in the cytoplasm and is not enclosed by a nuclear membrane.

**Coenocytic or Syncytial Organisms:** Some organisms, such as the fungus *Rhizopus* or the alga *Vaucheria*, have large cytoplasmic masses containing many nuclei that are not separated into individual cells by cell membranes.

**Protozoans:** These are single-celled organisms, but their entire body is just one cell that carries out all life functions. Some consider them *acellular* rather than cellular, as their body is not divided into multiple cells.

# Microscopy

The study of cells and their structures, which are too small to be seen by the naked eye, is made possible through the technique of **microscopy**.

<InlineNoteTag label="Cells The Basic Unit Of Life" notePath="biology-11/1-cells---the-basic-unit-of-life" />

## 1. Light Microscopy

A light microscope, also known as a **compound microscope**, uses visible light and a system of two lenses to magnify specimens: the *ocular lens*, located in the eyepiece, and the *objective lens*, located in the revolving nosepiece. It works by illuminating the specimen with a light source; the *objective lens* then forms an initial image that is *real, inverted, and magnified*, which acts as the object for the *ocular lens (eyepiece)*. The eyepiece in turn produces the *final, virtual, and magnified image* that the observer sees, inverted relative to the original specimen.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930192301.webp" alt="Light microscope" caption="Fig.1.2 Light microscope" />

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930192451.webp" alt="The working principle of light microscope" caption="Fig. 1.3 The working principle of light microscope" />

### Magnification

*Magnification* is the ability of an optical instrument to increase the apparent size of an object. It is represented by the letter 'X'.

$$\text{Magnification} = \frac{\text{Size of Image}}{\text{Actual Size of Object}}$$

-   **Total Magnification:** Calculated by multiplying the magnification of the objective lens by the magnification of the ocular lens.

$$\text{Total Magnification} = \text{Objective Lens Power} \times \text{Ocular Lens Power}$$

-   **Maximum Magnification:** The maximum for a light microscope is typically **1500X** (e.g., a 100X objective lens with a 15X ocular lens).

### Resolution

*Resolution* (or resolving power) is a measure of the clarity of an image; it is the ability to distinguish between two points that are very close together.

-   Higher resolution means the microscope can distinguish between points that are closer together (smaller minimum distance).
-   **Resolving Power (minimum resolvable distance):**
    -   *Naked Eye:* 0.1 mm
    -   *Light Microscope:* **200 nm (0.2 μm)**
    -   *Electron Microscope:* **0.2 nm**
-   Increasing magnification does **not** increase resolution. Beyond a certain point, further magnification only produces a blurry image, this is called *empty magnification*.

## 2. Electron Microscopy

An electron microscope uses a beam of accelerated electrons as its source of illumination, allowing for significantly higher resolution and magnification than a light microscope. An *electron gun* produces a beam of electrons, which *electromagnetic lenses* (condenser lenses) focus onto the specimen; the specimen itself must be an extremely thin section (20–100 nm). As the beam passes through, denser regions of the specimen scatter more electrons than lighter regions, and this scattered electron pattern passes through an *objective lens* and then an *ocular lens* to create a highly magnified image on a fluorescent screen.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930192524.webp" alt="Working principle of electron microscope" caption="Fig.1.5 Working principle of electron microscope" />

An electron microscope can magnify specimens from 1 to **50 million times**, and has a resolving power of **0.2 nm**, approximately 1000× better than a light microscope.

### Types of Electron Microscopes

1. **Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM):** Electrons pass *through* an ultra-thin section of the specimen. Used to study the **internal ultrastructure** of cells and organelles.
2. **Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM):** Electrons are *scanned across* the surface of the specimen. Used to study the **surface architecture (3D topography)** of a specimen.

## Comparison: Light vs. Electron Microscope

| Feature | Light Microscope | Electron Microscope |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |
| **Illumination Source** | Visible light (wavelength ~400–700 nm) | Beam of electrons (wavelength ~1 nm) |
| **Maximum Magnification** | ~1500X | Up to 50,000,000X |
| **Resolution** | ~200 nm (0.2 μm) | ~0.2 nm |
| **Specimen State** | Can view both live and dead specimens | Can only view dead and dried specimens |
| **Image Formation** | Based on light absorption/transmission | Based on electron scattering |
| **Image Color** | Usually colored | Black and white |
| **Operational Needs** | No vacuum required | Requires a vacuum |
| **Lenses Used** | Glass lenses | Electromagnetic lenses |

## Tools and Units in Ultrastructure Studies

**Electron Microscopes**: Powerful microscopes required to view cellular ultrastructure. <InlineNoteTag label="Microscopy" notePath="biology-11/3-microscopy" />

- **Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)**: Forms an image by passing a beam of electrons *through* a very thin specimen. It is used to view the internal structure of organelles.
- **Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM)**: Forms an image by scanning the surface of a specimen with a focused beam of electrons, detecting *secondary or backscattered electrons*. It produces detailed 3D images of the specimen's surface.

**Svedberg Unit (S)**: A non-metric unit used to measure the sedimentation rate of a particle (like a ribosome) during centrifugation.

- The rate depends on the particle's *molecular mass, volume, and shape*.
- Larger, more compact particles have a higher Svedberg value. For example, eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S, while prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S. <InlineNoteTag label="Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells" notePath="biology-11/9-prokaryotic-and-eukaryotic-cells" />

## Possible Questions and Answers

**Q:** What is cellular ultrastructure?
**A:** It is the fine, detailed structure of a cell and its organelles that can only be observed using an electron microscope.

**Q:** What are the three main structural differences between an animal cell and a plant cell?
**A:** Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole, all of which are absent in animal cells. Animal cells have centrioles, which are absent in higher plant cells.

**Q:** What is the difference between a TEM and an SEM?
**A:** A TEM (Transmission Electron Microscope) transmits electrons *through* a specimen to show internal structures, while an SEM (Scanning Electron Microscope) scans the *surface* of a specimen to create a 3D image.

# 1.5 Cell Wall and Plasma Membrane

The cell wall is a rigid, extracellular structure found outside the plasma membrane. It is not present in animal cells, likely due to their locomotor lifestyle.

It is present in plant cells, prokaryotes, fungi, and some protists. It protects the cell, maintains the cell's shape, prevents excessive uptake of water (preventing lysis), and provides structural support to the entire plant against gravity. The cell wall itself is porous and fully permeable, allowing free passage of water and dissolved substances.

### Chemical Composition

The chemical makeup of the cell wall varies between different types of organisms.

| Organism    | Main Component  |
|:---------- |:---------------|
| Plants      | Cellulose       |
| Fungi       | Chitin          |
| Prokaryotes | Peptidoglycan   |

### Structure of the Plant Cell Wall

The plant cell wall is secreted by the cell and consists of three main layers.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930192749.webp" alt="Plant cell wall layers" caption="Figure 1.9: Plant cell wall layers" />

1. **Primary Cell Wall.** A true wall that develops in newly growing cells, lying inner to the middle lamella. It is thin, flexible, and adapted for growth, stretching irreversibly as the cell expands. It is composed of cellulose microfibrils arranged in a crisscross pattern within a matrix of other polysaccharides like *hemicelluloses* and *pectin*, an arrangement that provides great strength.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930192829.webp" alt="Crisscross arrangement of cellulose" caption="Figure 1.8: Crisscross arrangement of cellulose" />

2. **Secondary Cell Wall.** Forms only after a cell has stopped growing, lying between the primary cell wall and the plasma membrane. It is found in specialized cells like sclerenchyma, which are often dead at maturity. It is very thick and rigid, and does not allow for further growth. It consists of cellulose, hemicelluloses, *lignin*, inorganic salts, and waxes, with lignin cementing the cellulose fibers together to provide rigidity, giving the cell definite shape and strong mechanical support.

3. **Middle Lamella.** A layer present between the primary walls of adjacent cells. It acts as a cement, holding the cells together to form tissues, and is a sticky, gel-like substance made of pectin, calcium, and magnesium salts.

## Plasma Membrane

Also called the *cell membrane*, it is the living boundary of the protoplasm and is found in all prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. It is *selectively permeable*, controlling the passage of materials into and out of the cell to maintain a stable internal environment (homeostasis).

<InlineNoteTag label="Membrane Transport Mechanism" notePath="biology-11/7-membrane-transport-mechanism" />

### Composition

The plasma membrane is chemically composed mostly of proteins (60-80%) and lipids (20-40%, mainly phospholipids and cholesterol), with a small quantity of carbohydrates found on the outer surface.

### Structure: The Fluid Mosaic Model

This model describes the plasma membrane as a dynamic and fluid structure.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930192937.webp" alt="Fluid mosaic model of plasma membrane" caption="Figure 1.10: Fluid mosaic model of plasma membrane" />

The core of the membrane is a double layer of phospholipids, the phospholipid bilayer, whose *hydrophilic (water-loving) heads* face the watery environments inside and outside the cell while the *hydrophobic (water-fearing) tails* face inward, away from water. Protein molecules are embedded either partially or wholly within the bilayer, like "icebergs floating in a sea," and these proteins can drift sideways. Cholesterol molecules are wedged between phospholipids, helping to stabilize the membrane and regulate its fluidity. Chains of carbohydrates are attached to proteins (*glycoproteins*) or lipids (*glycolipids*) on the outer surface, where they act as cell surface markers. Filaments of the cytoskeleton are attached to the inner surface, providing support.

### Functions of Membrane Components

Proteins determine most of the membrane's functions. Some act as channels or carriers that transport substances across the membrane, others catalyze metabolic reactions on the membrane surface (enzymatic activity), some function as receptors that bind specific molecules like hormones to transmit signals into the cell (signal transduction), and others act as antigens that let cells recognize each other (cell recognition). Glycoproteins and glycolipids act as *cell surface markers* for cell-to-cell recognition, together forming the glycocalyx, and help cells stick together to form tissues. Lipids, the phospholipids and cholesterol, control the fluidity of the membrane and restrict the passage of polar molecules and ions.

# 1.6 Cell Signaling

The process by which cells communicate with other cells or their external environment, involving the transfer of information to elicit a response. This is a crucial aspect of maintaining homeostasis and coordinating multicellular functions.

## Cell Signaling

Cell signaling is the fundamental process of cellular communication, where a cell detects and responds to signals in its environment. This process is vital for the Ultrastructure of Animal and Plant Cells to function as a cohesive unit.

## Ligands

Signaling molecules that initiate the process. They can be diverse, including proteins, lipids, amino acids, and gases. Also known as the *first messenger*.

## Receptors

Proteins that specifically bind to ligands, initiating a response. Each receptor is typically specific to one type of ligand, often located on the Plasma Membrane.

## Stages of Cell Signaling

Cell signaling occurs in three primary stages:

1.  **Reception:** The target cell's detection of an external signal molecule (ligand) when it binds to a receptor protein on the cell surface or inside the cell.
2.  **Transduction:** The conversion of the signal into a form that can bring about a specific cellular response. This often involves a multi-step pathway called a **signal transduction pathway**.
3.  **Response:** The final action triggered by the transduced signal. This can include enzyme catalysis, rearrangement of the cytoskeleton, or the activation of specific genes.

## Types of Signaling Pathways

### Protein Signaling

This pathway is used by *water-soluble* (hydrophilic) ligands like protein and peptide hormones that cannot pass through the cell's plasma membrane.

**Mechanism:**

1.  The ligand (*first messenger*) binds to a specific receptor on the outer surface of the plasma membrane.
2.  This binding triggers a series of intracellular events, often leading to the generation of a **second messenger** (e.g., $\mathrm{cAMP}$, Cyclic Adenosine Monophosphate).
3.  The second messenger relays the signal inside the cell, triggering the final response (e.g., activating enzymes, gene activation).

![](/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930193110.webp)

### Steroid Signaling

This pathway is used by small, *hydrophobic* (lipophilic or "fat-loving") ligands like steroid hormones. Because of their chemical nature, they interact differently with the Membrane Transport Mechanism.

**Mechanism:**

1.  The steroid hormone diffuses freely across the plasma membrane.
2.  It binds to an internal receptor located in the cytoplasm or the nucleus.
3.  This forms an active **receptor-hormone complex**.
4.  The complex moves into the nucleus and binds directly to $\mathrm{DNA}$, acting as a **transcription factor** to regulate gene expression (turning genes on or off).

**Examples:** Estrogen, progesterone, testosterone.

![](/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930193218.webp)

## Comparison of Signaling Pathways

| Feature | Protein Signaling | Steroid Signaling |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |
| **Ligand Type** | Water-soluble (hydrophilic) proteins/peptides | Fat-soluble (lipophilic) steroids |
| **Membrane Permeability** | Cannot cross plasma membrane | Can diffuse across plasma membrane |
| **Receptor Location** | On the cell surface (plasma membrane) | Intracellular (cytoplasm or nucleus) |
| **Signal Transduction** | Involves second messengers (e.g., $\mathrm{cAMP}$) | Receptor-hormone complex acts directly |
| **Primary Action** | Activates enzymes, changes cell metabolism | Acts as a transcription factor to alter gene expression |
| **Response Speed** | Generally faster | Generally slower |

## Biological Significance

Cell signaling is essential for coordinating cellular activities, allowing for growth, development, immunity, and homeostasis in multicellular organisms. It is closely linked to processes like Cell Division and the differentiation of Stem Cells.

# Membrane Transport Mechanism

The main concepts related to the mechanisms controlling the movement of solutes (ions, small molecules) across a biological membrane. This transport is dependent on the membrane's permeability, solute concentration, and the size and charge of the solute.

<InlineNoteTag label="Cells The Basic Unit Of Life" notePath="biology-11/1-cells---the-basic-unit-of-life" />

## Passive Transport

A type of transport that does **not** require cellular energy (ATP). Molecules move *down* a concentration gradient (from high to low concentration).

### Simple Diffusion

Simple diffusion is the movement of molecules from a region of *higher concentration* to a region of *lower concentration*, down a **concentration gradient**, without the aid of transport proteins. The **concentration gradient** is the difference in the concentration of a substance between two regions. Small, non-polar molecules like *oxygen* ($\text{O}_2$), *carbon dioxide* ($\text{CO}_2$), and *water* can pass directly through the phospholipid bilayer of the cell membrane by this mechanism, which equalizes the concentration of these molecules inside and outside the cell, with each type of molecule moving down its own gradient independently.

### Facilitated Diffusion

Facilitated diffusion is a type of passive transport in which molecules move from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration, *assisted by transport proteins*, without using ATP. It is used for molecules that cannot easily cross the phospholipid bilayer, polar molecules, larger ions, and large molecules, and it relies on two types of transport proteins. **Channel proteins** create aqueous pores in the membrane for charged or polar molecules to pass through: *open channel proteins* are always open, while *gated channel proteins* can be opened or closed to regulate substance flow. **Carrier proteins** bind to specific molecules, undergo a conformational (shape) change, and release the molecules on the other side of the membrane, and their rate is affected by temperature and saturation.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930193258.webp" alt="Facilitated diffusion" caption="Fig. 1.13 Facilitated diffusion" />

### Comparison: Simple vs. Facilitated Diffusion

| Feature | Simple Diffusion | Facilitated Diffusion |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |
| Type | Passive transport | Passive transport |
| Direction | Higher → Lower concentration | Higher → Lower concentration |
| Energy (ATP) | Not required | Not required |
| Transport proteins | **Not required** | **Required** (channel or carrier) |
| Rate | Generally slower | Faster, but limited by protein availability |
| Molecules transported | Small nonpolar (O₂, CO₂) | Polar molecules (glucose, amino acids), ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻) |

### Osmosis

Osmosis is the net movement of solvent molecules (typically water) from a solution of *low solute concentration* (high water potential) to a solution of *high solute concentration* (low water potential) through a **semipermeable membrane**, a membrane that allows solvent molecules (like water) to pass through but not larger solute molecules (like sugar or salt). Two related concepts describe this movement: **osmotic pressure** is the minimum pressure needed to prevent the inward flow of water across a semipermeable membrane, and **water potential** is a measure of the tendency of water to move from one area to another, with pure water having the highest water potential.

Tonicity determines the direction and effect of this water movement. In a **hypotonic solution** (lower solute than the cell), water enters the cell, so an animal cell swells and may lyse while a plant cell becomes **turgid**. In a **hypertonic solution** (higher solute than the cell), water leaves the cell, so an animal cell shrinks (crenation) while a plant cell undergoes **plasmolysis**. In an **isotonic solution** (equal solute), there is no net water movement.

Everyday examples of osmosis include feeling thirsty after eating salty food, the swelling of raisins and seeds when soaked in water, and kidney dialysis.

## Active Transport

Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of *lower concentration* to a region of *higher concentration*, **against a concentration gradient**, requiring cellular energy. It requires energy in the form of **ATP**, carried out by specific membrane proteins (pumps).

### Types of Active Transport

**Primary active transport** directly uses chemical energy (ATP) to transport molecules against their gradient. Its key example is the **Sodium-Potassium Pump (Na⁺/K⁺ pump)**, which pumps **3 Na⁺ ions out** of the cell and **2 K⁺ ions in** per cycle, against their respective gradients; this process consumes over one-third of a cell's total ATP.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930193331.webp" alt="Sodium- Potassium pump" caption="Fig.1.14 Sodium- Potassium pump." />

**Secondary active transport** uses an **electrochemical gradient** (created by primary active transport) as an indirect energy source. A transporter protein couples the movement of an ion (e.g., Na⁺) *down* its gradient to the movement of another molecule *against* its gradient.

In plants, root cells use active transport to absorb minerals and water from the soil, allowing the plant to accumulate nutrients at concentrations higher than in the surrounding soil.

Further examples of active transport include the movement of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ions out of cardiac muscle cells, the transportation of amino acids across the intestinal lining, the secretion of proteins like enzymes and hormones, and mineral uptake by root hair cells in plants.

| **Facilitated Diffusion** | No | High → Low | Yes (channel/carrier) | Glucose, Na⁺, Cl⁻ |
| **Osmosis** | No | High → Low water potential | No (aquaporins optional) | Water movement |
| **Active Transport** | Yes (ATP) | Low → High | Yes (pumps) | Na⁺/K⁺ pump, mineral uptake |

**Biological Significance**: Membrane transport is fundamental for life, enabling cells to acquire nutrients, eliminate waste products, maintain ion gradients, and communicate with their environment, thereby maintaining homeostasis.

# Cytoplasm and Organelles

This section outlines the structure and function of the cytoplasm and the various organelles found within eukaryotic cells. These specialized compartments allow for efficient and organized cellular activity.

<InlineNoteTag label="Cells The Basic Unit Of Life" notePath="biology-11/1-cells---the-basic-unit-of-life" />

**Cytoplasm and Organelles**

The **protoplasm** is the entire living matter of a cell. In eukaryotic cells, the protoplasm is physically divided into two major regions: the **cytoplasm** and the **nucleus**.

The **cytoplasm** is the cellular region extending between the nuclear membrane (envelope) and the plasma membrane. It is a fundamental component present in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. It consists of approximately **90% water** and forms a solution that contains the essential biochemicals of life, organized into two physical states: a **true solution**, a homogeneous mixture of ions and small molecules such as salts, sugars, amino acids, fatty acids, nucleotides, vitamins, and dissolved gases, and a **colloidal solution**, a heterogeneous mixture formed by larger molecules, primarily proteins, suspended in the fluid medium. In general, the cytoplasm acts as a major storehouse of raw materials and biochemicals required by the cell and serves as a primary site of cellular metabolism.

The **cytosol** is the specific, water-soluble liquid phase of the cytoplasm in an intact cell, forming the fluid matrix surrounding the organelles. Because prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles, the cytosol is the site where almost all chemical reactions of metabolism take place. In eukaryotes, the cytosol surrounds the membrane-bound organelles and serves as the primary medium for major metabolic pathways, including **glycolysis** (the anaerobic breakdown of glucose), **protein synthesis** (translation), and various other biochemical reactions and metabolic pathways.

## **Cell Organelles**

These are highly organized, discrete structures within the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells, each performing a specific function.

### **Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)**
An extensive network of interconnected membranous tubules and flattened sacs called *cisternae*. The internal space is known as the *ER lumen*.

-   **Rough ER (RER):**
    -   *Structure:* Its cytoplasmic surface is studded with **ribosomes**, giving it a "rough" appearance.
    -   *Function:*
        1.  **Protein Synthesis:** Synthesizes proteins destined for secretion, insertion into membranes, or delivery to certain organelles.
        2.  **Protein Modification:** Modifies and folds proteins within the ER lumen.
        3.  **Membrane Factory:** Produces membrane proteins and phospholipids for the cell, which can be transferred via *transport vesicles*.

-   **Smooth ER (SER):**
    -   *Structure:* Lacks ribosomes, resulting in a smooth surface.
    -   *Function:*
        1.  **Lipid Synthesis:** Synthesizes lipids, including oils, phospholipids, cholesterol, and steroid hormones.
        2.  **Detoxification:** Detoxifies drugs and poisons, particularly in liver cells.
        3.  **Calcium Storage:** Stores calcium ions. In muscle cells, it is a specialized form called the *sarcoplasmic reticulum*.

| Feature | **Rough ER (RER)** | **Smooth ER (SER)** |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |
| **Ribosomes** | Present | Absent |
| **Primary Function**| Protein synthesis and modification, membrane production | Lipid synthesis, detoxification, Ca²⁺ storage |
| **Structure** | Network of flattened sacs (cisternae) | Network of tubules |

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930193439.webp" alt="Endoplasmic reticulum" caption="Figure 1.15 Endoplasmic reticulum, showing both rough and smooth regions." />

### **Ribosomes**
Non-membranous organelles responsible for *protein synthesis*.
-   *Composition:* Made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein.
-   *Structure & Types:* Composed of two subunits (large and small).
    -   **Eukaryotic Ribosomes:** 80S (composed of 60S and 40S subunits).
    -   **Prokaryotic Ribosomes:** 70S (composed of 50S and 30S subunits).
-   **Polysome:** A chain of multiple ribosomes translating the same mRNA molecule simultaneously, allowing for rapid production of many protein copies.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930193552.webp" alt="Ribosome structure" caption="Fig.1.16: Eukaryotic 80S ribosome structure." />

### **Golgi Complex (Apparatus)**
A stack of flattened, membrane-bound sacs (*cisternae*) that functions as a cellular "post office."
-   *Structure:* Has a receiving (*cis* face) and a shipping (*trans* face).
-   *Function:*
    1.  **Modification:** Modifies proteins and lipids received from the ER (e.g., glycosylation).
    2.  **Sorting & Packaging:** Sorts and packages these molecules into vesicles for transport to other destinations.
    3.  **Formation of other Organelles:** Produces lysosomes and peroxisomes.
    4.  **Plant Cell Division:** In plants, forms the *phragmoplast* (cell plate) during cytokinesis.

<InlineNoteTag label="Cell Division" notePath="biology-11/12-cell-division" />

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930193613.webp" alt="Golgi complex" caption="Fig. 1.18: Golgi complex." />

### **Lysosomes**
Single-membraned vesicles containing powerful *hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes*.
-   *Function:* Act as the cell's waste disposal and recycling system.
    -   **Phagocytosis:** Digest food particles or foreign invaders taken in by the cell.
    -   **Autophagy:** Break down and recycle the cell's own old or damaged organelles ("self-eating").
    -   **Autolysis:** Programmed cell death where lysosomes rupture and digest the entire cell, earning them the nickname *"suicidal bags"*.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930193750.webp" alt="Functions of Lysosomes" caption="Fig. 1.22: Functions of Lysosomes, including phagocytosis and autophagy." />

### **Microbodies (Peroxisomes & Glyoxysomes)**
Small, single-membraned vesicles containing specialized enzymes.
-   **Peroxisomes:** Contain oxidative enzymes like *catalase* and *peroxidase*.
    -   *Function:* Break down fatty acids and detoxify harmful substances (e.g., alcohol) by forming and then neutralizing hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$). Involved in photorespiration in plants.
-   **Glyoxysomes:** Specialized peroxisomes found in plant seeds.
    -   *Function:* Convert stored fatty acids into carbohydrates for energy during germination via the *glyoxylate cycle*.

### **Vacuoles**
Large, membrane-bound sacs with diverse functions. The membrane is called the *tonoplast*.
-   **Food Vacuoles:** Formed by phagocytosis to enclose food particles (in animals and protists).
-   **Contractile Vacuoles:** Pump excess water out of freshwater protists.
-   **Central Vacuole (Plants):** A large vacuole in mature plant cells.
    -   *Function:* Stores water, nutrients, pigments, and waste products in the *cell sap*. Maintains *turgor pressure*, providing structural support to the cell.

### **Mitochondria**
The "powerhouses" of the cell, responsible for cellular respiration and ATP generation.
-   *Structure:* Enclosed by a double membrane.
    -   **Outer Membrane:** Smooth and permeable.
    -   **Inner Membrane:** Folded into *cristae* to increase surface area. Contains the electron transport chain and *ATP synthase* enzymes.
    -   **Mitochondrial Matrix:** The inner compartment containing circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, and enzymes for the Krebs cycle.
-   **Self-Replicating:** Can divide by binary fission.
-   **Endosymbiont Theory:** Mitochondria are thought to have evolved from ancient prokaryotes that were engulfed by a host cell. Evidence includes their circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, and double membrane.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930193819.webp" alt="Structure of a Mitochondrion" caption="Fig. 1.25: Structure of a Mitochondrion." />

### **Plastids (in Plants and Algae)**
A family of related organelles found exclusively in plant cells and algae, each enclosed by a double membrane.

-   **Chloroplasts:** The site of *photosynthesis*.
    -   *Structure:* Enclosed by a double membrane envelope surrounding the **stroma** (fluid matrix). Within the stroma are stacks of membrane-bound discs called **thylakoids**, organized into stacks called **grana**. Thylakoid membranes contain photosynthetic pigments (chlorophyll).
    -   *Function:* Convert light energy into chemical energy (glucose) via photosynthesis.
    -   *Endosymbiont Evidence:* Like mitochondria, chloroplasts contain circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, supporting the Endosymbiotic Theory.

-   **Chromoplasts:** Contain pigments (carotenoids) other than chlorophyll.
    -   *Function:* Provide yellow, orange, and red colours to flowers and fruits, attracting pollinators and seed dispersers.

-   **Leucoplasts:** Colourless plastids found in non-photosynthetic tissues (roots, seeds).
    -   *Function:* Store starch (**amyloplasts**), oils (**elaioplasts**), or proteins (**proteinoplasts**).

| Plastid | Pigment | Location | Function |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |:--- |
| Chloroplast | Chlorophyll (green) | Leaves, green stems | Photosynthesis |
| Chromoplast | Carotenoids (yellow/orange/red) | Flowers, fruits | Colour attraction |
| Leucoplast | None (colourless) | Roots, seeds | Storage |

> **Note:** Plastids can interconvert, e.g., leucoplasts can develop into chloroplasts when exposed to light (greening of potatoes).

### **Centrioles**
Non-membranous cell organelles found mainly in animal cells, as well as in fungi-like protists (such as slime molds and water molds). They are rod-shaped structures that typically occur in pairs, positioned at right angles to each other near one pole of the nucleus, and each individual centriole is composed of nine triplets of microtubules arranged circularly around a central axis.

During cell division, just before the division begins, the centriole pair duplicates to form two pairs, which then migrate to opposite sides of the nucleus, and both pairs generate microtubules (spindle fibers). The complete network of spindle fibers is called the **mitotic apparatus**, which coordinates the distribution of chromosomes to daughter cells. Centrioles also give rise to **basal bodies**, which serve as the anchor points for cilia and flagella.

---

### **Cytoskeleton**
A network of fibrous structures distributed throughout the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells, extending from the nucleus to the plasma membrane. It is composed of three distinct types of fibers: **microfilaments**, **microtubules**, and **intermediate filaments**.

---

### **Science Titbits (Cell Nucleation States)**
Cells vary in their nucleus count depending on their type, stage of development, and function. **Enucleated cells** are exceptional living cells that lack a nucleus at maturity (e.g., human red blood cells and plant sieve tube cells). **Binucleate / dikaryotic cells** are cells that contain exactly two nuclei, and **multinucleate / coenocytic cells** are cells containing many nuclei.

---

### **The Nucleus**
The most prominent and vital organelle within a eukaryotic cell.

In **animal cells**, the nucleus is located centrally, with the exception of muscle fiber cells, while in **adult plant cells** it is pushed slightly away from the center due to the presence of a large central vacuole. A typical eukaryotic nucleus is composed of four main parts: the nuclear envelope, nucleoplasm, nucleoli, and chromatin.

#### **1. Nuclear Envelope (Nuclear Membrane)**

The nuclear envelope is a double-membrane barrier consisting of an outer and inner lipid bilayer separated by a fluid-filled **perinuclear space**. The outer membrane is directly connected with the membranes of the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) and is covered with ribosomes on its outer surface. Numerous **nuclear pores**, openings where the inner and outer nuclear membranes fuse together, are made of a specialized transport protein complex called **nucleoporin**. These pores regulate the bidirectional flow of materials between the nucleus and the cytoplasm: RNA molecules and ribosomal proteins are exported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, while carbohydrates, signaling molecules, lipids, and proteins (such as DNA polymerase) are imported from the cytoplasm into the nucleus. Small molecules pass through by simple diffusion, while larger macromolecules are recognized by specific signal sequences and actively transported through the pores via nucleoporin.

#### **2. Nucleoplasm**

The nucleoplasm is the transparent, semi-fluid ground substance of the nucleus, chemically distinct from the cytoplasm. It is composed of histone and non-histone proteins, free nucleotides and metal cofactors (such as $\text{Mg}^{2+}$) essential for nucleic acid synthesis, and enzymes needed to produce DNA and RNA (e.g., DNA and RNA polymerases).

#### **3. Nucleolus**

The nucleolus is a non-membrane-bound structure suspended in the nucleoplasm, and a cell may possess one or more nucleoli. It is visible during interphase but temporarily disappears during cell division. It has two internal zones: a **peripheral granular area**, which contains ribosomal subunits in development, and a **central fibrillar area**, which contains ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal DNA (rDNA). Its function is primarily ribosome biogenesis, the construction and assembly of ribosomes.

#### **4. Chromatin and Chromosomes**

**Chromatin** is a relaxed network of thin, thread-like structures composed of DNA and associated proteins (histones). During cell division, chromatin fibers condense and coil tightly into thick, distinct structures called **chromosomes**, visible under a light microscope. A typical duplicated chromosome consists of two sister **chromatids** joined together at a constricted region called the **centromere**, a specialized constriction related to chromosome movement during cell division. **Kinetochores** are complex protein structures situated on opposite sides of the centromere constriction, serving as attachment sites for spindle fibers.

Two primary types of structurally different cells have evolved: **prokaryotic cells** and **eukaryotic cells**. Prokaryotic cells include organisms like *archaea*, *bacteria*, and *cyanobacteria*, while all other forms of life (plants, animals, fungi, protists) are composed of eukaryotic cells.

# 1.10 Stem Cells

Stem cells are undifferentiated or partially differentiated cells that can differentiate into various cell types and proliferate indefinitely to produce more of the same stem cell. They are fundamental to an organism's development, growth, repair, and maintenance.

<InlineNoteTag label="Cells The Basic Unit Of Life" notePath="biology-11/1-cells---the-basic-unit-of-life" />

## Stem Cell

A cell with the unique ability to develop into specialized cell types in the body. They are characterized by two key properties:

1.  **Self-Renewal:** The ability to divide and produce more stem cells.
2.  **Differentiation:** The ability to develop into specialized cells (e.g., nerve cells, blood cells, muscle cells) under the right conditions.

**Advantages of Stem Cells:**

- Provide new cells to replace those that are damaged or lost.
- Can divide repeatedly to produce a continuous supply of new cells.
- Can differentiate into various types of cells needed by the body.

**Benefits of Stem Cell Therapy:**
Potential treatments for spinal cord injuries, Type 1 diabetes, Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, heart disease, stroke, burns, cancer, and osteoarthritis.

## Main Types of Stem Cells

The three main types of stem cells discussed in research are:

| Type | Description | Potency | Source |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |:--- |
| **Adult Stem Cells** | Supply new cells as an organism grows and to replace damaged cells. | *Multipotent* (can only become a limited range of cell types). | Various tissues in the body (e.g., bone marrow, skin). |
| **Embryonic Stem Cells (ESCs)** | Provide new cells for a developing embryo. | *Pluripotent* (can become any cell type in the body). | Early-stage embryo (blastocyst). |
| **Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (iPSCs)** | Specialized adult cells that are reprogrammed in a laboratory to an embryonic stem cell-like state. | *Pluripotent* (can develop into any cell type). | Laboratory-generated from adult somatic cells. |

<CaptionedImage src="kg232khw8820mka018s0canf2x8dh4kx" alt="Stem cells diagram" caption="Figure 1.31: Stem cells - the body's master cells" />

## Classification by Differentiation Potential (Potency)

Stem cells are categorized based on their ability to differentiate into other cell types.

| Potency | Differentiation Potential | Example |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |
| **Totipotent** | Can differentiate into **all** possible cell types, including the extraembryonic tissues (placenta). | The first few cells of a zygote after division. |
| **Pluripotent** | Can differentiate into **almost any** cell type of the three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm). | Embryonic Stem Cells (ESCs), Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (iPSCs). |
| **Multipotent** | Can differentiate into a **closely related family** of cells. | Adult hematopoietic stem cells (can become red/white blood cells, platelets). |
| **Oligopotent**| Can differentiate into a **few different** cell types. | Adult lymphoid or myeloid stem cells. |
| **Unipotent** | Can only produce **one type of cell** (its own kind), but can still self-renew. | Adult muscle stem cells. |

## Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (iPSCs)

iPSCs are a significant breakthrough in stem cell research, first described by Shinya Yamanaka in 2006.

**Generation:** Scientists reintroduce specific signals (genes) into a specialized cell (like a skin cell). These signals switch off genes that define the cell's specialty and switch on genes that revert it to a stem cell-like state.

**Advantages:**

- Can give rise to almost any desired cell type.
- Avoids the ethical concerns associated with using human embryos.
- Can be generated from a patient's own cells, reducing the risk of immune rejection for new organs or tissues.

**Disadvantages:**

- The use of retroviruses to deliver reprogramming genes can be risky, as they can insert DNA randomly into the genome and potentially trigger cancer-causing genes.
- There is a risk of tumor formation (cancer) from the overgrowth of transplanted iPSCs.

## Possible Questions/Answers

**Q:** What is a stem cell?
**A:** A cell with the unique ability to self-renew (create more stem cells) and differentiate into specialized cell types in the body.

**Q:** What is the difference between pluripotent and multipotent stem cells?
**A:** *Pluripotent* stem cells (like embryonic stem cells) can become any cell type in the body. *Multipotent* stem cells (like adult stem cells) are more limited and can only differentiate into a closely related family of cells.

**Q:** What are iPSCs and why are they important?
**A:** Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (iPSCs) are specialized adult cells that have been reprogrammed in a lab to become like embryonic stem cells. They are important because they can be created from a patient's own cells, avoiding ethical issues and reducing the risk of immune rejection in therapies.

## Biological Significance

Stem cells are the foundation of regenerative medicine, offering the potential to repair or replace damaged tissues and treat a wide range of diseases, from heart disease to neurodegenerative disorders.

# 1.11 Endocytosis and Exocytosis

These are processes of **active, bulk transport** used to move large molecules, bacteria, and waste materials across the cell membrane that cannot pass through the lipid bilayer via diffusion.

Both processes require energy (ATP) and involve the formation and fusion of vesicles with the cell membrane. For a deeper understanding of how the membrane structure facilitates this, see <InlineNoteTag label="Cell Wall and Plasma Membrane" notePath="biology-11/5-cell-wall-and-plasma-membrane" /> and <InlineNoteTag label="Membrane Transport Mechanism" notePath="biology-11/7-membrane-transport-mechanism" />.

---

## Endocytosis

Endocytosis is the general term for the process of bringing substances **into** the cell. It involves the cell membrane folding inward (invaginating) to surround the target material and budding off to form an intracellular vesicle.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930194226.webp" alt="Endocytosis diagram" caption="Figure 1.32: Endocytosis showing the cell membrane engulfing extracellular material." />

There are two main subtypes of endocytosis.

### 1. Phagocytosis ("Cell Eating")

- **Definition:** The ingestion of large, solid particles like other cells or bacteria.
- **Process:**
  1. Extensions of the cytoplasm called **pseudopodia** ("false feet") surround the target particle.
  2. The pseudopodia fuse, enclosing the particle within a large vesicle called a **phagosome** or food vacuole.
- **Examples:**
  - *Amoeba* uses phagocytosis for feeding.
  - In higher animals, immune cells like **white blood cells** (phagocytes) use it as a defensive mechanism to destroy pathogens and antigens.

### 2. Pinocytosis ("Cell Drinking")

- **Definition:** The ingestion of extracellular fluid and its dissolved solutes, such as ions, enzymes, and hormones.
- **Process:** The cell membrane invaginates (folds inward) to trap a droplet of fluid, then buds off to form a small vesicle called a **pinosome**.

### Receptor-Mediated Endocytosis

A specific type of pinocytosis.

- **Process:** Macromolecules bind to specific **receptors** on the cell surface. These receptors cluster in regions called **coated pits** (often coated with the protein **clathrin**). The pit then invaginates and forms a **coated vesicle**, bringing the specific target molecule (ligand) into the cell.
- **Example:** Cholesterol absorption by cells.

| Feature | Phagocytosis | Pinocytosis |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |
| **Nickname** | "Cell Eating" | "Cell Drinking" |
| **Material Ingested** | Large, solid particles (e.g., bacteria, cell debris) | Extracellular fluid and dissolved solutes |
| **Vesicle Formed** | Phagosome (large) | Pinosome (small) |
| **Mechanism** | Extension of pseudopodia | Invagination of the cell membrane |
| **Specificity** | Non-specific (but can be triggered) | Generally non-specific, but highly specific in receptor-mediated endocytosis |

---

## Exocytosis

Exocytosis is the process of moving materials **from inside** the cell **to the outside**. It is a form of **active transport**. Substances are first packaged into **vesicles** within the cell, often by the Golgi apparatus. The vesicle is then transported to the cell membrane, where its phospholipid bilayer fuses with the cell's plasma membrane, releasing the vesicle's contents into the extracellular fluid. Examples include the release of **neurotransmitters** at a synapse between nerve cells (see <InlineNoteTag label="Cell Signaling" notePath="biology-11/6-cell-signaling" />), the secretion of hormones or enzymes from glandular cells, and the release of waste products.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930194411.webp" alt="Exocytosis diagram" caption="Figure 1.33: Exocytosis showing a vesicle fusing with the plasma membrane to release contents." />

# Cell Division

Cell division is the fundamental process by which a parent cell divides into two or more daughter cells, ensuring the continuity of life. It is essential for growth, repair, and reproduction. The two main types are **mitosis** (for somatic cells) and **meiosis** (for germ line cells).

## Cell Cycle

The cell cycle is the ordered series of events a cell undergoes, leading to its division and the production of two daughter cells. It is broadly divided into two main phases: **Interphase** and the **M (Mitotic) phase**.

<InlineNoteTag label="Cells The Basic Unit Of Life" notePath="biology-11/1-cells---the-basic-unit-of-life" />

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930194547.webp" alt="Cell cycle" caption="Figure 1.34: Cell cycle" />

**Interphase** is the longest stage of the cell cycle, during which the cell grows and copies its DNA in preparation for division. It has three sub-stages: in the **G1 phase (First Gap)**, the cell grows by producing proteins and cytoplasmic organelles; in the **S phase (Synthesis)**, the cell duplicates its chromosomes through DNA replication; and in the **G2 phase (Second Gap)**, the cell continues to grow and completes its preparations for division. Some cells instead enter the **G0 phase**, a quiescent stage where they exit the cell cycle and perform their normal metabolic functions without active division.

The **M phase (Mitotic phase)** is the shortest part of the cell cycle and includes both nuclear and cytoplasmic division: **mitosis (karyokinesis)** is division of the nucleus, and **cytokinesis** is division of the cytoplasm. Throughout the cycle, **cell cycle checkpoints** (at G1, G2, and during mitosis) act as control mechanisms that ensure the cell cycle proceeds correctly and without errors.

## Mitosis

Mitosis is the division of a cell's nucleus in which the chromosomes are duplicated and distributed equally, resulting in **two genetically identical diploid ($2n$) daughter cells**. It is a continuous process divided into five phases for descriptive purposes.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930194643.webp" alt="Stages of mitosis" caption="Figure 1.35: Stages of mitosis" />

### Phases of Mitosis

During **interphase**, the cell prepares for mitosis: the nuclear envelope is intact, chromosomes are duplicated but not condensed (visible as chromatin), and two centrosomes have formed.

In **prophase**, chromatin fibers coil and condense into visible chromosomes, and each chromosome consists of two identical **sister chromatids** joined at the **centromere**. The nucleoli disappear, and the mitotic spindle begins to form from the centrosomes, which move apart.

In **prometaphase**, the nuclear envelope breaks down and spindle microtubules invade the nuclear area. Each chromatid develops a **kinetochore** (a protein structure at the centromere) to which microtubules attach.

In **metaphase**, the centrosomes are at opposite poles of the cell, and the chromosomes align on the **metaphase plate**, an imaginary plane equidistant between the two poles, with the kinetochores of sister chromatids attached to microtubules from opposite poles.

**Anaphase** is the shortest stage of mitosis. Sister chromatids suddenly **separate**, becoming individual chromosomes, and the newly formed chromosomes are pulled towards opposite poles as kinetochore microtubules shorten while the cell elongates.

In **telophase**, two daughter nuclei begin to form as nuclear envelopes reform around the two sets of chromosomes and the chromosomes become less condensed, completing mitosis (karyokinesis).

### Cytokinesis

Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm, which typically begins during late telophase.

- **Animal cells:** A **cleavage furrow** (a pinching of the cell membrane) forms and deepens until the cell is split into two daughter cells. This is driven by a contractile ring of **actin microfilaments**.
- **Plant cells:** A **cell plate** (phragmoplast) forms due to the fusion of **Golgi-derived vesicles** containing pectin and cellulose at the equatorial plane.

### Significance of Mitosis

- Essential for the growth and multiplication of cells.
- Ensures daughter cells have the same number of chromosomes and genetic information as the parent cell.
- Crucial for wound healing, regeneration, and replacing old or damaged cells.
- Uncontrolled mitosis can lead to the formation of tumors or cancer.

## Meiosis

Meiosis is a specialized type of cell division where a single diploid ($2n$) cell divides **twice** to produce **four haploid ($n$) daughter cells**, each with half the original amount of genetic information. It is the process that produces gametes (sperm and eggs) in animals and spores in plants.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930194714.webp" alt="Stages of meiosis" caption="Figure 1.37: Stages of meiosis" />

### Meiosis I: The Reductional Division

This division separates **homologous chromosomes**, reducing the chromosome number from diploid to haploid.

**Prophase I** is a long and complex phase, unique to meiosis. Chromosomes condense and become visible, and in **synapsis**, homologous chromosomes pair up lengthwise to form a **bivalent** or **tetrad** (a structure of four chromatids). During **crossing over**, genetic material is exchanged between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes at points called **chiasmata**, creating new combinations of genes (genetic recombination).

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20250930194822.webp" alt="Crossing over" caption="Figure 1.36: Crossing over" />

In **metaphase I**, the homologous pairs (tetrads) line up at the metaphase plate, and microtubules from each pole attach to one chromosome of each homologous pair. In **anaphase I**, the **homologous chromosomes** are pulled apart and move to opposite poles; crucially, **sister chromatids remain attached** at their centromeres (unlike mitosis anaphase). In **telophase I and cytokinesis**, the chromosomes arrive at opposite poles, each pole now has a **haploid set** of chromosomes (though each chromosome still consists of two sister chromatids), and the cytoplasm divides, forming two haploid daughter cells.

### Meiosis II: The Equational Division

This division separates **sister chromatids** and is mechanistically similar to mitosis.

**Interphase II** is a brief phase where **DNA replication does NOT occur**. In **prophase II**, the spindle apparatus forms, and in **metaphase II**, the chromosomes (each still with two chromatids) align at the metaphase plate. In **anaphase II**, the centromeres divide and **sister chromatids separate**, and the separated chromatids (now individual chromosomes) move to opposite poles. Finally, in **telophase II and cytokinesis**, nuclei reform around the chromosomes at each pole and cytokinesis occurs, resulting in a total of **four genetically unique haploid daughter cells**.

### Significance of Meiosis

- Reduces the chromosome number by half, ensuring the chromosome count remains constant from one generation to the next after fertilization.
- Introduces **genetic variability** through crossing over and the independent assortment of homologous chromosomes.
- Provides the raw material for natural selection and evolution.

## Comparison: Mitosis vs. Meiosis

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |
| **Purpose** | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Production of gametes/spores for sexual reproduction |
| **Number of Divisions** | One | Two (Meiosis I and Meiosis II) |
| **Daughter Cells** | Two, diploid ($2n$), genetically identical | Four, haploid ($n$), genetically unique |
| **Synapsis & Crossing Over** | Does not occur | Occurs during Prophase I |
| **Anaphase Event** | Sister chromatids separate | Anaphase I: homologs separate; Anaphase II: sister chromatids separate |
| **Occurs in** | Somatic (body) cells | Germ line cells (gonads) |
