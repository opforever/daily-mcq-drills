# 15.1 Ecosystem

## What is an Ecosystem?

An **ecosystem** is a self-sustaining structural and functional unit of the biosphere. It consists of a **community of living organisms (biotic factors)** interacting with their **non-living physical environment (abiotic factors)**. The term was coined by **A.G. Tansley** in 1935.

Ecosystems can be as small as a pond or as large as a tropical rainforest. They represent the level of biological organization above a community.

### Ecological Hierarchy

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| Organism | Individual living being |
| Population | Group of same species in an area |
| Community | All populations in an area |
| Ecosystem | Community + abiotic environment |
| Biome | Group of similar ecosystems |
| Biosphere | All ecosystems on Earth |

---

## Components of an Ecosystem

Every ecosystem has two major components:

### 1. Abiotic Components (Non-living)
These are the physical and chemical factors of the environment:
- **Light**,  primary energy source for photosynthesis
- **Temperature**,  affects metabolic rates
- **Water**,  essential for all life processes
- **Soil**,  provides minerals and anchorage
- **Atmosphere**,  supplies $CO_2$, $O_2$, and $N_2$
- **pH and salinity**

### 2. Biotic Components (Living)
Organisms are classified by their feeding relationships:

#### Producers (Autotrophs)
- Synthesize their own food from inorganic raw materials using **light energy** (photosynthesis) or **chemical energy** (chemosynthesis).
- Examples: green plants, algae, cyanobacteria.
- Occupy the **first trophic level**.

#### Consumers (Heterotrophs)
Cannot synthesize their own food; depend on producers directly or indirectly.

| Type | Also Called | Feeds On | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary Consumer | Herbivore | Producers | Grasshopper, Rabbit |
| Secondary Consumer | Carnivore (1st order) | Primary consumers | Frog, Fox |
| Tertiary Consumer | Carnivore (2nd order) | Secondary consumers | Snake, Eagle |
| Omnivore |,  | Both plants & animals | Human, Bear |

#### Decomposers (Saprotrophs / Detritivores)
- Break down **complex organic matter** from dead organisms into **simple inorganic nutrients**.
- Return minerals to the soil and atmosphere, enabling **nutrient cycling**.
- Examples: bacteria, fungi.
- Without decomposers, nutrients would remain locked in dead organic matter and become unavailable to producers.

---

## Habitat vs. Niche

| Term | Definition | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Habitat** | The physical place where an organism lives | 'Address' |
| **Niche** | The functional role of a species in its ecosystem | 'Profession' |

- **Joseph Grinnell** (1917) first used the term niche to describe the microhabitat of a species.
- **Charles Elton** (1927) redefined niche as the **functional role** of an organism,  what it eats, what eats it, and how it interacts with other species.

No two species can occupy the **same niche** in the same ecosystem at the same time,  this is the **Competitive Exclusion Principle** (Gause's Law).

---

## Types of Ecosystems

### Natural Ecosystems
- **Terrestrial:** forests, grasslands, deserts, tundra
- **Aquatic (Freshwater):** ponds, lakes, rivers, wetlands
- **Aquatic (Marine):** oceans, coral reefs, estuaries

### Artificial Ecosystems
- Created and maintained by humans
- Examples: croplands, aquariums, gardens

---

## Key Features of a Healthy Ecosystem

1. **Energy flow**,  unidirectional, from producers through consumers
2. **Nutrient cycling**,  elements cycle between biotic and abiotic components
3. **Biodiversity**,  variety of species ensures stability
4. **Self-regulation**,  feedback mechanisms maintain balance (homeostasis)

---

# 15.2 Energy Flow

Energy flow describes how energy moves through an ecosystem from one organism to another. Unlike nutrients, energy flows in **one direction only** (unidirectional) and cannot be recycled.

---

## Source of Energy

The **primary source of energy** for almost all ecosystems is **solar radiation (sunlight)**. Energy enters the biotic (living) component of an ecosystem through **producers (autotrophs)**, which capture light energy and convert it into chemical energy via **photosynthesis**.

$$\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \text{Glucose} + \text{O}_2$$

A small number of ecosystems (e.g., deep-sea hydrothermal vents) rely on **chemosynthesis** instead of sunlight.

---

## Trophic Levels

A **trophic level** is a specific step or position in a food chain occupied by organisms that obtain energy from the same source.

| Trophic Level | Organisms | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Producers (Autotrophs) | Grass, Algae |
| 2nd | Primary Consumers (Herbivores) | Grasshopper, Rabbit |
| 3rd | Secondary Consumers (Carnivores) | Frog, Fox |
| 4th | Tertiary Consumers | Snake, Eagle |
|,  | Decomposers | Bacteria, Fungi |

**Decomposers** (saprotrophs) break down dead organic matter at every level and return nutrients to the environment.

---

## Food Chains and Food Webs

### Food Chain
A **food chain** is a **linear sequence** showing the transfer of energy from one organism to the next:

$$\text{Grass} \rightarrow \text{Grasshopper} \rightarrow \text{Frog} \rightarrow \text{Snake} \rightarrow \text{Eagle}$$

### Food Web
A **food web** is a **complex network** of multiple interconnected food chains within an ecosystem. It more accurately represents real feeding relationships because most organisms feed on more than one species.

> **Key difference:** A food chain is linear; a food web is a network of many food chains.

---

## Energy Transfer: The 10% Rule (Lindeman's Law)

When energy passes from one trophic level to the next, only approximately **10% is transferred**; the remaining **~90% is lost** as:
- Heat (during cellular respiration)
- Energy used for metabolic processes (movement, growth, reproduction)
- Undigested material passed as waste
- Undecomposed organic matter

### Example Calculation

If producers contain **10,000 J** of energy:

| Trophic Level | Energy Available |
|---|---|
| Producers | 10,000 J |
| Primary Consumers | 1,000 J (10%) |
| Secondary Consumers | 100 J (10%) |
| Tertiary Consumers | 10 J (10%) |

This explains why **food chains rarely exceed 4–5 trophic levels**,  too little energy remains to support a viable population at higher levels.

---

## Unidirectional and Non-Cyclic Energy Flow

Energy flow is:
- **Unidirectional**,  flows only from sun → producers → consumers; never in reverse.
- **Non-cyclic**,  energy lost as heat cannot be reused (Second Law of Thermodynamics).

This contrasts with **nutrients**, which cycle continuously through biotic and abiotic components (biogeochemical cycles).

---

## Ecological Pyramids

An **ecological pyramid** is a graphical representation of the quantitative relationship between trophic levels. There are three types:

### 1. Pyramid of Numbers
- Shows the **number of organisms** at each trophic level.
- Usually upright but can be **inverted** (e.g., one tree supporting many insects).

### 2. Pyramid of Biomass
- Shows the **total dry mass** (biomass) of organisms at each trophic level.
- Usually upright; can be inverted in aquatic ecosystems.
- **Biomass** = total dry mass of organic matter at a trophic level at a given time.

### 3. Pyramid of Energy
- Shows the **total energy** available at each trophic level.
- **Always upright**,  energy is always lost moving up levels; it can never increase.
- Most accurate representation of energy flow.

> The Pyramid of Energy is the only ecological pyramid that is **always upright** and can never be inverted.

---

## Productivity

- **Gross Primary Productivity (GPP):** Total rate of photosynthesis (total energy fixed by producers).
- **Net Primary Productivity (NPP):** Energy available to consumers after producers use some for their own respiration.

$$\text{NPP} = \text{GPP} - \text{Respiration}$$

---

## Summary

| Concept | Key Point |
|---|---|
| Energy source | Sunlight (solar radiation) |
| Entry point | Producers via photosynthesis |
| Energy transfer | ~10% per trophic level |
| Energy flow direction | Unidirectional, non-cyclic |
| Food chain limit | 4–5 levels due to energy loss |
| Always upright pyramid | Pyramid of Energy |

---

# 15.3 Biogeochemical Cycles

Matter, unlike energy, is **recycled** within ecosystems. The pathways through which chemical elements move between the living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) components of the environment are called **Biogeochemical Cycles** (bio = living; geo = earth/geological; chemical = elements involved).

## Key Concepts

### Reservoir Pool vs. Exchange Pool

| Feature | Reservoir Pool | Exchange Pool |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Large | Small |
| Movement | Slow | Rapid |
| Nature | Usually abiotic | Biotic + abiotic |
| Example | Atmosphere ($N_2$), Rocks (P) | Soil nutrients, plant biomass |

The **reservoir pool** stores nutrients for long periods, while the **exchange pool** is where active cycling between organisms and environment occurs.

---

## 1. The Carbon Cycle

Carbon is the backbone of all organic molecules. Its primary reservoir is the **atmosphere** (as $CO_2$) and the **oceans** (as dissolved $CO_2$ and carbonates).

**Key Processes:**
- **Photosynthesis:** Producers fix atmospheric $CO_2$ into organic compounds
  $$6CO_2 + 6H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{light}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2$$
- **Respiration:** All organisms release $CO_2$ back to the atmosphere
- **Decomposition:** Decomposers break down dead organic matter, releasing $CO_2$
- **Combustion:** Burning of fossil fuels and biomass releases stored carbon as $CO_2$
- **Sedimentation:** Carbon becomes locked in fossil fuels and limestone over geological time

> **Human Impact:** Burning fossil fuels and deforestation have increased atmospheric $CO_2$, contributing to the **greenhouse effect** and global warming.

---

## 2. The Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen ($N_2$) makes up ~78% of the atmosphere but most organisms **cannot use it directly**. It must be converted into usable forms.

### Steps of the Nitrogen Cycle

**1. Nitrogen Fixation**
Conversion of $N_2$ → $NH_3$ (ammonia) or $NO_3^-$ (nitrates)
- **Biological:** *Rhizobium* (in root nodules of legumes), *Azotobacter* (free-living)
- **Physical:** Lightning converts $N_2$ to $NO_3^-$
- **Industrial:** Haber process (fertilizer production)

**2. Ammonification**
Decomposers break down dead organic matter and excretory products → releasing $NH_4^+$ (ammonium) into soil.

**3. Nitrification**
Aerobic bacteria convert ammonium to nitrates (plant-usable form):
- *Nitrosomonas*: $NH_4^+$ → $NO_2^-$ (nitrites)
- *Nitrobacter*: $NO_2^-$ → $NO_3^-$ (nitrates)

**4. Assimilation**
Plants absorb $NO_3^-$ from soil and incorporate nitrogen into proteins, nucleic acids, and chlorophyll.

**5. Denitrification**
Anaerobic bacteria (e.g., *Pseudomonas*) convert $NO_3^-$ → $N_2$, returning nitrogen to the atmosphere.

---

## 3. The Phosphorus Cycle

Phosphorus is essential for **ATP, DNA, RNA, and cell membranes** (phospholipids).

**Key Features:**
- Primary reservoir: **Sedimentary rocks** (lithosphere)
- **No gaseous phase**,  it is a purely sedimentary cycle
- Slowest of the major nutrient cycles

**Pathway:**
1. **Weathering** of rocks releases phosphate ($PO_4^{3-}$) into soil and water
2. **Absorption** by plants and algae
3. **Consumption** by animals through food
4. **Decomposition** returns phosphate to soil
5. **Sedimentation**,  phosphate washes into oceans and becomes locked in sediment/rock

> **Note:** Because phosphorus has no atmospheric reservoir, it is often a **limiting nutrient** in ecosystems.

---

## 4. Reservoir vs. Exchange Pool,  Summary

---

## Summary Table: Comparing Major Biogeochemical Cycles

| Cycle | Primary Reservoir | Gaseous Phase? | Key Organisms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon | Atmosphere, Oceans | Yes ($CO_2$) | Plants, decomposers |
| Nitrogen | Atmosphere ($N_2$) | Yes ($N_2$) | *Rhizobium*, *Pseudomonas* |
| Phosphorus | Sedimentary rocks | **No** | Plants, decomposers |
| Water | Oceans, glaciers | Yes ($H_2O$ vapour) | All organisms |

> **Key Principle:** Unlike energy (which flows in one direction and is lost as heat), **matter is recycled**,  the same atoms have been cycling through ecosystems for billions of years.

---

# 15.4 Ecological Succession

Ecological succession is the **gradual, predictable, and directional process** by which the species composition of a community changes over time. It proceeds from a simple pioneer community through a series of intermediate stages (seral communities) to a final stable **climax community**.

---

## Key Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Sere** | The entire sequence of communities from pioneer to climax |
| **Seral Stage / Seral Community** | Each intermediate community in the sequence |
| **Pioneer Species** | First organisms to colonize a barren habitat |
| **Climax Community** | Final, stable community in equilibrium with the environment |

---

## Types of Ecological Succession

### 1. Primary Succession

Primary succession occurs in a **lifeless area** where **no soil exists** and no previous community was present, such as bare rock exposed by a retreating glacier, newly formed volcanic islands, or sand dunes. It begins with pioneer species that can tolerate extreme conditions and initiate soil formation, and because soil must first be built up from nothing, it is a very slow process that may take hundreds to thousands of years.

### 2. Secondary Succession

Secondary succession occurs where a **previous community was destroyed** but **soil remains intact**, such as on abandoned farmland, in areas after forest fires, or in logged forests. It is faster than primary succession because soil and seed banks are already present.

---

## Pioneer Species

Pioneer species are the **first colonizers** of a barren environment. They are characterized by:
- Tolerance of extreme conditions (drought, temperature extremes, nutrient-poor substrate)
- Ability to modify the environment, making it suitable for subsequent species
- In Xerosere: **crustose lichens** are the pioneers on bare rock

---

## Xerosere (Xerarch Succession)

Xerosere is succession that begins on **bare dry rock** (xeric = dry). It is the most commonly studied example of primary succession.

### Seral Stages of Xerosere:

The sequence begins with **crustose lichens** as the pioneer stage: they encrust the bare rock surface, secrete organic acids that weather the rock, and trap dust and organic matter, which initiates soil formation. As a thin soil layer develops, **foliose lichens** (leaf-like lichens) colonize and add more organic matter upon death. **Mosses** then colonize the thin soil layer, retaining moisture, adding more organic matter, and accelerating soil development. Next, **herbs and grasses** colonize, first as annuals and then as perennials, and their root systems further break down rock and deepen the soil. **Shrubs** follow, as woody shrubs outcompete herbs for light and provide shade that further modifies the microclimate. The sequence ends at the **climax forest** stage, where trees dominate (the exact species composition depending on climate) in a stable, self-perpetuating community.

---

## Hydrosere (Hydrarch Succession)

Hydrosere is succession that begins in **open freshwater** (e.g., a pond or lake). Over time, the water body fills with sediment and organic matter, eventually becoming dry land.

### Seral Stages of Hydrosere:

The sequence begins with the **phytoplankton stage**, the pioneer stage, in which microscopic algae and cyanobacteria colonize open water and begin adding organic matter to the bottom. This is followed by the **submerged aquatic plant stage**, in which rooted plants (such as *Hydrilla* and *Potamogeton*) grow on the bottom and accumulate sediment and organic debris. In the **floating plant stage**, plants with floating leaves (such as water lilies, *Nymphaea*) colonize and reduce light penetration, which suppresses the submerged plants. The **reed swamp stage** follows, in which emergent plants (reeds, rushes, *Phragmites*) colonize the shallow margins and rapidly fill in the pond with organic matter. As the water level drops further, the **marsh meadow stage** sees sedges and grasses replace the reeds, and the soil becomes waterlogged but not permanently flooded. Finally, the **woodland / climax forest stage** is reached, where trees and shrubs colonize the now-dry land and a climax community is established.

---

## Climax Community

The climax community is the **final, stable stage** of succession. It is marked by high species diversity and complex food webs, and by high niche specialization, meaning species occupy very specific roles. It maintains a dynamic equilibrium, where species composition remains relatively constant, and shows lower net productivity compared to early successional stages because most energy goes to respiration. It is also self-perpetuating: it reproduces itself unless disturbed by fire, flood, human activity, and similar events.

The type of climax community is determined primarily by **climate** (temperature and rainfall). A tropical rainforest climax forms in humid tropics, a temperate deciduous forest in temperate regions, and a grassland climax in semi-arid regions.

---

## Summary Comparison

| Feature | Primary Succession | Secondary Succession |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Bare rock / no soil | Disturbed area with soil |
| Pioneer species | Lichens (Xerosere) / Phytoplankton (Hydrosere) | Weeds, grasses |
| Speed | Very slow | Relatively fast |
| Example | Volcanic island, retreating glacier | Post-fire forest, abandoned farmland |


---

# 15.5 Population Dynamics

Population dynamics is the branch of ecology that studies **how and why populations change in size and composition over time**. It examines the interplay of birth, death, immigration, and emigration.

---

## 1. The Basic Demographic Equation

The change in population size ($\Delta N$) over a time period is given by:

$$\Delta N = (B + I) - (D + E)$$

Where:
- $B$ = Births (Natality)
- $I$ = Immigration (individuals entering)
- $D$ = Deaths (Mortality)
- $E$ = Emigration (individuals leaving)

If $(B + I) > (D + E)$, the population grows. If $(B + I) < (D + E)$, it declines.

---

## 2. Population Growth Models

### 2.1 Exponential Growth (J-shaped Curve)

Under **ideal conditions** with unlimited resources, populations grow exponentially:

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = rN$$

- $N$ = current population size
- $r$ = intrinsic rate of natural increase ($r = b - d$)
- $t$ = time

This produces a **J-shaped curve**. The growth rate accelerates as $N$ increases. Examples: bacterial cultures, introduced species with no predators.

### 2.2 Logistic Growth (S-shaped Curve)

In reality, resources are limited. As population density increases, **environmental resistance** slows growth. The logistic growth equation is:

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\frac{(K - N)}{K}$$

- $K$ = Carrying Capacity (maximum sustainable population size)
- The term $\frac{(K-N)}{K}$ represents the fraction of unused resources

This produces an **S-shaped (sigmoidal) curve**:
- When $N$ is small: growth is nearly exponential
- When $N \approx K/2$: growth rate is maximum
- When $N \approx K$: growth rate approaches **zero** (population stabilizes)

---

## 3. Carrying Capacity ($K$)

**Carrying Capacity ($K$)** is the maximum number of individuals of a species that a given environment can sustainably support, given available food, water, space, and other resources.

- At $N = K$: $\frac{dN}{dt} = 0$ (no net growth)
- Populations fluctuate around $K$ in nature
- Human activities can reduce $K$ by degrading habitats

---

## 4. Limiting Factors

Factors that restrict population growth are called **limiting factors**. They are classified into two types:

### 4.1 Density-Dependent Factors

These are **biotic factors** whose effect **intensifies as population density increases**. They act as negative feedback mechanisms:

| Factor | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Intraspecific competition | Increased competition for food/space at high density |
| Predation | Predator populations increase when prey is abundant |
| Disease & Parasitism | Pathogens spread more easily in dense populations |
| Stress & Aggression | Overcrowding increases stress hormones, reducing reproduction |

### 4.2 Density-Independent Factors

These are **abiotic factors** that affect population size **regardless of density**:

- Natural disasters: floods, fires, earthquakes, volcanic eruptions
- Temperature extremes (frost, heat waves)
- Drought and seasonal changes
- Pollution

A sudden frost kills the same proportion of a population whether it has 10 or 10,000 individuals.

---

## 5. r-Selection vs. K-Selection

Species have evolved different reproductive strategies based on their ecological niche:

| Feature | r-Selected Species | K-Selected Species |
|---|---|---|
| Growth rate | High $r$ | Low $r$ |
| Body size | Small | Large |
| Offspring number | Many | Few |
| Parental care | Little/none | Extensive |
| Lifespan | Short | Long |
| Population size | Variable, often below $K$ | Stable, near $K$ |
| Environment | Unstable, unpredictable | Stable, predictable |
| Examples | Bacteria, insects, annual plants | Elephants, whales, humans |

**r-strategists** maximize reproductive rate to exploit temporary resources. **K-strategists** invest in quality offspring and compete effectively in stable, resource-limited environments.

---

## 6. Population Regulation Summary

- **Exponential growth** is theoretical,  it cannot continue indefinitely.
- **Logistic growth** is more realistic,  populations are regulated by carrying capacity.
- **Density-dependent factors** provide negative feedback that stabilizes populations near $K$.
- **Density-independent factors** can cause sudden, unpredictable population crashes.
- The balance between $r$ (biotic potential) and environmental resistance determines actual population size.

---

# 15.6 Coral Reefs

A **coral reef** is a diverse underwater ecosystem built and maintained by colonies of tiny marine animals called **coral polyps**. Coral reefs are among the most productive and biodiverse ecosystems on Earth, often referred to as the **'Rainforests of the Sea'**.

---

## Coral Polyps,  The Builders

Coral polyps are small, soft-bodied organisms belonging to **Class Anthozoa**, **Phylum Cnidaria**. Key features include:

- **Body plan:** Cylindrical polyp with a central mouth surrounded by tentacles
- **Cnidocytes:** Specialized cells containing **nematocysts** (stinging organelles) used for capturing prey
- **Exoskeleton:** Each polyp secretes a hard cup of **Calcium Carbonate ($CaCO_3$)** around itself
- **Colonial life:** Thousands of polyps live together in colonies; when they die, their $CaCO_3$ skeletons remain and new polyps build on top, gradually forming massive reef structures

> **Note:** Unlike other cnidarians (e.g., jellyfish), Anthozoa exist **only as polyps**,  there is no medusa stage.

---

## Zooxanthellae,  The Essential Symbiont

Coral reefs depend critically on a **mutualistic symbiosis** between coral polyps and photosynthetic algae called **Zooxanthellae** (*Symbiodinium* spp.), which are dinoflagellates living within coral tissues.

| Partner | Benefit Received |
|---|---|
| Coral polyp | Up to **90% of energy needs** supplied by algal photosynthesis; also receives $O_2$ |
| Zooxanthellae | Shelter inside coral tissue; $CO_2$ and nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus) from coral metabolism |

Zooxanthellae also give corals their characteristic **brown/golden colour**.

### Coral Bleaching
When corals are stressed (e.g., by elevated water temperature, pollution, or ocean acidification), they **expel their zooxanthellae**. Without the algae, corals turn white,  a phenomenon called **coral bleaching**. Prolonged bleaching leads to coral death.

---

## Types of Coral Reefs

There are three main types of coral reefs, classified by their structure and relationship to land:

### 1. Fringing Reefs
- Grow **directly attached to** or very close to a shoreline
- No significant lagoon separates them from land
- Most common type of reef
- *Example:* Reefs along the Red Sea coast

### 2. Barrier Reefs
- Separated from the mainland by a **wide, deep lagoon**
- Run parallel to the coastline
- *Example:* **Great Barrier Reef**, Australia (largest coral reef system in the world)

### 3. Atolls
- **Ring-shaped** reefs enclosing a shallow central lagoon
- Form when a **volcanic island gradually subsides** (sinks) into the ocean, leaving only the surrounding coral reef
- *Example:* Maldives, Marshall Islands

---

## Ecological Importance of Coral Reefs

Despite covering **less than 0.1%** of the ocean floor, coral reefs:

- Support approximately **25% of all known marine species** (fish, invertebrates, algae, etc.)
- Provide **coastal protection** by absorbing wave energy and reducing erosion
- Participate in the **carbon and nitrogen cycles**
- Serve as **nursery grounds** for many commercially important fish species
- Have significant **economic value** through fisheries, tourism, and pharmaceutical research

---

## Threats to Coral Reefs

| Threat | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Climate change** (rising sea temperatures) | Coral bleaching and death |
| **Ocean acidification** (increased $CO_2$) | Dissolves $CaCO_3$ skeletons; inhibits reef building |
| **Pollution** (agricultural runoff, sewage) | Algal overgrowth smothers corals |
| **Overfishing** | Disrupts ecological balance |
| **Physical damage** (destructive fishing, tourism) | Direct destruction of reef structure |

---

## Summary

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Builders | Coral polyps (Class Anthozoa, Phylum Cnidaria) |
| Skeleton material | Calcium Carbonate ($CaCO_3$) |
| Key symbiont | Zooxanthellae (dinoflagellate algae) |
| Types | Fringing, Barrier, Atoll |
| Biodiversity | ~25% of marine species in <0.1% of ocean area |
| Nickname | 'Rainforests of the Sea' |

---

# 15.7 Ecosystems of Pakistan

Pakistan is a country of remarkable ecological diversity. Its ecosystems range from coastal mangroves at sea level to alpine meadows above 4,000 m, shaped by dramatic variations in altitude, latitude, temperature, and precipitation.

## Factors Determining Ecosystem Diversity

The primary factors that create Pakistan's diverse ecosystems are:
- **Altitude**: Ranges from sea level (Arabian Sea coast) to 8,611 m (K2)
- **Latitude**: From tropical south to temperate north
- **Precipitation**: From hyper-arid deserts (<100 mm/year) to monsoon-fed forests (>1,500 mm/year)
- **Temperature**: From extreme heat in Sindh to sub-zero winters in Gilgit-Baltistan

---

## Major Ecosystems of Pakistan

### 1. Alpine Meadows

Alpine meadows occur above the tree line in Gilgit-Baltistan and KPK, above roughly 3,500 m. The vegetation is low-growing perennial grasses, sedges, and wildflowers such as *Primula* and *Saxifraga*, and these plants have short growing seasons and are adapted to extreme cold and UV radiation. The fauna includes the Snow Leopard (*Panthera uncia*), Marco Polo Sheep, Ibex, and Golden Eagle.

### 2. Temperate Coniferous Forests

Temperate coniferous forests occur in northern Pakistan, in Kaghan, Swat, Murree, and Chitral, between 1,500 and 3,500 m. The forests are dominated by evergreen trees with needle-like leaves, namely the Deodar Cedar (*Cedrus deodara*, Pakistan's national tree), the Blue Pine (*Pinus wallichiana*), and the Silver Fir (*Abies pindrow*). Their conical shape and needle leaves shed snow, and a waxy cuticle reduces water loss. The fauna includes Musk Deer, Black Bear, and Pheasants.

### 3. Sub-tropical Scrub Forests

Sub-tropical scrub forests occur in the foothills of Punjab and KPK, on the Pothohar Plateau and Salt Range. The vegetation includes Phulai (*Acacia modesta*), Olive (*Olea cuspidata*), and Sanatha. These forests are characterized by drought-resistant shrubs and small trees with an open canopy.

### 4. Tropical Thorn Forests

Tropical thorn forests occur on the plains of Punjab and Sindh. The vegetation consists of thorny trees and shrubs such as *Acacia nilotica* (Kikar) and *Prosopis cineraria* (Jand), adapted to hot, dry conditions with sparse rainfall.

### 5. Indus River Plain (Riverine Ecosystem)

The Indus River Plain runs along the Indus River and its tributaries across Punjab and Sindh. It has alluvial, fertile soil deposited by river flooding, and historically supported tropical thorn forests. It is now dominated by irrigation-based agriculture (wheat, rice, cotton). The fauna includes the Indus River Dolphin (*Platanista gangetica minor*), diverse waterfowl, and the Mugger Crocodile.

### 6. Desert Ecosystems

Pakistan has four major desert ecosystems:

| Desert | Location | Type |
|--------|----------|------|
| **Thar Desert** | Eastern Sindh | Hot, sandy desert |
| **Cholistan Desert** | Southern Punjab | Hot, sandy desert |
| **Thal Desert** | Between Jhelum & Indus, Punjab | Hot, sandy desert |
| **Kharan Desert** | Balochistan | Cold/sandy desert (high altitude) |

The vegetation is made up of drought-tolerant plants such as *Calligonum*, *Haloxylon* (Saxaul), and *Tamarix*. The fauna includes the Chinkara Gazelle, Desert Fox, Sand Grouse, and various reptiles, which show adaptations such as nocturnal behavior, water conservation, and deep root systems (in the plants).

### 7. Mangrove Ecosystem

The mangrove ecosystem is found in the Indus Delta (Sindh) and along the Makran Coast (Balochistan), with *Avicennia marina* (Grey Mangrove) as the dominant species. These are salt-tolerant (halophytic) trees growing in tidal zones, with prop roots (pneumatophores) for gas exchange in waterlogged soil, and Pakistan has one of the world's largest mangrove forests. Ecologically, mangroves serve as nursery and breeding grounds for fish, shrimp, and crabs, protect the coastline from erosion and storms, and contribute to carbon sequestration. The fauna includes Flamingos, Herons, Marine turtles, and Dolphins.

### 8. Wetlands and Lakes

Examples include Keenjhar Lake, Haleji Lake, and Manchhar Lake in Sindh, and the Uchhali Complex in Punjab. These wetlands are critical stopover points for migratory birds on the Central Asian Flyway, and their fauna includes the Siberian Crane, Flamingo, and various ducks and waders.
