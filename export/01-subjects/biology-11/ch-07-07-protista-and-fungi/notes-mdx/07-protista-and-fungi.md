# PROTISTS - The Evolutionary Relationship

## PROTISTS - The Evolutionary Relationship

This section outlines the defining characteristics, evolutionary origins, and remarkable diversity of the organisms grouped within the kingdom Protista.

## General Characteristics

Protists are a diverse group of eukaryotic organisms. Unlike prokaryotes (bacteria), their cells contain a true nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles (e.g., mitochondria, plastids). They are often considered a "catch-all" kingdom for any eukaryote that is not a plant, animal, or fungus.

<InlineNoteTag label="Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells" notePath="biology-11/9-prokaryotic-and-eukaryotic-cells" />

## Polyphyletic Origin

The term **polyphyletic** describes a group of organisms that do not share a single common ancestor. Protists evolved from multiple, independent ancestral lines. This means the kingdom Protista is a grouping of convenience rather than a reflection of a close evolutionary relationship. If classified by specific shared traits, they could form as many as **twenty distinct kingdoms**.

According to the **Endosymbiotic Theory**, the first eukaryotic cells arose approximately **1.5 billion years ago** when one prokaryote engulfed another, with the engulfed prokaryote eventually becoming organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts.

## Evolutionary Links to Other Kingdoms

Despite their diversity, specific protist groups are believed to be the direct ancestors of the three multicellular kingdoms:

### Protists → Kingdom Plantae
**Green algae (Chlorophyta)** are the direct ancestors of land plants. Evidence includes:
- Both possess **chlorophyll $a$ and $b$**
- Both have **cellulose** cell walls
- Both store carbohydrates as **starch**

### Protists → Kingdom Animalia
**Choanoflagellates** share common ancestry with animals. These sessile protists are strikingly similar to the **collar cells (choanocytes)** found in sponges (Phylum Porifera), providing strong morphological evidence for the protist-animal link.

### Protists → Kingdom Fungi
**Slime molds** (or flagellated protist ancestors) are believed to be ancestral to Fungi. The transition involved loss of motility and adoption of an **absorptive heterotrophic** lifestyle ,  secreting digestive enzymes externally and absorbing the products.

## The Remarkable Diversity of Protists

### Size and Structure

Protists exhibit a vast range in size, from microscopic single-celled organisms (protozoa) to massive multicellular forms like giant kelp (up to 60 meters). Cellular organization varies:

- **Unicellular:** Most are single-celled.
- **Colonial:** Some form groups of cells.
- **Coenocytic:** Some have multiple nuclei within a single large cell.

Even multicellular protists have simple body plans and lack the specialized tissues found in plants, animals, and fungi.

### Nutrition

Protists display all major types of eukaryotic nutrition.

| Nutritional Mode | Description | Example | Analogy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Photosynthetic (Autotrophic) | Produce their own food using chlorophyll and sunlight | Algae | Plant-like |
| Absorptive (Heterotrophic) | Absorb nutrients from their environment | Water molds | Fungus-like |
| Ingestive (Heterotrophic) | Engulf and consume food particles or other organisms | Protozoa, Slime molds | Animal-like |

### Habitat and Mode of Life

**Habitat:** They are highly adaptable, found in aquatic environments (oceans, freshwater as plankton), damp soil, leaf litter, and even extreme environments like snow and hot springs (extremophiles).

**Mode of Life:**

- **Free-living:** Most live independently.
- **Symbiotic:** Some form close relationships with other organisms.
  - **Mutualism:** Both organisms benefit.
  - **Parasitism:** One organism (the protist) benefits at the expense of the host.

### Reproduction

- **Asexual:** Reproduction without mating.
- **Sexual:** Involves both **meiosis** (cell division to produce gametes) and **syngamy** (the fusion of gametes).

<InlineNoteTag label="Cell Division" notePath="biology-11/12-cell-division" />

A key distinction from animals and plants is that protists typically **lack multicellular sex organs** and do **not form embryos**.

### Locomotion

Most protists are motile at some point in their life cycle.

- **Amoeboid motion:** Extending cellular protrusions called **pseudopods**.
- **Cilia:** Using short, hair-like structures that beat in coordinated waves.
- **Flagella:** Propelling themselves with long, whip-like structures.

Some species may use a combination of these methods.

# 7.2 Major Groups of Protists

This section outlines the major groups within the Kingdom Protista, a diverse group of eukaryotic organisms. They are broadly categorized into animal-like (Protozoa), plant-like (Algae), and fungi-like (Myxomycota and Oomycota) protists based on their characteristics.

## **7.2.1 Protozoa: The Animal-Like Protists**

Protozoa are a polyphyletic group of microscopic, single-celled organisms that exhibit animal-like behaviors such as movement and heterotrophic nutrition. They are found in a wide range of aquatic (freshwater, marine) and terrestrial environments (soil, decaying organic matter), and many are parasitic: freshwater examples include *Amoeba* and *Paramecium*, marine examples include Radiolaria, and parasitic examples include *Plasmodium* and *Entamoeba histolytica*.

Structurally, a protozoan is unicellular, consisting of a single mass of cytoplasm containing all necessary organelles. Some, like foraminifera, possess a protective outer shell. Vacuoles are specialized membrane-bound sacs that carry out much of the cell's housekeeping: *food vacuoles* digest ingested food particles using enzymes, *contractile vacuoles* regulate water balance (osmoregulation) by expelling excess water, especially in freshwater species, and *storage vacuoles* store nutrients or waste products. Reproduction occurs through both *asexual* and *sexual* methods.

Locomotion is classified by the method of movement used. Pseudopodia ("false feet") are cytoplasmic extensions used for movement and feeding, as in *Amoeba*. Flagella are long, whip-like tails, as in *Trypanosoma*. Cilia are short, hair-like structures that beat in unison, as in *Paramecium*. Parasitic forms, such as *Plasmodium*, often lack motility structures altogether. To survive harsh, unfavorable environmental conditions, protozoa can form resistant *cysts*.

### **Subgroups of Protozoa**

Amoebas are free-living or parasitic protozoa that use *pseudopodia* for movement and feeding, such as the free-living *Amoeba proteus* and the parasitic *Entamoeba histolytica*, which causes amoebic dysentery.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002025533.webp" alt="Amoeba proteus" />
<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002025550.webp" alt="Entamoeba histolytica" caption="Fig 7.1(a) Amoeba proteus, (b) Entamoeba histolytica" />

Zooflagellates are protozoa that move using one or more *flagella*, such as *Giardia intestinalis*, a parasite causing giardiasis.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002025625.webp" alt="Giardia" caption="Fig 7.2 (a) Giardia" />

Choanoflagellates are sessile (stalk-anchored) marine or freshwater flagellates, characterized by a single flagellum surrounded by a collar of microvilli, resembling the collar cells of sponges.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002025717.webp" alt="Colonial Choanoflagellate" caption="Fig 7.2 (b) A colonial Choanoflagellate" />

Ciliates are protozoa covered in numerous short, hair-like *cilia* used for movement and feeding, such as *Paramecium*, *Stentor*, and *Vorticella*.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002025807.webp" alt="Ciliates" caption="Fig 7.3 Ciliates" />

Foraminifera and Actinopods are marine protozoa that produce protective shells called *tests*: foraminifera shells are made of *calcium carbonate*, while actinopod shells are made of *silica*.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002025836.webp" alt="Foraminifera" />
<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002025859.webp" alt="Actinopods" caption="Fig 7.4: a,Foraminifera b, Actinopods" />

Apicomplexans are a large group of parasitic protozoa with complex life cycles, named for the specialized structures at their apex used to invade host cells. *Plasmodium*, the parasite that causes malaria, is a key example.

## **7.2.2 Algae: The Plant-Like Protists**

Algae are a diverse group of photosynthetic organisms responsible for 50-60% of global photosynthesis. They can be unicellular, colonial, filamentous, or complex multicellular, and some filamentous algae are *coenocytic*, meaning they have a multinucleated structure lacking cross-walls between cells. The body of a multicellular alga, called a *thallus*, is undifferentiated and lacks true roots, stems, or leaves, and the cell wall often contains cellulose. Algae possess chlorophyll 'a' and various accessory pigments like carotenoids, xanthophyll, and phycoerythrin, and they exhibit diverse life cycles, most having a motile, flagellated stage.

| Feature | Algae | Higher Plants |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sex Organs** | Unicellular | Multicellular |
| **Zygote Protection**| Zygote is not protected by the parent organism | Zygote develops into an embryo protected by parent tissue |
| **Embryo Formation**| Do not form embryos | Form embryos |

### **Subgroups of Algae**

Euglenoids are freshwater organisms; about one-third have chloroplasts and are photosynthetic, while the others are heterotrophic (ingest/absorb food). *Euglena* is a typical example.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002025943.webp" alt="Euglena" caption="Figure 7.5 Euglena" />

Dinoflagellates are mostly unicellular, with two flagella. Cellulose plates impregnated with silica form a protective armor, and some are *bioluminescent*, while others cause "red tides" (harmful algal blooms).

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030003.webp" alt="Dinoflagellates" caption="Fig: 7.6 Dinoflagellates" />

Diatoms are numerous unicellular algae found in marine and freshwater environments, characterized by a two-part silica cell wall (frustule) that fits together like a box.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030024.webp" alt="Diatoms" caption="Fig 7.7 Diatoms" />

Brown algae (Phaeophyta) are multicellular and found in cooler marine waters, ranging from small filaments to giant kelps up to 75 meters long, such as *Laminaria* (kelp).

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030057.webp" alt="Brown Algae Laminaria" caption="Fig 7.8 Brown Algae Laminaria" />

Red algae (Rhodophyta) are multicellular and found primarily in warm seawater. They are anchored to surfaces by a *holdfast* and lack flagellated cells in their life cycle.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030138.webp" alt="Red Algae" caption="Fig 7.9 Red Algae" />

Green algae (Chlorophyta) are believed to be ancestors of land plants due to shared characteristics such as a cellulose cell wall and chlorophylls a and b. They exhibit great diversity in form (unicellular, colonial, filamentous, multicellular), with examples including *Chlamydomonas*, *Spirogyra*, *Volvox*, and *Ulva*.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030232.webp" alt="Green Algae" caption="Fig 7.10 Green Algae" />

## **7.2.4 Fungi-Like Protists**

These protists are heterotrophs that absorb nutrients from dead or decaying organic matter (saprotrophs) or from living hosts (parasites).

### **Myxomycota: Slime Molds**

The vegetative stage is a **plasmodium**: a diploid, multinucleated mass of cytoplasm enveloped in a slime sheath. Its life cycle runs as follows. Under unfavorable conditions (e.g., drought), the plasmodium differentiates to form stalked reproductive structures called **sporangia**. Within the sporangia, meiosis occurs, producing haploid **spores**. Spores are released and germinate into haploid flagellated or amoeboid cells. Two of these cells fuse to form a diploid zygote. The zygote grows and its nucleus divides repeatedly without cytokinesis to form a new multinucleated plasmodium.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030343.webp" alt="Life cycle of Plasmodial slime mold" caption="Fig: 7.11 Life cycle of Plasmodial slime mold (Physarum)" />

### **Oomycota: Water Molds**

Oomycota includes water molds, white rusts, and downy mildews, living as parasites or saprotrophs in aquatic or moist terrestrial environments. The key difference from true fungi is that their cell walls are made of **cellulose**, not chitin. They are composed of filamentous structures called **hyphae**, which are *coenocytic* (multinucleated and lacking septa/cross-walls). Reproduction is both asexual, producing motile, flagellated **zoospores** inside a sporangium, and sexual, involving two types of gametangia: the female **oogonium** (contains eggs) and the male **antheridium** (contains male nuclei). Fertilization leads to the formation of a thick-walled, resistant zygote called an **oospore**. *Phytophthora infestans*, the causative agent of late blight in potatoes and the Irish Potato Famine, is a well-known example.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030428.webp" alt="Life cycle of Phytophthora infestans" caption="Fig: 7.12 Life cycle of Phytophthora infestans" />

## **Possible Questions/Answers**

-   **Q:** What are the three main types of vacuoles in protozoa and their functions?
    **A:**
    1.  **Food Vacuole:** Digests food particles.
    2.  **Contractile Vacuole:** Regulates water balance by expelling excess water.
    3.  **Storage Vacuole:** Stores nutrients or waste products.

-   **Q:** How do algae differ from true plants?
    **A:** Algae typically have unicellular sex organs, do not protect their zygotes within the parent organism, and do not form embryos, unlike true plants.

-   **Q:** What is a key difference between the cell walls of Oomycota (water molds) and true fungi?
    **A:** The cell walls of Oomycota are made primarily of cellulose, whereas the cell walls of true fungi are made of chitin.

-   **Q:** What is a plasmodium in the context of slime molds?
    **A:** It is the main vegetative stage, consisting of a diploid, multinucleated mass of cytoplasm that moves and feeds like an amoeba.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Algae (various)** | Oxygen production, base of aquatic food webs, food source (kelp), industrial products (alginates, agar), biofuel potential. | Harmful algal blooms (red tides), neurotoxin production (PSP). |

# General Characteristics of Fungi

# General Characteristics of Fungi

Fungi are a diverse kingdom of eukaryotic organisms that are not differentiated into roots, stems, and leaves. They are essential to ecosystems as decomposers, symbionts, and pathogens. The study of fungi is known as **mycology**.

## Eukaryotic Nature
Fungi are eukaryotic, meaning their cells contain a true nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles.

<InlineNoteTag label="Eukaryotic Cells" notePath="biology-11/9-prokaryotic-and-eukaryotic-cells" />

## Heterotrophic Nutrition
They lack chlorophyll and cannot perform photosynthesis. Instead, they obtain nutrients through external digestion (extracellular digestion), secreting enzymes to break down organic matter and then absorbing the simpler molecules. Because of this, they are often referred to as **absorptive heterotrophs**.

## Unique Cell Structure
Fungi possess a rigid cell wall made primarily of **chitin**, a nitrogen-containing polysaccharide, which distinguishes them from plants (cellulose) and animals (no cell wall). They store carbohydrates as **glycogen**, similar to animals.

<InlineNoteTag label="Cell Wall" notePath="biology-11/5-cell-wall-and-plasma-membrane" />

## Body Plan (Thallus)
The main body of most fungi is a multicellular network of filaments called a **mycelium**. This network is composed of individual threads called **hyphae**, which maximize the surface area for nutrient absorption.

### Hyphae Types
- **Septate**: Divided by cross-walls called *septa*. Example: *Penicillium*.
- **Nonseptate (Aseptate)**: Lack cross-walls, resulting in a continuous cytoplasmic mass with many nuclei. This form is also called **coenocytic hyphae**. Example: *Rhizopus*.

## Habitat and Mode of Life
Fungi are found in a wide variety of habitats, including terrestrial (forests, grasslands) and aquatic (lakes, rivers) environments. Their modes of life are diverse:

*   **Saprophytic**: Act as decomposers, breaking down dead organic matter.
*   **Mutualistic**: Form symbiotic relationships where both organisms benefit (e.g., **Mycorrhizae**, a partnership between fungi and plant roots).
*   **Parasitic**: Live on or in other organisms, causing harm (e.g., fungal diseases in humans and crops).
*   **Predatory**: Actively trap and consume other microorganisms.

## Size and Locomotion
*   **Size**: Varies from microscopic single-celled organisms like *yeasts* (3–10 micrometers) to massive organisms like *Armillaria ostoyae* (honey mushroom), which is considered the largest living organism on Earth.
*   **Locomotion**: Fungi are **non-motile** and lack flagella at any stage of their life cycle. They grow towards food sources.

## Reproduction
Fungi exhibit both asexual and sexual reproduction to alternate between stages.

### Asexual Reproduction
*   **Budding**: A small outgrowth or "bud" forms on the parent cell and detaches. Common in yeasts.
*   **Fragmentation**: The mycelium breaks into fragments, each capable of growing into a new individual.
*   **Mitotic Spores**: Spores are produced through mitosis and can develop into new, genetically identical individuals.

### Sexual Reproduction
Involves the fusion of specialized sexual cells from different mating types, leading to genetic recombination and diversity.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030514.webp" alt="Generalised life cycle for Fungi" caption="Figure 7.14: A generalised life cycle for Fungi" />

## Why Fungi are a Separate Kingdom
Fungi were once classified as plants but are now in their own kingdom because they share characteristics with both plants and animals, while also having unique features.

| Feature Comparison | Fungi | Plants | Animals |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nutrition** | Heterotrophic (absorptive) | Autotrophic (photosynthesis) | Heterotrophic (ingestive) |
| **Cell Wall** | Present, made of **chitin** | Present, made of **cellulose** | Absent |
| **Carbohydrate Storage** | **Glycogen** | Starch | Glycogen |
| **Chlorophyll** | Absent | Present | Absent |
| **Motility** | Non-motile | Generally non-motile | Generally motile |
| **Body Structure** | Thallus (mycelium/hyphae) | Roots, stems, leaves | Complex organ systems |
| **Cell Division** | **Nuclear Mitosis** | Standard Mitosis | Standard Mitosis |

### Nuclear Mitosis
A unique form of cell division in fungi where the mitotic spindle forms *inside* the nucleus, and the nuclear envelope does not break down until late in the process.

<InlineNoteTag label="Cell Division" notePath="biology-11/12-cell-division" />

## Biological Significance
Fungi are vital to ecosystems as primary decomposers, recycling nutrients back into the soil. They form critical symbiotic relationships (mycorrhizae) with plants, aid in food production (yeast in bread, cheese molds), and are sources of medicines (e.g., penicillin), but can also be significant pathogens.

# Diversity Among Fungi

Fungi are a diverse kingdom of over 100,000 known terrestrial species. Their ancestry dates back approximately 570 million years, and they are thought to have evolved from red algae, as both groups lack flagella at any stage of their life cycles.

## Classification of Fungi

Fungi are classified into four main groups, primarily based on their methods of reproduction and the types of sexual reproductive structures they form. These groups also differ in their hyphal structure.

| Feature | Zygomycota (Conjugating Fungi) | Ascomycota (Sac Fungi) | Basidiomycota (Club Fungi) | Deuteromycota (Imperfect Fungi) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Common Name** | Conjugating Fungi | Sac Fungi | Club Fungi | Imperfect Fungi |
| **Hyphae** | Non-septate (coenocytic), well-developed mycelium | Septate with perforations allowing cytoplasmic flow | Septate with perforations | Septate (similar to Ascomycota) |
| **Asexual Reproduction** | Spores (conidia) produced in a sporangium | Conidia produced on modified hyphae called conidiophores; budding in yeasts | Occasionally by conidiospores | Conidia produced on conidiophores |
| **Sexual Reproduction** | Conjugation between (+) and (−) strains to form a zygospore | Ascospores (typically 8) formed inside a sac-like structure called an ascus, within a fruiting body (ascocarp) | Basidiospores (typically 4) formed on a club-shaped structure called a basidium, on a fruiting body (basidiocarp) | Sexual stage is absent or unknown |
| **Key Feature** | Formation of a thick-walled, resistant zygospore | Sac-like ascus containing ascospores | Club-shaped basidium bearing basidiospores | No known sexual life cycle; genetic recombination via parasexuality |
| **Examples** | *Rhizopus nigricans* (Black bread mold) | Yeasts, morels, truffles, *Neurospora* | Mushrooms (*Agaricus*), rusts, smuts, puffballs | *Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*, *Alternaria* |

## Zygomycota (Conjugating Fungi)

Zygomycota are found in soil and on decaying organic matter, acting as saprotrophs on bakery goods, fruits, and similar substrates; they are important decomposers, though some are pathogenic. Their hyphae are non-septate (coenocytic), forming a well-developed, branching mycelium. Asexually, spores are produced in a structure called a **sporangium**, as in the example *Rhizopus nigricans*.

Sexual reproduction occurs via conjugation between two different mating strains (+ and −). Hyphae of opposite strains meet and hormones cause the tips to form **gametangia**. Fusion of haploid nuclei (**karyogamy**) then forms a diploid zygote, which develops into a thick-walled, resistant **zygospore**. Under favorable conditions, the zygospore germinates, undergoes meiosis, and produces a sporangium that releases haploid spores.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030544.webp" alt="Reproduction in Rhizopus" caption="Figure 7.15: Reproduction in Rhizopus" />

## Ascomycota (Sac Fungi)

Ascomycota are a large, diverse group found in various ecosystems, including economically important fungi like morels, truffles, and yeast. Their hyphae are typically septate, but with pores that allow cytoplasm to move between compartments. Asexually, they reproduce either by **conidia**, spores that form on the tips of modified hyphae called **conidiophores** (the color of conidia gives many molds their characteristic tint of blue, green, or pink), or by **budding** in unicellular yeasts, where a small bud separates from the parent cell.

Sexual reproduction begins when two hyphae fuse their cytoplasm (**plasmogamy**), but not their nuclei, forming a **dikaryotic** ($n+n$) hypha. These hyphae form a fruiting body called an **ascocarp** (e.g., cup-shaped, flask-shaped), inside which specialized sac-like structures called **asci** (singular: ascus) develop. Within each ascus, the two nuclei fuse (**karyogamy**) to form a diploid zygote ($2n$), which undergoes meiosis (forming 4 haploid nuclei) and then mitosis (forming 8 haploid nuclei total). Each nucleus develops into an **ascospore**, and the mature ascus bursts, releasing the 8 ascospores.

## Basidiomycota (Club Fungi)

Basidiomycota include mushrooms, puffballs, shelf fungi, rusts, and smuts, and can be decomposers or form mycorrhizal relationships with plants. Their hyphae are septate with perforations, and the mycelium often grows extensively underground. Asexual reproduction occurs only occasionally, via conidiospores.

Sexual reproduction begins when a primary mycelium (monokaryotic, $n$) fuses with another of a different mating type (**plasmogamy**), forming a **secondary mycelium** that is dikaryotic ($n+n$). This secondary mycelium grows into a compact mass (button) which develops into the fruiting body, the **basidiocarp** (e.g., a mushroom), consisting of a stalk and a cap. Under the cap are **gills**, on which club-shaped structures called **basidia** (singular: basidium) form. Within each basidium, the two haploid nuclei fuse (**karyogamy**) to form a diploid zygote ($2n$), which undergoes meiosis to produce four haploid nuclei that develop into **basidiospores** on the tip of the basidium. Basidiospores are released and can germinate into new primary mycelia.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030652.webp" alt="Life of a Mushroom" caption="Figure 7.17: Life of a Mushroom" />

## Deuteromycota (Imperfect Fungi)

- **Defining Feature:** These fungi are called "imperfect" because they have no known sexual stage in their life cycle.
- **Reproduction:** They reproduce asexually by forming **conidiospores** on conidiophores.
- **Lifestyle:** Many are saprophytic or parasitic on plants.
- **Genetic Recombination:** Despite the lack of sexual reproduction, they can achieve genetic diversity through **parasexuality** ,  the exchange of chromosomal portions between two nuclei within the same hypha.
- **Examples:** *Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*, *Alternaria*, *Fusarium*.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030722.webp" alt="Penicillium" caption="Figure 7.18: Penicillium" />

## Key Terminology

| Term | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Plasmogamy** | Fusion of the cytoplasm of two haploid fungal cells |
| **Karyogamy** | Fusion of two haploid nuclei to form a diploid ($2n$) nucleus |
| **Dikaryotic ($n+n$)** | State where a cell contains two genetically distinct haploid nuclei |
| **Parasexuality** | Genetic recombination in Deuteromycota without a true sexual cycle |
| **Ascus** | Sac-like structure in Ascomycota containing 8 ascospores |
| **Basidium** | Club-shaped structure in Basidiomycota bearing 4 basidiospores |
| **Zygospore** | Thick-walled resistant spore formed by conjugation in Zygomycota |

## Summary

Fungi are a diverse kingdom classified into four major phyla:

- **Zygomycota** (e.g., *Rhizopus* ,  black bread mold): non-septate hyphae; sexual reproduction by conjugation forming a resistant **zygospore**.
- **Ascomycota** (e.g., yeasts, morels, truffles): septate hyphae; sexual reproduction produces 8 **ascospores** inside a sac-like **ascus** within an **ascocarp**.
- **Basidiomycota** (e.g., mushrooms, rusts, smuts): septate hyphae; sexual reproduction produces 4 **basidiospores** on club-shaped **basidia** within a **basidiocarp**.
- **Deuteromycota** (e.g., *Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*): no known sexual stage; reproduce asexually by conidia; genetic diversity via **parasexuality**.

All groups except Deuteromycota pass through a **dikaryotic ($n+n$) phase** between plasmogamy and karyogamy during sexual reproduction.

# 7.5 Importance of Fungi

Fungi are significant organisms with major roles in ecology, medicine, and industry. They can be both beneficial, acting as decomposers and sources of food and medicine, and harmful, causing diseases in plants and animals.

## Importance of Yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*)

Yeast is a single-celled fungus renowned for its diverse applications in food production, research, and biotechnology. Through fermentation, yeast metabolizes sugars (like glucose) into ethyl alcohol and carbon dioxide. This process is fundamental to brewing, producing alcoholic beverages like beer and wine and creating their characteristic alcohol content and flavors, and it is equally central to baking, where yeast acts as a leavening agent: the carbon dioxide gas produced gets trapped in dough, causing it to rise and giving bread its light, airy texture.

As a simple eukaryote with a well-understood genome, yeast is also an ideal genetic research model organism for studying fundamental biological processes, including mutations, genetic recombination, and cell biology.

In biotechnology, yeast can incorporate foreign DNA, making it a valuable tool for producing eukaryotic proteins like interferons (used in cancer research) and the hepatitis B vaccine, and it is also used to produce commercial enzymes and biofuels. Beyond baking and brewing, yeast further contributes to the production of foods like cheese, yogurt, and soy sauce.

## Mutualism: Lichens and Mycorrhizae

*Mutualism* is a symbiotic relationship where both participating organisms benefit. Fungi form two key mutualistic associations.

### Lichens

- **Definition:** A composite organism arising from a symbiotic relationship between a fungus (the *mycobiont*, usually an Ascomycete) and a photosynthetic partner (the *photobiont*, either a green alga or a cyanobacterium).
- **Structure:**
  - The fungus provides a protective structure and absorbs water and minerals.
  - The alga or cyanobacterium performs photosynthesis, providing carbohydrates for the fungus.
- **Nature of Relationship:** While traditionally viewed as mutualistic, recent studies suggest it may be a form of *controlled parasitism*, where the fungus cultivates and exploits the algal cells.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-11/biology/Pasted image 20251002030802.webp" alt="Lichen cross section" caption="Figure 7.19: A cross section of lichen" />

### Mycorrhizae

- **Definition:** A mutualistic association between a fungus and the roots of a plant (occurs in approximately 95% of higher plant families).
- **Function:** The fungal hyphae vastly increase the surface area of the plant's root system, enhancing the uptake of essential soil nutrients like phosphorus, zinc, and copper. In return, the plant provides the fungus with sugars produced via photosynthesis.

- **Types of Mycorrhizae:**

| Feature | **Endomycorrhizae** | **Ectomycorrhizae** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Interaction** | Fungal hyphae *penetrate* the outer cells of the plant root. | Fungal hyphae form a sheath *around* the root and grow between cells. |
| **Appearance** | Not visible on the root surface. | Forms a visible mantle or sheath on the root exterior. |
| **Associated Plants** | Most herbaceous plants, crops. | Primarily trees like pines and firs. |

## Edible and Poisonous Fungi

Many fungi are edible: mushrooms include approximately 200 edible species, such as those from the *Agaricus* genus, while the Ascomycetes include delicacies like morels (*Morchella esculenta*) and truffles, and *Aspergillus* species are used to ferment soybeans for soy sauce. Others are poisonous. Among these poisonous fungi, or toadstools, there is no simple way to distinguish edible from poisonous mushrooms, so expert identification is required. The genus *Amanita* contains some of the deadliest known mushrooms, including the destroying angel (*Amanita virosa*) and death cap (*Amanita phalloides*); ingesting even one can be fatal.

## Ecological Impact of Fungi

- **Decomposers and Nutrient Cycling:** Fungi are primary decomposers, breaking down dead organic matter (like wood and leaves) and returning essential nutrients to the soil. This process is critical for soil health and humus formation.

- **Carbon Cycling:** Fungi release carbon dioxide into the atmosphere during decomposition, playing a key role in the global carbon cycle.
- **Predators:** Some fungi are predatory, helping to control populations of microscopic organisms like nematodes in the soil.
- **Pollutant Degradation:** Fungi can perform biotransformation, breaking down environmental pollutants.

## Pathogenic Role of Fungi

Fungi are causative agents of numerous diseases in plants and animals.

### Plant Diseases

Fungi cause devastating crop diseases, leading to massive economic losses.

- **Ascomycetes:** Cause powdery mildew, chestnut blight, Dutch elm disease, apple scab, and brown rot of fruits.
- **Basidiomycetes:** Cause smuts and rusts that infect cereal crops like wheat and corn.
- **Ergot of Rye (*Claviceps purpurea*):** Infects rye grains and produces toxins harmful to humans.

### Animal and Human Diseases (Mycoses)

Fungal diseases in animals and humans, known as mycoses, fall into several categories. Superficial infections affect the skin, hair, and nails: ringworm is caused by fungi like *Microsporium audouni*, and athlete's foot is caused by *Tinea pedis*. Systemic infections instead affect internal tissues and organs: candidiasis, or thrush, is an infection caused by *Candida* yeast; histoplasmosis is a serious lung infection caused by inhaling spores of the *Histoplasma* fungus; and aspergillosis is a respiratory infection caused by *Aspergillus fumigatus*, common in birds and immunocompromised humans. Mycotoxins are toxic compounds produced by fungi: aflatoxins are cancer-causing toxins produced by *Aspergillus flavus* in improperly stored grains, and ergotism is caused by ingesting rye infected with *Claviceps purpurea*, with symptoms including nervous spasms, convulsions, and gangrene. Opportunistic infections affect individuals with weakened immune systems, such as Pneumocystis Pneumonia (PCP), caused by *Pneumocystis jirovecii*, a major concern for individuals with HIV/AIDS.
