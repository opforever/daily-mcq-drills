# 13.1 Morphology of Chromosomes

Chromosomes are thread-like structures of DNA and protein found in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells. Their morphology (shape and structure) is most clearly visible during **metaphase** of mitosis, when they are maximally condensed.

---

## Basic Structure of a Chromosome

A typical eukaryotic chromosome consists of three essential components:

### 1. Centromere (Primary Constriction)
The centromere is a constricted region that divides the chromosome into two arms. It is the point where:
- The two sister chromatids are held together
- The **kinetochore**,  a specialized protein complex,  assembles
- Spindle fibres (microtubules) attach during cell division to pull chromatids to opposite poles

### 2. Chromosome Arms
The centromere divides each chromosome into two arms:
- **p arm** (from French *petit* = small): the **shorter** arm
- **q arm**: the **longer** arm

The relative lengths of these arms determine the chromosome type (see below).

### 3. Telomeres
Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences (e.g., TTAGGG in humans) located at the tips of each chromosome arm. Their functions include:
- Protecting chromosome ends from degradation
- Preventing end-to-end fusion with other chromosomes
- Maintaining chromosomal stability during replication

---

## Secondary Constriction and Satellite Chromosomes

Some chromosomes possess a **secondary constriction** in addition to the primary constriction (centromere). This region is called the **Nucleolar Organizer Region (NOR)** because:
- It contains genes for ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis
- It directs the formation of the **nucleolus** after cell division

When a secondary constriction is present, it separates a small terminal segment of the chromosome called the **satellite**. Chromosomes bearing a satellite are called **SAT chromosomes** (satellite chromosomes). In humans, chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21, and 22 are SAT chromosomes.

---

## Types of Chromosomes Based on Centromere Position

Chromosomes are classified into four types based on the position of the centromere:

| Type | Centromere Position | Arm Ratio | Anaphase Shape |
|------|--------------------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Metacentric** | Exactly in the middle | p = q (equal arms) | V-shaped |
| **Sub-metacentric** | Slightly off-centre | p slightly < q | L-shaped |
| **Acrocentric** | Near one end | p very short, q very long | J-shaped |
| **Telocentric** | At the very tip | Only q arm visible | I-shaped |

> **Note:** Telocentric chromosomes are not found in the normal human karyotype but are present in mice.

---

## Karyotype Analysis

A **karyotype** is an organized profile of all chromosomes in a cell, arranged by size and centromere position. Karyotypes are prepared from cells arrested at **metaphase**, when chromosomes are most condensed and easiest to photograph and analyse. Karyotype analysis is used to:
- Detect chromosomal abnormalities (e.g., trisomy 21 in Down syndrome)
- Determine sex chromosomes
- Study chromosome morphology

# 13.2 DNA as the Hereditary Material

For many years, scientists debated whether **DNA** or **protein** was the hereditary material. Three landmark experiments settled this question.

---

## 1. Griffith's Experiment (1928)

Frederick Griffith worked with two strains of *Streptococcus pneumoniae*:

| Strain | Appearance | Virulence |
|--------|------------|-----------|
| **S-strain** (Smooth) | Smooth colonies (polysaccharide capsule) | **Virulent**,  kills mice |
| **R-strain** (Rough) | Rough colonies (no capsule) | **Non-virulent**,  harmless |

### Experimental Results

| Injection | Result |
|-----------|--------|
| Live R-strain | Mouse survives |
| Live S-strain | Mouse dies |
| Heat-killed S-strain | Mouse survives |
| Live R-strain + Heat-killed S-strain | **Mouse dies** |

When Griffith examined the dead mice from the last group, he found **live S-strain bacteria**. The heat-killed S-strain had somehow converted the harmless R-strain into virulent S-strain.

### Conclusion
Griffith proposed that a **"transforming principle"**,  some chemical substance,  passed from the dead S-strain to the live R-strain, permanently changing its hereditary properties. This heritable change is called **transformation**.

> **Transformation** is the process by which a cell takes up foreign DNA from its surroundings and incorporates it into its own genome, resulting in a heritable change in phenotype.

---

## 2. Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty Experiment (1944)

Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty set out to identify the chemical nature of Griffith's transforming principle.

### Method
They extracted material from heat-killed S-strain bacteria and treated separate samples with different enzymes:

| Enzyme Treatment | Molecule Destroyed | Transformation Occurred? |
|-----------------|-------------------|-------------------------|
| **Protease** | Proteins | **Yes** |
| **RNase** | RNA | **Yes** |
| **DNase** | DNA | **No** |
| **Lipase** | Lipids | **Yes** |

Only **DNase** (which destroys DNA) prevented transformation. This conclusively proved that **DNA is the transforming principle**,  i.e., DNA is the hereditary material.

---

## 3. Hershey-Chase Experiment (1952)

Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase used **T2 bacteriophages** (viruses that infect *E. coli*) to provide further proof that DNA is the genetic material.

### Rationale for Isotope Choice

- **$^{32}P$ (Phosphorus-32)** → labels **DNA** (DNA contains phosphorus in its sugar-phosphate backbone; proteins do not)
- **$^{35}S$ (Sulfur-35)** → labels **Protein** (amino acids methionine and cysteine contain sulfur; DNA does not)

### Procedure

1. Two batches of T2 phages were prepared:
   - Batch 1: proteins labeled with $^{35}S$
   - Batch 2: DNA labeled with $^{32}P$
2. Each batch was used to infect *E. coli* bacteria.
3. After infection, the mixture was **agitated** in a blender to separate phage coats from bacteria.
4. The mixture was **centrifuged**: bacteria (heavier) formed a **pellet**; phage coats remained in the **supernatant**.

### Results

| Labeled Component | Location After Centrifugation | Conclusion |
|------------------|------------------------------|------------|
| $^{35}S$ (Protein) | **Supernatant** (outside bacteria) | Protein did NOT enter the cell |
| $^{32}P$ (DNA) | **Pellet** (inside bacteria) | DNA **entered** the cell |

Since only DNA entered the bacterial cell and directed the production of new phage particles, **DNA is the genetic material**, not protein.

---

## Summary Comparison of the Three Experiments

| Experiment | Year | Key Finding |
|-----------|------|-------------|
| Griffith | 1928 | Identified a "transforming principle" that is heritable |
| Avery, MacLeod & McCarty | 1944 | Proved the transforming principle is **DNA** |
| Hershey & Chase | 1952 | Confirmed **DNA** (not protein) enters host cells and carries genetic information |

---

# 13.3 DNA Replication

DNA replication is the process by which a cell copies its DNA before cell division. It ensures that each daughter cell receives an identical copy of the genetic information.

## Semi-Conservative Replication

DNA replication is described as **semi-conservative** because each new DNA molecule consists of:
- One **original (parental)** strand
- One **newly synthesized (daughter)** strand

This model was proven by the **Meselson-Stahl Experiment (1958)**.

### Meselson-Stahl Experiment

1. Bacteria were grown in a medium containing **heavy nitrogen ($^{15}N$)** until all DNA was labelled with $^{15}N$.
2. Bacteria were then transferred to a medium containing **light nitrogen ($^{14}N$)**.
3. After **one generation**, DNA was extracted and centrifuged. Only **hybrid ($^{15}N$/$^{14}N$) DNA** was found,  intermediate density.
4. After **two generations**, both hybrid and light ($^{14}N$/$^{14}N$) DNA were found in a 1:1 ratio.

This result was only consistent with the **semi-conservative model**, ruling out conservative and dispersive models.

---

## Steps of DNA Replication

### 1. Initiation,  Unwinding the Double Helix

- Replication begins at specific sequences called **origins of replication**.
- **Helicase** unwinds the double helix by breaking the **hydrogen bonds** between complementary base pairs, creating a **replication fork**.
- **Topoisomerase** relieves the tension (supercoiling) ahead of the replication fork.
- **Single-Strand Binding Proteins (SSBPs)** stabilize the separated strands.

### 2. Priming,  RNA Primer Synthesis

- DNA Polymerase **cannot** start a new strand from scratch; it can only **extend** an existing strand.
- **Primase** (an RNA polymerase) synthesizes a short **RNA primer** (~10 nucleotides) complementary to the template strand.
- The primer provides the free **$3'$-OH end** that DNA Polymerase III requires to begin adding DNA nucleotides.

### 3. Elongation,  New Strand Synthesis

- **DNA Polymerase III** is the main replicative enzyme. It adds complementary deoxyribonucleotides to the $3'$-OH end of the growing strand.
- Synthesis always proceeds in the **$5' \rightarrow 3'$ direction**.
- Because the two template strands are antiparallel, the two new strands are synthesized differently:

| Strand | Template Direction | Synthesis | Mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Leading strand** | $3' \rightarrow 5'$ toward fork | $5' \rightarrow 3'$ | Continuous |
| **Lagging strand** | $5' \rightarrow 3'$ away from fork | $5' \rightarrow 3'$ | Discontinuous |

#### Okazaki Fragments (Lagging Strand)

Because the lagging strand template runs away from the fork, DNA Polymerase III must repeatedly start new segments. These short, discontinuous segments are called **Okazaki fragments**.

### 4. Primer Removal and Gap Filling

- **DNA Polymerase I** removes the RNA primers using its **$5' \rightarrow 3'$ exonuclease** activity.
- It simultaneously fills the gaps left by the removed primers with DNA nucleotides.

### 5. Ligation,  Joining the Fragments

- **DNA Ligase** seals the nicks between adjacent Okazaki fragments (and between the filled gaps and existing DNA) by forming **phosphodiester bonds**.
- This produces a continuous, intact lagging strand.

---

## Summary of Key Enzymes

| Enzyme | Function |
|---|---|
| **Helicase** | Unwinds double helix; breaks hydrogen bonds |
| **Topoisomerase** | Relieves supercoiling ahead of fork |
| **Primase** | Synthesizes RNA primers |
| **DNA Polymerase III** | Main enzyme; synthesizes new DNA ($5' \rightarrow 3'$) |
| **DNA Polymerase I** | Removes RNA primers; fills gaps with DNA |
| **DNA Ligase** | Joins Okazaki fragments; seals nicks |

---

## Key Terms

- **Replication Fork**: The Y-shaped region where the double helix is unwound and new strands are synthesized.
- **Origin of Replication**: Specific DNA sequence where replication begins.
- **Okazaki Fragments**: Short DNA segments synthesized discontinuously on the lagging strand.
- **RNA Primer**: Short RNA sequence that provides the starting point for DNA synthesis.

---

# 13.4 Gene Expression

Gene expression is the process by which the information encoded in a gene is used to produce a functional product,  most commonly a **protein**. It is the fundamental mechanism by which genotype gives rise to phenotype.

---

## The Central Dogma of Molecular Biology

Proposed by Francis Crick (1958), the **Central Dogma** describes the directional flow of genetic information:

$$DNA \rightarrow RNA \rightarrow Protein$$

| Step | Process | Enzyme | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| $DNA \rightarrow mRNA$ | Transcription | RNA Polymerase | Nucleus (eukaryotes) |
| $mRNA \rightarrow Protein$ | Translation | Ribosomes | Cytoplasm |

> **Note:** Reverse transcriptase (found in retroviruses) can also copy RNA back into DNA, which is an exception to the classical Central Dogma.

---

## Stage 1: Transcription

Transcription is the synthesis of **mRNA** from a DNA template.

### Key Components
- **Template strand (antisense strand):** The DNA strand read by RNA polymerase (3' → 5' direction)
- **Coding strand (sense strand):** The non-template strand with the same sequence as the mRNA (except T is replaced by U)
- **RNA Polymerase:** The enzyme that catalyzes mRNA synthesis (5' → 3' direction)

### Stages of Transcription
1. **Initiation:** RNA polymerase binds to the **promoter** region and unwinds the DNA double helix
2. **Elongation:** RNA polymerase moves along the template strand, adding complementary ribonucleotides
3. **Termination:** Transcription stops at a terminator sequence; the mRNA is released

### The Promoter Region
The **promoter** is a specific DNA sequence upstream (5') of a gene where RNA polymerase binds to initiate transcription. In eukaryotes, the **TATA box** (a conserved AT-rich sequence ~25–30 bp upstream of the start site) is a key promoter element recognized by transcription factors.

---

## RNA Processing in Eukaryotes (Pre-mRNA → Mature mRNA)

In eukaryotes, the initial transcript is called **pre-mRNA** and must be processed before leaving the nucleus:

### 1. 5' Capping
A modified guanosine nucleotide (7-methylguanosine cap) is added to the 5' end. It protects mRNA from degradation and assists ribosome binding.

### 2. 3' Poly-A Tail
A string of ~200 adenine nucleotides is added to the 3' end, protecting mRNA from degradation and aiding export from the nucleus.

### 3. RNA Splicing,  Introns and Exons

| Feature | Exons | Introns |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | **Ex**pressed sequences | **Int**ervening sequences |
| Fate | Retained in mature mRNA | Removed (spliced out) |
| Translation | Yes,  translated into protein | No |

**Alternative splicing** allows different combinations of exons to be joined, producing multiple different proteins from a single gene,  greatly expanding the proteome.

---

## Stage 2: Translation

Translation is the synthesis of a **polypeptide chain** using the mRNA sequence as a template, occurring on **ribosomes** in the cytoplasm.

### The Genetic Code
- mRNA is read in triplets called **codons** (e.g., AUG, UUU)
- Each codon specifies one amino acid
- **AUG** = start codon (methionine)
- **UAA, UAG, UGA** = stop codons
- The code is **degenerate** (multiple codons can specify the same amino acid) and **universal** (same in almost all organisms)

### Roles of the Three Types of RNA

| RNA Type | Role in Translation |
|---|---|
| **mRNA** (messenger RNA) | Carries the genetic code from DNA to ribosome |
| **tRNA** (transfer RNA) | Carries specific amino acids; anticodon pairs with mRNA codon |
| **rRNA** (ribosomal RNA) | Structural and catalytic component of ribosomes |

### Stages of Translation
1. **Initiation:** Ribosome assembles at the start codon (AUG) on mRNA
2. **Elongation:** tRNA molecules bring amino acids; peptide bonds form between successive amino acids
3. **Termination:** Ribosome reaches a stop codon; polypeptide is released

---

## Gene Expression and Cellular Differentiation

### Differential Gene Expression

All somatic cells in a multicellular organism contain the **same genome** (identical DNA). However, different cell types (e.g., liver cells, neurons, muscle cells) look and function very differently because they **express different subsets of genes**.

This selective activation and silencing of genes is called **differential gene expression** and is the basis of:
- **Cell differentiation**,  development of specialized cell types
- **Tissue formation**,  organization of cells into tissues and organs
- **Development**,  coordinated changes during embryonic development

**Example:** A liver cell expresses genes for albumin and clotting factors; a neuron expresses genes for neurotransmitter synthesis. Both cells have both sets of genes, but only the relevant ones are expressed.

---

## Summary: Levels of Gene Expression Control

Gene expression can be regulated at multiple levels:

1. **Transcriptional control**,  most common; controls whether a gene is transcribed (promoters, transcription factors)
2. **RNA processing control**,  alternative splicing, capping, polyadenylation
3. **mRNA stability control**,  degradation rate of mRNA
4. **Translational control**,  whether mRNA is translated
5. **Post-translational control**,  protein modification, folding, degradation

---

# 13.5 Regulating Gene Expression

Gene expression must be carefully regulated so that cells produce the right proteins at the right time and in the right amounts. Regulation occurs at multiple levels and differs significantly between prokaryotes and eukaryotes.

---

## Gene Regulation in Prokaryotes ,  The Operon Model

In prokaryotes, genes that encode enzymes for the same metabolic pathway are often grouped together into a functional unit called an **operon**.

### Components of an Operon

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Promoter** | DNA sequence where RNA polymerase binds to initiate transcription |
| **Operator** | DNA sequence between the promoter and structural genes; binding site for the repressor protein |
| **Structural genes** | Genes that encode the enzymes of the metabolic pathway |
| **Regulator gene** | Located elsewhere; codes for the repressor protein |

### Types of Operons

Operons are classified based on whether they are normally active or inactive:

#### 1. Inducible Operons ,  The Lac Operon

The **lac operon** of *E. coli* controls the metabolism of lactose. It is normally **OFF** (repressed).

When lactose is absent, the repressor protein (coded by the *lacI* gene) is active. The repressor binds to the **operator**, blocking RNA polymerase, so the structural genes (*lacZ*, *lacY*, *lacA*) are **not transcribed**. When lactose is present, it is converted to **allolactose** (the true inducer). Allolactose binds to the repressor, causing a conformational change so that the repressor can no longer bind the operator. RNA polymerase then transcribes the structural genes, and enzymes for lactose digestion are produced.

> **Key concept:** Allolactose is the **inducer**; it *inactivates* the repressor.

#### 2. Repressible Operons ,  The Trp Operon

The **trp operon** controls the biosynthesis of the amino acid tryptophan. It is normally **ON** (active).

When tryptophan is scarce, the repressor protein is synthesised in an **inactive** form, so RNA polymerase transcribes the structural genes and tryptophan is synthesised. When tryptophan is abundant, it acts as a **co-repressor** by binding to the inactive repressor. The repressor-tryptophan complex undergoes a conformational change and becomes active, and the active complex binds to the **operator**, blocking transcription. Tryptophan synthesis then stops (end-product inhibition at the gene level).

> **Key concept:** Tryptophan is the **co-repressor**; it *activates* the repressor.

### Summary: Inducible vs. Repressible

| Feature | Inducible (lac) | Repressible (trp) |
|---|---|---|
| Default state | OFF | ON |
| Regulatory molecule | Inducer (allolactose) | Co-repressor (tryptophan) |
| Effect on repressor | Inactivates it | Activates it |
| Biological logic | Turn on when substrate available | Turn off when product is sufficient |

## Gene Regulation in Eukaryotes

Eukaryotic gene regulation is far more complex than in prokaryotes. Regulation can occur at multiple levels.

The first level is **chromatin remodelling**: tightly packed heterochromatin is inaccessible to RNA polymerase, so genes must be in loosely packed euchromatin to be expressed, and histone acetylation loosens chromatin to allow this. The most important level is **transcriptional control**, which involves several elements working together. **Promoters** are sequences upstream of the gene (such as the TATA box) where the transcription initiation complex assembles. **Enhancers** are distal DNA sequences that, when bound by transcription factors, increase the transcription rate and can be thousands of base pairs away from the gene, while **silencers** are sequences that decrease transcription. **Transcription factors** are proteins that bind to promoters or enhancers to promote or inhibit RNA polymerase recruitment.

**Post-transcriptional control** covers the processing of pre-mRNA: the addition of a **5' cap** and **3' poly-A tail** increases mRNA stability, **RNA splicing** removes introns and joins exons (a process absent in prokaryotes), and **alternative splicing** allows different exon combinations from one gene to produce multiple proteins. Beyond this, **translational control** regulates when and how much mRNA is translated, and **post-translational control** modifies proteins after synthesis, for example through phosphorylation or glycosylation.

### Transcription Factors

Transcription factors are regulatory proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences (promoters or enhancers) and interact with RNA polymerase or the transcription initiation complex. They either **activate** (activators) or **repress** (repressors) transcription, which allows cells with identical DNA to express different genes, a phenomenon known as **differential gene expression**.

> **Example:** A liver cell and a neuron have the same genome but express different sets of genes because different transcription factors are active in each cell type.

# 13.6 Mutations

A **mutation** is a sudden, permanent change in the nucleotide sequence of DNA. Mutations may alter the structure and function of the proteins encoded by the affected gene, with consequences ranging from no effect to serious disease.

---

## Types of Mutations

### 1. Based on Cell Type

| Type | Location | Heritable? | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Germ-line mutation** | Gametes (egg/sperm) | Yes | Passed to all cells of offspring |
| **Somatic mutation** | Body (non-reproductive) cells | No | Affects only the individual; can cause cancer |

### 2. Point Mutations

A **point mutation** is a change in a single nucleotide pair. There are two categories:

#### a) Substitution Mutations
- **Transition**,  a purine is replaced by a purine (A ↔ G), or a pyrimidine by a pyrimidine (C ↔ T).
- **Transversion**,  a purine is replaced by a pyrimidine, or vice versa (e.g., A → C).

Substitutions can produce three outcomes:

| Outcome | Description | Effect on Protein |
|---|---|---|
| **Silent mutation** | Codon changes but encodes the same amino acid (due to degeneracy of the genetic code) | No change |
| **Missense mutation** | Codon changes to encode a different amino acid | Altered protein (may be functional or non-functional) |
| **Nonsense mutation** | Codon changes to a stop codon | Premature termination → truncated, non-functional protein |

#### b) Insertion / Deletion (Frameshift) Mutations

A **frameshift mutation** occurs when nucleotides are inserted or deleted in a number that is **not a multiple of three**. This shifts the reading frame of all subsequent codons, usually producing a completely non-functional protein.

> **Example:** Inserting one base near the start of a gene alters every codon downstream,  the resulting protein bears no resemblance to the original.

If nucleotides are inserted or deleted in **multiples of three**, the reading frame is preserved and only one or a few amino acids are added or removed.

---

## Example: Sickle Cell Anaemia

Sickle cell anaemia is a classic example of the significance of a single point mutation:

- A **transition mutation** changes codon **GAG → GUG** in the beta-globin gene.
- This causes **Glutamic acid** (hydrophilic, negatively charged) to be replaced by **Valine** (hydrophobic, nonpolar) at position 6 of the beta-globin chain.
- The altered haemoglobin (HbS) molecules stick together under low-oxygen conditions, distorting red blood cells into a sickle shape.
- Sickled cells block capillaries and are destroyed rapidly, causing anaemia and organ damage.

This example illustrates **B-11-C-14**: the sequence of amino acids is critical to protein function.

---

## Mutagens

**Mutagens** are physical or chemical agents that increase the rate of mutation above the spontaneous background level.

| Type | Examples | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical mutagens** | UV radiation, X-rays, gamma rays | UV → thymine dimers; ionising radiation → strand breaks |
| **Chemical mutagens** | Ethidium bromide, nitrous acid, alkylating agents | Alter base structure or intercalate into DNA, causing mispairing |

---

## Summary Table

| Mutation Type | Cause | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Transition | Purine ↔ Purine or Pyrimidine ↔ Pyrimidine | May be silent, missense, or nonsense |
| Transversion | Purine ↔ Pyrimidine | May be silent, missense, or nonsense |
| Frameshift | Insertion/deletion (not multiple of 3) | Alters all downstream codons |
| Silent | Base substitution → synonymous codon | No change in protein |
| Missense | Base substitution → different amino acid | Altered protein (e.g., sickle cell) |
| Nonsense | Base substitution → stop codon | Truncated protein |
