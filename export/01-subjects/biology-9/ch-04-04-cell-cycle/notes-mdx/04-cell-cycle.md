# 4.1 Cell Cycle

The cell cycle is the sequence of events that involves the growth of a newly formed cell, replication of its genome, and its eventual division into two daughter cells. It consists of two main phases: interphase (further divided into G1, S, and G2) and the mitotic phase (M phase), during which the cell divides into daughter cells.

## G1 Phase

It starts with the production of the new cell. The cell grows in size, increases the number of its organelles, and forms needed proteins and other substances. During this phase the cell prepares itself for the next phase of the cell cycle (S phase), forming the enzymes and nucleotides of DNA required for the replication process.

## S Phase

S phase is the synthesis phase of DNA. The cell replicates its entire genetic material to form two copies. That is why each chromosome has two chromatids during cell division, one for each daughter cell. These chromatids are attached at one centromere and are called sister chromatids.

## G2 Phase

During this phase the cell prepares for the mitotic phase. The cell forms proteins, especially those required for the formation of spindle fibres, and accumulates energy to complete the division process. More mitochondria are formed.

## G0 Phase

During G1, a cell may exit the cell cycle and enter G0 phase. In G0 the cell stops dividing. Some cells, e.g. neurons, once mature never divide again; they remain in G0 forever. Cells of the liver and kidneys enter G0 temporarily; when needed they re-enter the cell cycle and start to divide again. Many epithelial cells divide continuously and never enter G0 phase.

## Cancer and the Cell Cycle

Cancer is a disease of the cell cycle. Unlike normal cells of the body, cancer cells do not have a properly functioning cell cycle control system and therefore divide excessively. This excessive growth can result in an abnormal mass of cells called a tumour. Not all tumours are cancerous: a benign tumour is an abnormal mass of essentially normal cells that always remains at its original site in the body. A malignant tumour is cancerous; it is capable of spreading into neighbouring tissues and often to other distant parts of the body. The spread of cancer cells beyond their original site is called metastasis.

# 4.2 Spindle Apparatus

## Formation and Function of the Spindle Apparatus

The cytoskeleton of the eukaryotic cell forms the spindle apparatus during cell division. The major component of the cytoskeleton is microtubules. The spindle apparatus separates chromatids or chromosomes during cell division and moves them to opposite poles. In this way the hereditary material of the parent cell is equally distributed into the daughter cells.

A pair of centrioles is situated near the nucleus of an animal cell. They duplicate and another pair is formed. Both pairs migrate to opposite poles. They also give rise to spindle fibres. The spindle apparatus is formed around the nucleus, but as soon as the nuclear envelope disintegrates, spindle fibres penetrate into the region of the nucleus. They later attach to the centromere of chromosomes and pull them to their poles.

There are no centrioles in plant cells; the spindle apparatus of a plant cell is formed without centrioles.

# 4.3 Mitosis

The cell division in which a parent cell produces two daughter cells with the same number of chromosomes as in the parent cell. Mitosis is a continuous and very fast process which takes less than an hour, but to study it easily it is divided into karyokinesis and cytokinesis.

## Karyokinesis

Karyokinesis is the division of the nucleus, which forms two nuclei in a dividing cell. It is further divided into prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase.

## Prophase

During interphase hereditary material is found in the form of very thin threads called chromatin. If a cell is going to divide, all of its chromatin fibres duplicate during the S phase of interphase. Prophase is the longest phase of cell division. At the start of prophase, chromatin fibres coil up and condense into chromosomes. Due to duplication, all chromosomes consist of two chromatids. The chromatids of each chromosome are attached to each other at the centromere. The nucleolus disappears as its DNA is packed into chromosomes. At the end of prophase, the nuclear membrane splits into vesicles which disperse in the cytoplasm, and the spindle apparatus is formed.

<CaptionedImage src="kg28w7bwwyzxqzwpq6qf7pcz598dm0yr" alt="Diagram showing the stages of mitosis in a cell." caption="Fig 4.3: Mitosis" />
## Metaphase

Chromosomes are aligned at the equator of the spindle. Two spindle fibres, one from each pole, are attached at the centromere of each chromosome.

## Anaphase

Spindle fibres pull the centromeres, which split, and chromatids are separated from each other. Individual chromatids are pulled until they reach their respective poles.

## Telophase

Chromatids reach their poles. They uncoil and lengthen to form chromatin fibres again. The spindle apparatus disintegrates, the nuclear membrane re-forms around chromatin at each pole, and nucleoli reappear in both nuclei.

## Cytokinesis

Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm, which results in the formation of two daughter cells. It starts while telophase is in progress. The pattern of cytokinesis differs between animal and plant cells.

<CaptionedImage src="kg24tpa5aabn1845h98njww99d8dn3qz" alt="Diagram showing cytokinesis in an animal cell, forming a cleavage furrow." caption="Fig 4.4: Cytokinesis in animal cell" />

In animal cells, the cell membrane begins to invaginate in the equator region. As a result, a cleavage furrow is formed which continues to grow inward. The cell membranes in the furrow finally join up and separate the two daughter cells. In plant cells, spindle fibres in the equator region form a structure called the phragmoplast. The Golgi apparatus forms vesicles which appear in the centre of the phragmoplast initially and then grow at the equatorial plane. The contents of these vesicles form the middle lamella and primary walls of the daughter cells. Later, some cells form secondary walls.

<CaptionedImage src="kg22ajn8v7w7m88eh5ryvthv618e781v" alt="Fig 4.5: Cytokinesis in plant cell" caption="Fig 4.5: Cytokinesis in plant cell" />
## Significance of Mitosis

The main function of mitosis is to maintain the number of chromosomes and genetic material in all cells of an organism. The following is the significance of mitosis.

**Genetic stability:** Mitosis produces two daughter cells with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. Before the start of cell division, the DNA of the parental chromosomes replicates to form two exact copies. Daughter cells thus have the same genetic information.

**Development and growth:** The life of sexually reproducing multicellular organisms starts from a single cell, the zygote. The continuous cell division process leads to the development of the adult form. Organisms also grow in size by increasing cell number. Thus the development and growth of multicellular organisms depends on mitosis.

**Cell replacement and wound healing:** The replacement of worn-out cells involves mitosis. The epithelial cells of the skin, digestive tract, and respiratory tract die off regularly. They are replaced by identical cells produced by mitosis. Damage repair and wound healing depend on the process of mitosis.

**Regeneration:** Some animals have the ability to form a whole part of the body if it is removed accidentally; this is called regeneration. For example, a sea star can regenerate an arm, earthworms can regenerate a head, and a salamander can regenerate its limb. The production of new cells to form missing parts involves mitosis.

<CaptionedImage src="kg28rgkcrfq24pax1q96xvfjfh8dmd8q" alt="Photograph showing regeneration of an arm in a sea star." caption="Fig 4.6: Regeneration of arm in Sea star" />

**Asexual reproduction:** Mitosis is the basis of asexual reproduction. This reproduction involves only one parent. All asexually produced offspring are genetically identical to their parent organism. Vegetative propagation is very common in plants; it involves new generations from the root, stem, or leaf of a parent plant. Potato, onion, garlic, ginger, and grasses reproduce asexually. Many animal species like sponges, planaria, and hydra undergo asexual reproduction.

<CaptionedImage src="kg293we4jfk7rk59npe9h9zj5h8dn874" alt="Diagram showing budding in hydra as a form of asexual reproduction." caption="Fig 4.7: Budding in Hydra" />

**Cloning and tissue culture:** Mitosis has made it possible for scientists to produce a very large number of identical copies of living organisms in an artificial environment. This process is called cloning, or tissue culture if a small mass of tissue cells is used.

# 4.4 Meiosis

It is a type of cell division which gives rise to four daughter cells each having half the number of chromosomes of the parent cell. It takes place only in cells involved in sexual reproduction. Such parent cells are usually diploid which by this reduction division produce haploid daughter cells.

## Interphase I

Before the onset of division process, cell forms a copy of its genome by replication process. That is why chromosomes appear with two chromatids during division process.

## Meiosis I

It is the reduction division which forms daughter cells that have the number of chromosomes reduced to half. Firstly, karyokinesis forms two haploid nuclei which is followed by cytokinesis.

### Prophase I

It is the longest phase of meiosis. Chromosomes begin to appear as thicker fibres due to coiling of chromatin. Homologous chromosomes line up point to point against each other and form pairs. Their centromeres are in the same position. This pairing process of homologous chromosomes is called synapsis. Each pair is called bivalent.

<CaptionedImage src="kg2csstc317vjgaezy0jgchsg98cszrk" alt="Diagram showing crossing over between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during prophase I." caption="Fig. 4.9 Crossing over in non-sister chromatids." />

### Metaphase I

Bivalents are arranged at the equatorial plate. Spindle fibres are attached to the centromere of chromosome. One member of a homologous pair receives a spindle fibre from one pole and other member from the opposite pole.

### Anaphase I

Spindle fibres pull on the homologous chromosomes. Separated chromosomes of each pair move to the opposite poles. Each pole receives haploid set of chromosomes.

### Telophase I

Once at their pole, chromosomes uncoil into chromatin. A nuclear envelope is formed at each pole around the haploid set.

Cytokinesis occurs by cleavage in animal cell or by forming cell wall in plant cell. Although each daughter cell is haploid with half the number of chromosomes, their chromosomes are composed of two chromatids. Due to crossing over, these chromatids are not genetically identical. They must be separated in second meiosis. Two haploid daughter cells thus formed enter in interphase II.
## Interphase II

This phase varies in length but there is no further DNA replication during this phase.

## Meiosis II

It is similar to mitosis and is divided into prophase II, metaphase II, anaphase II and telophase II.

### Prophase II

Chromatin coils again so chromosomes appear as thick fibres. Spindle apparatus is organized. Nucleoli disappear and nuclear envelope disintegrates.

### Metaphase II

Chromosomes line up separately around the equator of the spindles. Spindle fibres are attached to the centromere of chromosome. Like mitosis, here each chromosome is attached by a fibre from both poles.

### Anaphase II

Centromeres divide and spindle fibres pull the chromatids to the opposite poles.

### Telophase II

Chromatids reach at their poles and uncoil. The spindle apparatus disappears. Nuclear envelopes are formed around chromatin at each pole.

Cleavage furrow is formed in animal cells and cell wall formation occurs in plant cells. It gives four daughter cells at the end of meiosis process.

## Maintenance of Chromosomes in Sexual Reproduction

Maintenance of chromosomes: During the life cycle of sexually reproducing organisms, diploid germ cells undergo meiosis to produce haploid daughter cells which act as gametes. Fusion of such haploid gametes thus maintains chromosome number specific for each species. Human have 46 chromosomes in their somatic cells. The haploid gametes (eggs and sperms) formed by meiosis have 23 chromosomes. In fertilization process a 23 chromosome sperm fuses with a 23 chromosome egg and zygote with 46 chromosomes is formed.

## Genetic Variations

Variations are the differences among the members of same species. They are necessary for the survival of species in always changing environment. It is because of meiosis that new combinations of genes appear in gametes and then in zygote. Crossing over and independently formed combinations of chromosomes in gametes are two important events of meiosis. Both crossing over and chromosomal combination provide basis of variations.
