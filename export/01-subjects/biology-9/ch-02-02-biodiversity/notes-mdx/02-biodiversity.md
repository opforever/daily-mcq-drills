# 2.1 Definition and Introduction to Biodiversity

## What Is Biodiversity

Living organisms share the basic characteristics of life: movement, respiration, sensitivity, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, and growth. At the same time, living things differ enormously from one another.

The term *biodiversity* comes from "biological diversity." Biodiversity has both ecological and economic importance: it provides us with nourishment, housing, fuel, clothing, and more. Biodiversity is defined as "the variety of all living organisms on Earth."


<CaptionedImage src="kg281sw457b6rtry6gmd5q0btn8cs181" alt="The enormous variety of living organisms on Earth is what we call biodiversity." caption="The enormous variety of living organisms on Earth is what we call biodiversity." />
## Why Biodiversity Matters

Natural biodiversity provides us with oxygen, clean water, and air. It supports the carbon cycle and helps fix nutrients, enabling plants to grow. Pests are kept in check by organisms such as insects, birds, and fungi. Biodiversity helps protect against flooding, regulates climate, and enables pollination and crop production.

Biodiversity is also the source of our food and of medicines derived mainly from plants. Many industrial materials, such as building materials, fibres, dyes, resins, gums, adhesives, rubber, and oil, are derived directly from plants.

# 2.2 Classification

## What Is Classification?

Classification is the grouping of related facts into classes. It is a process which brings together like things and separates unlike things.

## Advantages of Classification

Biologists have devised ways of grouping organisms. The grouping of organisms is called classification. Taxonomy is the branch of biology concerned with identification, naming and classification of organisms. The scientific study of diversity of organisms and their evolutionary relationship is called systematics.

The main aims and objectives of classification are:

1. To determine similarities and differences between organisms.
2. To arrange organisms on the basis of similarities and differences.
3. To identify the organisms to study them systematically.
4. To find out evolutionary relationships among organisms.

# 2.3 History of Classification

## Early Classification Scientists

The Greek philosopher Aristotle was the first person to classify living organisms. Abu Usama Aljahiz described 350 species of animals. By the end of the 15th century, many biologists had worked on classification methods.

- **Andrea Caesalpino (1519-1603):** divided plants into fifteen groups, which he called genera.
- **John Ray (1627-1705):** published important works on the classification of plants.
- **Tournefort (1656-1708):** introduced the taxa of class and species.
- **Carolus Linnaeus (1707-1778):** grouped species according to similar physical characteristics.

Classification systems developed over time from a two-kingdom system, to a three-kingdom system, and then to a five-kingdom system.

## Kingdom Classification Systems


<CaptionedImage src="kg20pjg0gdyemkvxkh2qbvyxks8cs1h8" alt="The development of kingdom classification systems." caption="The development of kingdom classification systems." />


1. **Two-kingdom system:** the oldest system, classifying organisms into the kingdoms Plantae and Animalia. Plantae included the autotrophs, as well as bacteria, fungi, and algae. Animalia included organisms that depend on autotrophs or other heterotrophs.

   Many unicellular organisms, such as *Euglena*, have both plant-like (chlorophyll present) and animal-like (heterotrophic nutrition in darkness, no cell wall) characteristics, so a separate kingdom was needed for such organisms.

2. **Three-kingdom system:** the German scientist Ernst Haeckel proposed a third kingdom, Protista, to accommodate *Euglena*-like organisms and separate unicellular microscopic organisms from multicellular ones.

3. **Five-kingdom system:** in 1937, E. Chatton suggested the terms "Procariotique" for bacteria and "Eucariotique" for plant and animal cells. In 1967, R. H. Whittaker introduced the five-kingdom classification system: **Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia.**

Organisms that lack a nucleus in their cells are called *prokaryotes*, while organisms that have a nucleus are called *eukaryotes*. In the five-kingdom system, bacteria and archaea were combined into a single kingdom, Monera, because they share the prokaryotic form of cell structure.

# 2.4 Classification - the Three Domains System

# 2.4 Classification: The Three Domains System

In biology, a domain means the largest of all groups in the classification of life. Domain is a group of kingdoms or taxonomic category above the kingdom. In 1990, Carl Woese introduced a three domains system of classification. The three domains of life are Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya.

Classification into three domains is based on difference in the sequence of nucleotides in the rRNA (ribosomal Ribonucleic acid) of the cell, the cell's membrane lipid structure and its sensitivity to antibiotics.


<CaptionedImage src="kg235pqtxzmgjv54wnvrj0xs9s8cs8h4" alt="Fig. 2.2 The three domains of life." caption="Fig. 2.2 The three domains of life." />
## The Domain Archaea

The domain Archaea have the following characteristics:

a. Archaea are prokaryotic cells.
b. The cell walls of Archaea contain no peptidoglycan.
c. The rRNA of Archaea is unique and differs from that found in Bacteria and Eukarya.
d. Archaea are not sensitive to some antibiotics that affect bacteria. They are sensitive to some antibiotics that affect the Eukarya.
e. Archaea often live in extreme environment.
f. Archaeal membrane can withstand higher temperature and stronger acid concentration.
g. Archaeal creatures include: Methanogens, Halophiles, Thermoacidophiles.
## The Domain Bacteria

The domain bacteria have the following characteristics:

a. Bacteria are prokaryotic cells.
b. The cell walls of bacteria contain peptidoglycan.
c. They contain rRNA that is unique to bacteria.
d. Bacteria are sensitive to traditional antibacterial antibiotics but are resistant to most antibiotics that affect eukarya.

## The Domain Eukarya

The domain Eukarya (also spelled Eucarya) have the following characteristics:

a. Eukarya have eukaryotic cells.
b. Not all Eukarya have cells with a cell wall. Their cell wall contains no peptidoglycan.
c. Eukarya contains rRNA that is unique to Eukarya.
d. Eukarya are resistant to traditional antibacterial antibiotics but are sensitive to most antibiotics that affect eukaryotic cells.


<CaptionedImage src="kg2dk6b9phqmm5hdkwa99sw8qx8csf60" alt="Fig. 2.4 The four kingdoms of Domain Eukarya." caption="Fig. 2.4 The four kingdoms of Domain Eukarya." />


The domain Eukarya are divided into four kingdoms: Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.

1. **Protista**: Protists include eukaryotic organisms with unicellular or colonial organization. These are mostly aquatic. It is a diverse group of organisms. It includes: Animal like protists called protozoa e.g., Amoeba. Plant like protists called algae e.g., Euglena. Fungi like protists e.g., slime molds.
2. **Fungi**: Fungi are eukaryotic organisms which have chitin in their cell wall. Fungi are saprotrophic decomposers. Mostly fungi are multicellular. Some fungi are unicellular. The examples of fungi are black bread mold, yeast, mushroom, etc.
3. **Plantae**: The members of kingdom plantae are eukaryotic multicellular and autotrophic with chloroplasts containing chlorophyll. Their cell wall is made up of cellulose e.g., moss, mustard.
4. **Animalia**: Animals are multicellular heterotrophic eukaryotes. Animals lack cell wall and chlorophyll. They can generally move from place to place. This kingdom includes invertebrates e.g., insects, starfish and vertebrates e.g., fish, frogs and man.

The organisms that are capable of producing their own food are called autotrophs (photosynthetic mode of nutrition) e.g., green plants, autotrophic bacteria, and algae. These are producers.
Organisms which eat other things as food are called heterotrophs (ingestive mode of nutrition) e.g., animals, animal like protists, etc. These are consumers
The organisms that depend on dead, decaying matter are called saprotrophs (absorptive mode of nutrition) e.g., fungi, bacteria. These are decomposers.

# 2.5 Taxonomic Ranks of Classification

## The Taxonomic Hierarchy

The group into which organisms are classified are known as taxonomic categories or taxa (singular 'taxon'). The taxa form a ladder, called taxonomic hierarchy. There are eight main taxonomic ranks: domain, kingdom, phylum or division, class, order, family, genus and species.

The kingdom is the largest taxon or rank below domain. Each kingdom is further divided into smaller taxa in the following way:

- **Phylum** (Division: for plants and fungi): A phylum is a group of related classes.
- **Class**: A class is a group of related orders.
- **Order**: An order is group of related families.
- **Family**: A family is a group of related genera.
- **Genus**: A genus is a group of related species.
- **Species**: A species is a group of similar organisms.

# 2.7 Binomial Nomenclature

Carolus Linnaeus introduced a naming system to give each organism a name consisting of two Latin names. The first name is genus name and the second name represents the particular species. The genus name begins with a capital letter but the species name begins with a small letter. Since each name has two parts so it is called binomial nomenclature, e.g., biological name of human beings is Homo sapiens. Our genus name is Homo and species name is sapiens. A genus may have many species e.g., all cats belong to genus Felis including lion.

## Importance of Binomial Nomenclature

Why do organisms need to be given a scientific name in Latin? Why can't we just use common names for organisms? A common name will vary from country to country just because different countries use different languages. Hence there was a need for a universal language such as Latin. Even those who speak the same language sometime use different common name for the same organisms. Example: Brinjal is Baigun in Urdu, Bataoon in Punjabi, Vagton in Sindhi. Is it not confusing? Its biological name is Solanum melongena. 1. Potato - Solanum tuberosum
2. Rice - Oryza sativa

A scientific name has the advantage of standing for a single kind of animal, plant or microorganism all over the world.

# 2.8 Complications of Classifying Viruses

## Living and Non-living Characteristics of Viruses

Viruses show characteristics of both living and non-living things, which is why they are difficult to classify.

**Living characteristics of viruses:**

1. They occur in different varieties.
2. They have their own genetic material in the form of either RNA or DNA.
3. They reproduce using the material of the host cell they infect.
4. They enter the cells of living organisms and cause diseases.

**Non-living characteristics of viruses:**

1. They lack cellular structure and enzyme system.
2. They can be crystallized and stored in a bottle.
3. They do not respire.
4. They behave as non-living, inert infectious particles outside the host.

Because viruses sit at the borderline of living and non-living, they are not included in any domain or kingdom under the modern classification system.

**Prions and viroids** are two other acellular infectious particles related to this classification problem. Prions are composed of protein only, and viroids are composed of circular RNA only. Both cause infectious diseases in certain plants and, like viruses, are not included in any kingdom of the classification system.
