# 7.1 Metabolism and Its Types

## Metabolism: Catabolism and Anabolism

The word metabolism means to change. The sum of all the chemical reactions going on within each cell of living organisms for the maintenance and sustainability of life is known as metabolism.

Metabolism is divided into two types: catabolism and anabolism. Biochemical reactions which break down complex molecules into simpler molecules are called catabolic reactions or catabolism. For example, digestion is the catabolic process in which larger and complex food molecules are broken down into smaller and simpler molecules. Cellular respiration is also a catabolic process where glucose molecules are broken down to CO2 and H2O by a series of reactions in all body cells.

Reactions which build complex molecules from simpler molecules are called anabolic reactions or anabolism. For example, photosynthesis is an anabolic process where simple molecules like CO2 and H2O are converted to the larger molecule glucose. Condensation of glucose molecules to form glycogen in muscle and liver cells is also an anabolic reaction. Usually, energy is released in catabolism and it is utilized in anabolism.

# 7.2 Enzymes and Their Characteristics

## What Are Enzymes?

Vital biochemical reactions taking place in the body must occur quickly and precisely for a cell to perform and survive. These metabolic reactions in living organisms are sped up by specific proteins in the body called biological catalysts or enzymes. "Enzymes are biologically active globular proteins made by living cells, which tremendously speed up the biochemical reactions". Each cell synthesizes its own enzymes and the type of enzymes produced in each cell are specific, determined by the genes active in that cell.

Enzymes act on specific molecules in the cell called substrates. The substrates are the molecules entering into chemical reactions. After binding to their specific enzyme, substrates undergo chemical changes resulting in a new bonding arrangement between the molecules. The substrates modified by enzymes are called products.

The enzymes which stay inside the cells to speed up the reactions are called intracellular enzymes (e.g., enzymes of glycolysis working in the cytoplasm). Often the enzymes made inside the cells are allowed to go out of the cell to do their work outside. These enzymes are called extracellular enzymes (e.g., pepsin enzyme working in the stomach cavity). Fungi and bacteria release extracellular enzymes to digest their food. Study of structure, type and role of enzymes is called enzymology.

> Science Titbits: If you chew your food longer, more starch digesting enzyme amylase is produced and mixed with food that helps in breakdown of food, especially starch. Moreover, the process of chewing also triggers the production of HCl in the stomach that regulates the lower pH in stomach to activate the enzyme pepsin, which breaks down protein.

Enzymes have the following characteristics.

## Enzymes Are Globular Proteins

All enzymes are made up of chains of amino acids folded to form 3D protein molecules called globular proteins. Few enzymes are RNA in nature, called ribozymes.

## Enzymes Increase Rate of Reaction

A reaction may initiate without involvement of enzymes, but it will be extremely slow and may take months or years to complete. Enzymes speed up reactions millions of times faster compared to non-enzymatic reactions. It is important to know that enzymes only speed up a reaction and do not start a chemical reaction.

## Enzymes Are Required in Small Quantity

The structure of an enzyme may slightly change during a reaction, but it regains its actual shape, so it remains unchanged at the end of the reaction. Thus, it can be used repeatedly, and a very small quantity of an enzyme is capable of catalysing a huge amount of substrate.

## Enzymes Are Specific

Enzymes are usually very specific, so they catalyse only one type of reaction and will not act on a different substrate. For example, amylase will only act on starch and not on proteins or fats.

## Enzymes Require a Cofactor

Many enzymes require an additional non-protein component for their proper functioning, called a cofactor. A cofactor usually acts as a bridge between the protein part of the enzyme and its substrate, or may provide energy to the enzyme. There are three types of cofactors:

a. **Activator:** Some enzymes require detachable inorganic ions as cofactor, called activators, such as Zn++, Fe++, Cu++ and chlorides. For example, the activity of salivary amylase is increased in the presence of chloride ions.
b. **Prosthetic group:** If the cofactor is an organic molecule tightly and permanently attached to the enzyme, it is known as a prosthetic group, e.g. Flavin Adenine Dinucleotide (FAD), the Haem group.
c. **Coenzyme:** If the cofactor is a detachable organic molecule, it is called a coenzyme. Examples of coenzymes are NAD (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide), coenzyme A, and vitamins A and C.

## Regulation of Enzyme Production and Activity

Synthesis of enzymes within the cells can be increased or decreased by a cell according to requirements. Enzyme activity can be regulated by inhibitors or activators from within or outside the cell.

## Enzymes Make Metabolic Pathways

Many enzymes work together in a specific sequence to make metabolic pathways. A metabolic pathway is a series of connected chemical reactions where multiple intermediate molecules are produced before conversion of an initial substrate into the final product.

## Energy of Activation

The minimum amount of extra energy required to activate a molecule to react or start a reaction is called the energy of activation. Enzymes lower the activation energy so that these reactions can take place at body temperature. Enzymes lower the activation energy by changing the shape and charge of the substrate. An enzyme may provide an entirely different mechanism of reaction to lower the activation energy.

# 7.3 Mechanism of Enzyme Action

Most enzymes are far larger protein molecules than the substrates they act on. Specific part on the globular surface of enzyme where substrate binds and actually take part in reaction is called the active site. The active site is usually a very small portion of the enzyme which is a charge bearing cavity having a specific shape.

Specific substrate molecule temporarily binds to the active site of enzyme to form Enzyme Substrate (ES) complex. Once the ES complex has formed, enzyme catalyzes the reaction to convert the substrate in to product, thus converting the ES complex to Enzyme Product (EP) Complex. Finally, EP complex breaks up into products and enzyme. The enzyme molecule remains unchanged at the end of the reaction and is available again if needed for same reaction again (fig 7.2).

# 7.4 Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

Several environmental factors affect the rate at which enzymatic reactions proceed.

## Temperature

The rate of reaction increases with increase in temperature but only up to a specific limit. Each enzyme works best at a specific temperature called its optimum temperature. Different enzymes have different optimum temperatures, e.g. the optimum temperature for human enzymes is 36°C to 38°C. Any increase or decrease in temperature affects the rate of enzymatic reaction. At very high temperatures, atoms of the enzyme molecule start vibrating vigorously, so the globular structure and active site of the enzyme are damaged; this is called denaturation. At very low temperatures the enzyme remains inactive due to a fall in kinetic energy.

## pH

The pH of the medium affects enzyme functioning. Each enzyme performs best at a specific pH, called its optimum pH. A slight change in pH of the reacting medium below or above the optimum pH reduces the rate of reaction. Extreme changes in pH denature the enzyme. For example, pepsin and trypsin are both protein-digesting enzymes: pepsin performs best in the acidic medium of the stomach at pH 2.00, whereas trypsin performs best in the alkaline medium of the small intestine at pH 8.00.

## Substrate Concentration

In the presence of enough enzyme molecules at the reaction site, the rate of reaction is directly proportional to the substrate available. So the rate of an enzymatic reaction increases with increasing substrate concentration, up to a certain limit. After that, a point is reached where further increase in substrate concentration has no effect on the rate of reaction. This happens because extra substrate molecules in the medium have occupied all the active sites of the available enzyme molecules; this condition is called saturation of the active sites.

<CaptionedImage src="kg24r5rm1vmeec03bzzqx3prv58dmv7v" alt="Graph showing enzyme reaction rate plateauing as substrate concentration increases, due to active-site saturation." caption="Fig. 7.5: Effect of Enzyme and substrate concentration on the rate of enzyme action" />

Inhibitors are molecules produced in the cell or entering it from outside that stop enzymatic reactions, while activators are molecules that increase the rate of an enzymatic reaction.

# 7.5 Enzyme Inhibition

Sometimes enzymes are not able to perform their role due to the presence and interference of some chemicals at the reaction site; this phenomenon is called enzyme inhibition. The chemical substance which reacts with the enzyme in place of the substrate, but does not get converted to products, thus inhibiting the enzyme's action, is called an inhibitor. Inhibitors may block or damage the active site temporarily or permanently. Enzyme activity may also be temporarily inhibited by accumulated products within the cell, to regulate the rate of reaction. External factors responsible for enzyme inhibition include poisons, cyanide, antibiotics, some drugs, or accumulated products. Enzyme inhibition may be of two major types:

## Competitive Inhibition

If enzymes are inhibited due to the presence of molecules which occupy the active site because of their similarity in structure and shape to the substrate, this is called competitive inhibition. Such inhibitors are neither converted to products, nor do they allow the actual substrate to bind, so the reaction does not proceed. Competitive inhibition is temporary and can be reversed if the concentration of the actual substrate is increased above the concentration of inhibitor molecules.

## Non-competitive Inhibition

The structure and shape of these inhibitors do not resemble the substrate molecules, so they do not occupy the active site. Instead, they may attach to the enzyme surface at a point other than the active site, called the allosteric site. They alter the globular shape of the enzyme and damage the active site, so the actual substrate is unable to bind and the reaction stops.

# 7.6 Role of ATP - the Energy Currency of Cell

## ATP: The Energy Currency of the Cell

Life of living organisms is possible only if a constant supply of energy is available in its cells. All life processes like movement, development, reproduction, thermoregulation, and active transport depend on the availability of energy. Living organisms use and store energy at the cellular level in the form of Adenosine triphosphate (ATP). ATP is commonly called the "energy currency" of the cell because it is an energy-rich compound that can store and release energy, much like money. When organic molecules (glucose, amino acids etc.) are broken down in the cells, energy is released that can be captured and stored in ATP molecules. This energy remains stored in ATP until it is needed; when energy is needed by the cell, the ATP molecule is hydrolysed to release it.

> Science Titbits: Human cells normally depend upon the hydrolysis of 100 to 150 moles of ATP per day to ensure proper functioning. ATP is synthesized by catabolic reactions, e.g. complete oxidation of one glucose molecule during cellular respiration generates a net 36 ATP molecules. Plants can also use light energy to produce chemical energy as ATP.

## ATP-ADP Cycle

ATP can be converted to ADP and inorganic phosphate by hydrolysis. The third phosphate group separates from ATP and remains in the cell in inorganic form. ADP and phosphate can be converted back to ATP by condensation. The ability of ATP to store and release energy is due to its unique structure.

<CaptionedImage src="kg24g11wjmr1hnx67mxzfajat58dnr0b" alt="Diagram of the ATP-ADP cycle showing hydrolysis releasing energy and condensation storing it." caption="Fig. 7.7 ATP- ADP cycle" />
## Structure of the ATP Molecule

An ATP molecule consists of three components:

1. Adenine (a double-ringed nitrogen base)
2. Ribose (a five-carbon sugar)
3. Three phosphate groups (PO4), linked in a chain called a triphosphate group

The adenine nitrogen base binds to the ribose sugar to become adenosine.

**Adenine + Ribose Sugar → Adenosine**

The first phosphate group binds with adenosine to form adenosine monophosphate (AMP). The second phosphate group binds with AMP to form adenosine diphosphate (ADP). The third phosphate group binds with ADP to form adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Thus all three phosphate groups are serially bonded in a linear chain with adenosine to form ATP.

<CaptionedImage src="kg21z5g0vp1s3tmnyn26rcks3n88w42y" alt="Diagram showing the structure of ATP: adenine, ribose sugar, and three phosphate groups." caption="Fig. 7.10: Structure of ATP." />

**High Energy Bonds:**

The covalent bonds between the phosphate groups are called "high energy" or "energy-rich" bonds. The two bonds linking the phosphate groups together are represented by a specific symbol of wavy lines. The energy stored in these bonds may be used by cells to carry out their functions.

# 7.7 Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is an important biochemical process taking place in plants, algae and cyanobacteria. During this process, plants absorb light energy in the presence of chlorophyll and use carbon dioxide and water to make glucose. Oxygen is produced as a by-product of photosynthesis.

<CaptionedImage src="kg2dvytyh94jh6gca0xhdeqdx188x1ar" alt="Summary equation of photosynthesis showing carbon dioxide and water converting to glucose and oxygen using light energy." caption="Fig. 7.11: Summary equation of photosynthesis." />

Nearly all life forms depend on photosynthesis for food, directly or indirectly. Photosynthesis involves a series of coordinated reactions.

## Light Dependent Reactions of Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis occurs in two phases, called light dependent and light independent reactions. In the light dependent reactions, light energy is absorbed and used to produce the energy-rich molecules ATP and NADPH. These reactions take place in chloroplasts, only in the presence of light.

<CaptionedImage src="kg252swfe85b7z5204yq72p6618cr0ef" alt="Diagram of the light dependent reactions of photosynthesis." caption="Fig. 7.13: Light dependent reactions overview." />

These reactions take place in thylakoid membranes, where photosynthetic pigments are arranged into clusters called photosystems. There are two types of photosystems: Photosystem I and Photosystem II. The process starts from Photosystem II.

<CaptionedImage src="kg27vxvr3qwx91qgae0k3p2dtd8dm4x6" alt="Diagram of Photosystem I and Photosystem II in the thylakoid membrane." caption="Fig. 7.10: Light dependent reactions of photosynthesis" />

1. Chlorophyll a of Photosystem II absorbs light and a pair of electrons is emitted from it, accepted by a molecule called the primary electron acceptor.
2. At the same time, a water molecule splits into an oxygen atom, two hydrogen ions (H+), and two electrons. This splitting compensates for the electron loss of light-affected chlorophyll in Photosystem II, and is called photolysis of water.
3. Oxygen is released out of the leaf through the stomata.
4. The electron pair from the primary electron acceptor passes through a chain of molecules called the electron transport chain (ETC). Electrons passing through the ETC gradually release energy, which is used for ATP synthesis.
5. Light also acts on Photosystem I, which gives out an electron pair. These electrons and the two H+ from water reduce NADP+ to NADPH.
## Light Independent Reactions (Calvin Cycle)

Once the light reactions produce ATP and NADPH, a photosynthetic cell can fix carbon dioxide to synthesize sugar molecules. These reactions do not depend directly on light, so they are also called dark reactions or light independent reactions. They take place in the stroma of the chloroplast.

<CaptionedImage src="kg2dwbvrryg3wmmwe4n1w316r58dn3jn" alt="Diagram of the Calvin cycle showing carbon fixation." caption="Fig. 7.11: Calvin cycle" />

The sequence of reactions in this phase was first described by Melvin Calvin and his colleagues, so it is also known as the Calvin cycle.

1. Carbon dioxide combines with an already-existing 5-carbon molecule (RuBP) to form a 6-carbon intermediate molecule.
2. This 6-carbon molecule is unstable and splits into two 3-carbon molecules (3PGA).
3. These 3-carbon molecules are reduced to a 3-carbon sugar molecule (G3P) by NADPH, using the energy of ATP.
4. These 3-carbon sugar molecules are used to synthesize glucose, and also to regenerate the original 5-carbon molecule for the next Calvin cycle.
## Limiting Factors of Photosynthesis

The rate of photosynthesis can be affected by the following environmental factors, called limiting factors of photosynthesis.

1. Greater light intensity and duration increase the process of photosynthesis.
2. Carbon dioxide is one of the raw materials (substrates) for the photosynthetic reaction; a higher CO2 concentration increases the rate of photosynthesis, but only up to a specific point.
3. The rate of photosynthesis is highest at 25°C, called the optimum temperature for photosynthesis. Temperature below or above the optimum affects the rate of photosynthesis.

# 7.8 Respiration

What happens when you burn a fuel like petrol? Energy is released in the form of light and heat. The same sort of thing happens in our body. Our fuel is glucose from our food. The process by which energy is produced from food is called respiration. Respiration is a chemical process which takes place in cells.

## Glycolysis and Anaerobic Respiration

The two main types of respiration are anaerobic respiration and aerobic respiration. The first phase of respiration, called glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm and is common to both anaerobic and aerobic respiration. During glycolysis, glucose is oxidized into two molecules of pyruvic acid, with a yield of two ATP molecules.

**Glucose → 2 Pyruvic Acid + 2 ATP**

**Anaerobic Respiration**

Anaerobic respiration means respiration in the absence of oxygen. During anaerobic respiration, glucose is not completely oxidized to carbon dioxide and water, but is instead converted into carbon dioxide and alcohol, or lactic acid. This process is also called fermentation.

*Alcoholic fermentation:* Pyruvic acid produced during glycolysis is converted into ethyl alcohol and carbon dioxide. It occurs in yeast and some bacteria.

> Pyruvic Acid → Ethyl Alcohol + Carbon dioxide

*Lactic acid fermentation:* During tough and continuous exercise, our heart and lungs cannot provide enough oxygen to skeletal muscles to meet increased demand. During such vigorous exercise or labour, muscle cells carry out anaerobic respiration, and each pyruvic acid molecule is converted into lactic acid. Bacteria that convert milk to yogurt also produce lactic acid.

> Pyruvic Acid → Lactic Acid

**Importance of Anaerobic Respiration**

Anaerobic respiration is important for living organisms in the following ways:

1. The earliest living organisms would have had to produce energy by anaerobic respiration, because early Earth had no free oxygen in its atmosphere.
2. Anaerobic respiration by bacteria is used in cheese and yogurt making.
3. Fermentation by yeast is used in the wine-making and baking industries.
4. Anaerobic respiration, in the form of lactic acid fermentation, provides some energy to muscle cells during running, labour, or strenuous exercise.

## Aerobic Respiration

Aerobic respiration means respiration in the presence and use of oxygen. During aerobic respiration, a glucose molecule is completely broken down in the presence of oxygen, producing a comparatively large amount of energy along with carbon dioxide and water. Aerobic respiration can be summed up by the following equation.

<CaptionedImage src="kg25hed5aqc6vzsxsnsws6zv0188xb2a" alt="Summary equation of aerobic respiration: glucose plus oxygen yields carbon dioxide, water, and energy." caption="Fig. 7.16: Aerobic respiration summary equation." />

## Aerobic Respiration Step 1: Glycolysis

The breakdown of glucose does not take place in a single step but in a series of chemical reactions. Energy released from the breakdown of glucose is stored as chemical energy in ATP. The complete process of aerobic respiration is divided into four main phases: glycolysis, formation of acetyl CoA, the Krebs cycle, and the electron transport chain.

Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm of the cell, outside the mitochondria. It occurs in both anaerobic and aerobic respiration. A single molecule of glucose is broken down into two molecules of pyruvic acid (3 carbons each). Two ATP molecules are produced as a net energy gain, and two NAD+ molecules are reduced to NADH.

$$\text{Glucose} \rightarrow 2 \text{ Pyruvic Acid} + 2 \text{ ATP} + 2 \text{ NADH}$$

## Aerobic Respiration Step 2: Formation of Acetyl CoA

Each pyruvic acid molecule is oxidized to a two-carbon acetyl group, which combines with coenzyme A to form acetyl CoA. Carbon dioxide is removed and NADH is produced. The acetyl CoA enters the mitochondrion, where it takes part in the Krebs cycle.

$$2 \text{ Pyruvic Acid} + 2 \text{ CoA} \rightarrow 2 \text{ Acetyl CoA} + 2 \text{ CO}_2 + 2 \text{ NADH}$$

## Aerobic Respiration Step 3: Krebs Cycle

The Krebs cycle is a cyclic process that takes place in the mitochondrial matrix. Coenzyme A is released and the acetyl group passes through a series of reactions. The products of this process are CO2, NADH, and FADH2. Some energy is released to produce ATP directly, while more energy is produced by oxidation of NADH and FADH2 in the electron transport chain.

$$2 \text{ Acetyl CoA} \rightarrow 4 \text{ CO}_2 + 2 \text{ ATP} + 6 \text{ NADH} + 2 \text{ FADH}_2$$

## Aerobic Respiration Step 4: Electron Transport Chain (ETC)

The electron transport chain is a series of electron carrier molecules located in the inner membrane of the mitochondria. Electrons removed by oxidation of glucose during glycolysis and the Krebs cycle are carried to the ETC by NADH and FADH2. When electrons from NADH pass through the ETC, they provide enough energy to synthesize three ATP molecules; each FADH2, however, produces only two ATP molecules. The final electron acceptor is an oxygen atom, which combines with hydrogen ions to produce water.

<CaptionedImage src="kg2c1s46z4sry3r50kt3pbnrpd8dn3v4" alt="Diagram of the electron transport chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane." caption="Fig. 7.12: An overview of aerobic respiration" />
## How the Body Uses ATP from Respiration

The energy produced as ATP by cellular respiration is used in many ways, e.g. the working of muscles, nerve impulse transmission, growth and repair of cells, active transport, and glandular activity. In the human body, the brain uses the maximum energy in the form of ATP, i.e. 25% of ATP.
