# The Distance Formula

## Applying the Distance Formula

Given the two points $P_{1}\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)$ and $P_{2}\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)$, the distance between these points is given by the formula:

$$
\mathrm{d}\left(\mathrm{P}_{1}, \mathrm{P}_{2}\right)=\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2}=\sqrt{\left(x_{2}-x_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(y_{2}-y_{1}\right)^{2}}
$$

Proof: If $x_{1} \neq x_{2}$ and $y_{1} \neq y_{2}$, then plot two points $\mathrm{P}_{1}$ and $\mathrm{P}_{2}$ with coordinates $\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)$ and $\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)$ respectively as

Key Fact illustrated in figure (ii).
<CaptionedImage src="kg20n966gdbar10nj1t7as0jts8efbqk" alt="A coordinate-plane diagram showing two points P1(x1,y1) and P2(x2,y2) with their projections onto the axes, used to derive the distance formula." caption="A coordinate-plane diagram showing two points P1(x1,y1) and P2(x2,y2) with their projections onto the axes, used to derive the distance formula." />

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2}=\mathrm{d}\left(\mathrm{P}_{1}, \mathrm{P}_{2}\right) \\
& \mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2}^{2}=\left(\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2}\right)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the above figure $\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{~A}$ and $\mathrm{P}_{2} \mathrm{~B}$ are perpendiculars drawn from two points $\mathrm{P}_{1}\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)$ and $\mathrm{P}_{2}\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)$ on $x$-axis, and $\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{P}_{2} \mathrm{D}$ are perpendiculars on $y$-axis.
The coordinates of the points $\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{C}$ and D are also shown in figure accordingly.
Let the point where $\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{C}$ meets $\mathrm{P}_{2} \mathrm{~B}$ be $\mathrm{P}_{3}$, with coordinate $\left(x_{2}, y_{1}\right)$ so that $\Delta \mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2} \mathrm{P}_{3}$ is a right triangle with right angle at $\mathrm{P}_{3}$.
From figure, we have

$$
\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{~A}, \mathrm{~B})=\mathrm{AB}=\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{3}=\left|x_{2}-x_{1}\right|
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{C}, \mathrm{D})=\mathrm{CD}=\mathrm{d}\left(\mathrm{P}_{2}, \mathrm{P}_{3}\right)=\mathrm{P}_{2} \mathrm{P}_{3}=\left|y_{2}-y_{1}\right|
$$

We next use the Pythagoras Theorem in right triangle $\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2} \mathrm{P}_{3}$

$$
\left[d\left(P_{1}, P_{2}\right)\right]^{2}=\left[d\left(P_{1}, P_{3}\right)\right]^{2}+\left[d\left(P_{2}, P_{3}\right)\right]^{2}
$$

or

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{3}^{2}+\mathrm{P}_{2} \mathrm{P}_{3}^{2} \\
& \mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2}^{2}=\left|x_{2}-x_{1}\right|^{2}+\left|y_{2}-y_{1}\right|^{2} \\
& \mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2}=\sqrt{\left(x_{2}-x_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(y_{2}-y_{1}\right)^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\mathrm{d}\left(\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2}\right)=\mathrm{P}_{1} \mathrm{P}_{2}=\sqrt{\left(x_{2}-x_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(y_{2}-y_{1}\right)^{2}}=\sqrt{\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)^{2}+\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)^{2}}$

Example 2:
Find the distance between the two points $\mathrm{A}(-1,3)$ and $\mathrm{B}(4,15)$.
Solution:
By distance formula distance between two points with coordinates $\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)$ and $\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)$ is

$$
d=\sqrt{\left(x_{2}-x_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(y_{2}-y_{1}\right)^{2}}
$$

Let ( $-1,3$ ) be denoted by ( $x_{1}, y_{1}$ ) i.e. $x_{1}=-1, y_{1}=3$ and (4,15) be denoted by ( $x_{2}, y_{2}$ )
i.e. $x_{2}=4, y_{2}=15$
Now put them into the distance formula

$$
\begin{aligned}
A B=d & =\sqrt{(4-(-1))^{2}+(15-3)^{2}} \\
d & =\sqrt{(4+1)^{2}+(12)^{2}} \\
d & =\sqrt{5^{2}+12^{2}} \\
d & =\sqrt{25+144} \\
d & =\sqrt{169} \\
d & =13
\end{aligned}
$$

Collinear and Non-Collinear Point
A set of points that lie on a same straight line are said to be ccollinear.
If three points $\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}$ and C are collinear then, by distance formula, we have:

$$
d(A, C)=d(A, B)+d(B, C)
$$

or

$$
A C=A B+B C
$$

When three or more points do not lie on the same straight line, they are said to be non-collinear points.

Example 3:
Prove that A (3, 2), B (4, 4) and C (5, 6) are collinear points.
Solution:
In first step we find the distance between them.

By Distance Formula, distance between two points $\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)$ and $\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)$ is

$$
d=\sqrt{\left(x_{2}-x_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(y_{2}-y_{1}\right)^{2}}
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
d(A, B)= & A B=\sqrt{(4-3)^{2}+(4-2)^{2}} \\
& A B=\sqrt{1^{2}+2^{2}}=\sqrt{1+4} \\
& A B=\sqrt{5} \ldots \ldots \ldots . \tag{i}
\end{align*}
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{~B}, \mathrm{C})=\mathrm{BC} & =\sqrt{(5-4)^{2}+(6-4)^{2}} \\
\mathrm{BC} & =\sqrt{1^{2}+2^{2}}=\sqrt{1+4} \\
\mathrm{BC} & =\sqrt{5} \ldots \ldots \ldots(\text { ii }) \\
\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{~A}, \mathrm{C})=\mathrm{AC} & =\sqrt{(5-3)^{2}+(6-2)^{2}} \\
\mathrm{AC} & =\sqrt{2^{2}+4^{2}} \\
& =\sqrt{4+16}=\sqrt{20} \\
& =2 \sqrt{5} \ldots \ldots .(\text { iii }) \tag{iii}
\end{align*}
$$

<CaptionedImage src="kg2f1e11esxv74csac3cqa2mvd8eetc4" alt="A coordinate-plane diagram showing three points A(3,2), B(4,4), and C(5,6) plotted and connected, used in a distance-formula example." caption="A coordinate-plane diagram showing three points A(3,2), B(4,4), and C(5,6) plotted and connected, used in a distance-formula example." />
From (i), (ii) and (iii), we find that

$$
\begin{aligned}
& A C=A B+B C \\
& 2 \sqrt{5}=\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{5} \\
& 2 \sqrt{5}=2 \sqrt{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, the given points are collinear.
Example 4:
Prove that the points $(3,4),(3,1)$ and $(8,4)$ are the vertices of a right triangle.
Solution:
Suppose $\mathrm{A}(3,4), \mathrm{B}(3,1)$ and $\mathrm{C}(8,4)$ are the vertices of a triangle.
In first step we find the distance between them.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{~A}, \mathrm{~B})=\mathrm{AB} & =\sqrt{(1-4)^{2}+(3-3)^{2}} \\
& =\sqrt{(-3)^{2}+0^{2}} \\
\mathrm{AB} & =\sqrt{9+0} \\
\mathrm{AB} & =3
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{~B}, \mathrm{C})=\mathrm{BC} & =\sqrt{(4-1)^{2}+(8-3)^{2}} \\
& =\sqrt{3^{2}+5^{2}} \\
& =\sqrt{9+25} \\
\mathrm{BC} & =\sqrt{34} \\
\mathrm{~d}(\mathrm{~A}, \mathrm{C})=\mathrm{AC} & =\sqrt{(8-3)^{2}+(4-4)^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

<CaptionedImage src="kg2d2zq9xe7rbzfg6ntxtkxvn18bt2qd" alt="" caption="" />

$$
\mathrm{AC}=\sqrt{25+0}=5
$$

In right triangle we know that the greatest length is always the hypotenuse. So, BC is the hypotenuse. By Pythagoras Theorem,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathrm{BC}^{2}=\mathrm{AB}^{2}+\mathrm{AC}^{2} \\
&(\sqrt{34})^{2}=5^{2}+3^{2} \\
& 34=25+9 \\
& 34=34
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence; $\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}$ and C form a right triangle.

Mid-Point of Two given Points in a Coordinate Plane
Suppose, we have two points $(2,4)$ and $(6,10)$ and we want to find the distance between them. First, we plot the points on the graph and join them. Then we can draw the lines that form a right triangle by using these points as two of the corners.

In the diagram, $\Delta \mathrm{ABC}$ is drawn by drawing AC parallel to the $x$-axis and BC parallel to the $y$-axis. The coordinates of C are $(6,4)$, where two lines AC and BC intersect.
Let $\mathrm{P}(x, y)$ be the mid point of AB . Draw PD parallel to the $y$-axis and PE parallel to the $x$-axis. Then the coordinate of D and E are $(x, 4)$ and $(6, y)$ and, $\triangle \mathrm{APD}$ and $\triangle \mathrm{PBE}$ are congruent by (ASA).

We have $\mathrm{AD}=\mathrm{PE} \quad, \quad \mathrm{PD}=\mathrm{BE}$

$$
\begin{array}{rlrl}
x-2 & =6-x, & & y-4 \\
2 x & =8, & & 10-y \\
x & =4, & & 2 y=14 \\
& =7
\end{array}
$$

The mid point of $A B$ is $P(4,7)$.
We can also find the mid point of $A(2,4)$ and $B(6,10)$ in this way

# Mid-Point Formula

## Finding Midpoints in a Coordinate Plane

In the above figure (iii) if we take $\mathrm{A}\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)$ instead of $\mathrm{A}(2,4)$ and $\mathrm{B}\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)$ instead of $B(6,10)$, then we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& x-2=x-x_{1} \text { and } \quad 6-x=x_{2}-x \\
& y-4=y-y_{1} \text { and } \quad 10-y=y_{2}-y
\end{aligned}
$$

We have already proved that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{AD} & =\mathrm{PE} & , & \mathrm{PD}
\end{aligned}=\mathrm{BE} ~ g-x=y_{1}=y_{2}-y ~ 2 y=y_{1}+y_{2} . \quad y=\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}
$$

To apply the mid point formula remember that: the $x$-coordinate of the midpoint = the average of the $x$-coordinates and the $y$-coordinate of the midpoint = the average of the $y$-coordinates.

The mid-point $P$ of the line segment from $P_{1}\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)$ to $P_{2}\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)$ is

$$
\mathrm{P}\left(\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}, \frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\right) .
$$

Example 6:
If $(1,4)$ is the midpoint of the line segment joining $(6, b)$ and $(a, 2)$. Find $a$ and $b$.
Solution:
By mid point formula, mid point of the line segment with end points $\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)$ and $\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)$ is

$$
\left(\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}, \frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\right),
$$

So, mid point of the line segment joining the end points $(6, b)$ and $(a, 2)$ is

$$
\left(\frac{6+a}{2}, \frac{b+2}{2}\right)
$$

By using the given condition, we have

$$
\left(\frac{6+a}{2}, \frac{b+2}{2}\right)=(1,4)
$$

By the definition of equal ordered pairs,

$$
\begin{array}{llrl}
\frac{6+a}{2} & =1 & \text { and } & \\
6+a=2 & \text { and } & b+2 & b+2=8 \\
a=2-6 & \text { and } & b=8-2 \\
a=-4 & \text { and } & b=6 \\
a=-4 & \text { and } & b=6
\end{array}
$$
