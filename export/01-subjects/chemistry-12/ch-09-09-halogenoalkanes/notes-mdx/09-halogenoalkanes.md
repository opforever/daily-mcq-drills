# 9.1 Preparation of Halogenoarenes

Halogenoarenes are aromatic compounds in which one or more hydrogen atoms on the benzene ring are replaced by a halogen atom (F, Cl, Br, or I). Unlike halogenoalkanes, the C–X bond in halogenoarenes has partial double-bond character due to resonance with the ring, making direct nucleophilic substitution very difficult.

---

## Method 1: Direct Halogenation (Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution)

Benzene reacts with chlorine or bromine **in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst** (halogen carrier) to give chlorobenzene or bromobenzene.

### Reaction with Chlorine

$\text{C}_6\text{H}_6 + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{FeCl}_3 \text{ or AlCl}_3} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$

### Reaction with Bromine

$\text{C}_6\text{H}_6 + \text{Br}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{FeBr}_3 \text{ or AlBr}_3} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Br} + \text{HBr}$

### Role of the Lewis Acid Catalyst

The Lewis acid (e.g., $\text{FeCl}_3$) acts as a **halogen carrier**. It polarises the halogen molecule to generate a powerful electrophile:

$\text{Cl}_2 + \text{FeCl}_3 \rightarrow \text{Cl}^+ \cdots [\text{FeCl}_4]^-$

The $\text{Cl}^+$ (or the highly polarised $\text{Cl}^{\delta+}$–$\text{Cl}^{\delta-}$) then attacks the electron-rich benzene ring via the standard **electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS)** mechanism.

> **Note:** Direct halogenation without a catalyst does **not** work for benzene because the $\pi$ electrons of the ring are not nucleophilic enough to attack an unpolarised halogen molecule.

---

## Method 2: Why Phenol Cannot Be Used to Make Chlorobenzene

Unlike alcohols, phenol **cannot** be converted to chlorobenzene by reaction with HCl or PCl$_5$ under normal conditions. This is because:

- The lone pair on the oxygen atom of phenol overlaps with the $\pi$ system of the ring.
- This gives the C–O bond **partial double-bond character** through resonance.
- The C–O bond is therefore much **stronger and shorter** than in aliphatic alcohols and cannot be broken easily.

---

## Method 3: The Sandmeyer Reaction (via Diazonium Salts)

This is the most versatile method for preparing halogenoarenes, especially **fluorobenzene** and **iodobenzene** which cannot be made by direct halogenation.

### Step 1: Diazotisation of Aniline

Aniline (phenylamine) is converted to benzene diazonium chloride by reaction with sodium nitrite ($\text{NaNO}_2$) and hydrochloric acid at **below 10°C**:

$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 + \text{NaNO}_2 + 2\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{0\text{–}5°\text{C}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$

> The temperature must be kept **below 10°C** because diazonium salts are unstable and decompose to phenol and $\text{N}_2$ at higher temperatures.

### Step 2: Replacement of the Diazonium Group

| Target Product | Reagent | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Chlorobenzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl}$) | $\text{CuCl}$ / $\text{HCl}$ | Warm |
| Bromobenzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Br}$) | $\text{CuBr}$ / $\text{HBr}$ | Warm |
| Iodobenzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{I}$) | $\text{KI}$ (aq) | Warm, **no Cu catalyst needed** |
| Fluorobenzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{F}$) | $\text{HBF}_4$ then heat | Balz–Schiemann reaction |

### Sandmeyer Reaction (Cl and Br)

$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{CuCl} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} + \text{N}_2$

The cuprous salt ($\text{Cu}^+$) acts as a catalyst by facilitating the transfer of the halide to the aryl group.

### Iodobenzene Preparation

$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{KI} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{I} + \text{N}_2 + \text{KCl}$

No copper catalyst is required because $\text{I}^-$ is a good enough reducing agent to reduce the diazonium ion directly.

---

| Method | Halogen Introduced | Catalyst/Reagent |

| Sandmeyer reaction | Cl, Br | $\text{CuCl}$ or $\text{CuBr}$ |

# Reactivity of Halogenoalkanes

The reactivity of halogenoalkanes (also known as alkyl halides) and aryl halides is primarily determined by the strength and polarity of the carbon-halogen (C-X) bond.

### C-X Bond Polarity

Halogens are more electronegative than carbon. This difference in electronegativity causes the C-X bond to be polarized. The carbon atom attached to the halogen acquires a partial positive charge ($\delta+$), making it electrophilic, while the halogen atom acquires a partial negative charge ($\delta-$), making it nucleophilic.

$ C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-} $

This polarization makes the carbon atom susceptible to attack by nucleophiles. For example, chloroethane can undergo a nucleophilic substitution reaction where the chlorine atom is replaced by another nucleophile ($Nu^-$):

$ \mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-Cl} + \mathrm{Nu^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-Nu} + \mathrm{Cl^-} $

### Comparison: Alkyl Halides vs. Aryl Halides

The reactivity differs significantly between alkyl halides and aryl halides (e.g., chlorobenzene).

- **Alkyl Halides:** The C-X bond is polarized, and the halogen can be readily replaced by a nucleophile via nucleophilic substitution.
- **Aryl Halides (e.g., Chlorobenzene):**
  - The lone pair of electrons on the chlorine atom is delocalized over the benzene ring through resonance.
  - This delocalization gives the C-Cl bond a partial double bond character, making it stronger and more difficult to break compared to an alkyl C-Cl bond.
  - Consequently, the chlorine atom in chlorobenzene cannot be easily replaced by a nucleophile, meaning it does **not** readily undergo nucleophilic substitution.
  - Instead, the chlorine atom increases the electron density of the benzene ring due to resonance, directing incoming electrophiles to the *ortho* and *para* positions. Thus, chlorobenzene primarily undergoes **electrophilic substitution reactions**.

For instance, nitration of chlorobenzene yields a mixture of *o*-nitrochlorobenzene and *p*-nitrochlorobenzene:

## Reactions of Halogenoalkanes or Alkyl Halides

As discussed, the polar character of the C-X bond, known as the **inductive effect**, is responsible for the characteristic chemical reactions of alkyl halides.

$ C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-} $

Alkyl halides primarily undergo two types of reactions:
1. **Nucleophilic substitution reactions ($S_N$ Reactions)**
2. **Elimination reactions**

### Nucleophilic Substitution ($S_N$) Reactions of Alkyl Halides

A nucleophilic substitution reaction is one where a halogen atom of an alkyl halide is substituted or replaced by a strong nucleophile. These reactions involve three key components: the **Substrate**, the **Nucleophile**, and the **Leaving Group**.

- **Substrate Molecule:** This is the alkyl halide molecule upon which the nucleophile attacks.
- **Nucleophile ($Nu^-$ or $Nu$):** A species possessing a lone pair of electrons, which it donates to the electrophilic carbon of the alkyl halide. Nucleophiles can be neutral or negatively charged.

**Examples of Nucleophiles**

| Nucleophile | Name |
| :--- | :--- |
| $HO^-$ | Hydroxide ion |
| $C_2H_5O^-$ | Ethoxide ion |
| $HS^-$ | Hydrogen sulfide ion |
| $SCN^-$ | Thiocyanate ion |
| $NH_2^-$ | Amino group |
| $Cl^-$ | Chloride ion |
| $Br^-$ | Bromide ion |
| $NH_3$ | Ammonia |
| $H_2O$ | Water |

- **Leaving Group (LG):** The halogen atom of the alkyl halide is the leaving group. It departs with an unshared pair of electrons and acts as a nucleophile itself. For a successful substitution, the incoming nucleophile must generally be stronger than the departing leaving group.
  - **Good leaving groups:** $Cl^-$, $Br^-$, $I^-$, $HSO_4^-$
  - **Poor leaving groups:** $OH^-$, $OR^-$, $NH_2^-$
  - *Note:* Iodide ion ($I^-$) is both a good nucleophile and a good leaving group.

There are two fundamental events in a nucleophilic substitution reaction:
1. Breaking of the $\sigma$ bond between carbon and halogen.
2. Formation of a new $\sigma$ bond between carbon and the attacking nucleophile.

The specific mechanism of substitution is significantly influenced by the nature of the attacking nucleophile, the substrate (alkyl halide), and the leaving group. This leads to two distinct mechanisms: $S_N1$ and $S_N2$.

### Carbocations and their Stability

A **carbocation** is a carbon atom that is attached to three atoms or groups and possesses a unit positive charge. Carbocations are often intermediates in $S_N1$ reactions.

The general stability order of simple alkyl carbocations is:

This order is observed because alkyl groups are weak electron-donating groups due to **hyperconjugation** and **inductive effects**. The positive charge on the carbon is delocalized and stabilized by the electron donation from the surrounding alkyl groups. Therefore, the stability of a carbocation increases with an increase in the number of alkyl groups attached to the positively charged carbon.

### Reaction with Aqueous Silver Nitrate

When a haloalkane (alkyl halide) reacts with aqueous silver nitrate ($AgNO_3$), the silver ion ($Ag^+$) in the solution undergoes an exchange reaction with the halide ion ($X^-$) from the haloalkane. This results in the formation of a silver halide precipitate ($AgX$).

$ \mathrm{R-X} + \mathrm{AgNO_3} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R-NO_3} + \mathrm{AgX} \downarrow $

Where X represents a halogen: Cl, Br, or I.

*Note:* Alkyl fluorides (R-F) do not react readily with $AgNO_3$ because the C-F bond is exceptionally strong and the fluoride ion is not easily displaced.

The color of the silver halide precipitate depends on the specific halogen present:
- **AgCl:** White precipitate, soluble in $NH_4OH$ (aqueous ammonia) solution.
- **AgBr:** Cream-colored precipitate, partially soluble in $NH_4OH$ solution.
- **AgI:** Yellow precipitate, insoluble in $NH_4OH$ solution.

# Nucleophilic Substitution (S$_N$) Reactions of Alkyl Halides

## Definition of Nucleophilic Substitution Reaction

A **nucleophilic substitution reaction** (or **S$_N$ reaction**) is a reaction in which a halogen atom of an alkyl halide is substituted or replaced by a strong nucleophile.

The general representation of such reactions is:

$\text{R-X} + \text{Nu}^- \rightarrow \text{R-Nu} + \text{X}^-$

### Components of a Nucleophilic Substitution Reaction

#### 1. Substrate Molecule

The alkyl halide molecule (R-X) on which a nucleophile attacks is called the *substrate molecule*. The carbon atom directly bonded to the halogen is often referred to as the *electrophilic carbon* due to the polarity of the C-X bond.

#### 2. Nucleophile

A nucleophile is a species having a lone pair of electrons that it can donate to an electrophilic carbon of an alkyl halide. Nucleophiles can be neutral (e.g., H$_2$O, NH$_3$) or negatively charged (e.g., HO$^-$, Cl$^-$).

**Examples of Common Nucleophiles:**

| Name                 | Formula                       |
| :------------------- | :---------------------------- |
| Hydroxide ion        | HO$^-$                        |
| Ethoxide ion         | C$_2$H$_5$O$^-$               |
| Hydrogen sulphide ion | HS$^-$                      |
| Thiocyanate ion      | SCN$^-$                       |
| Water                | H$_2$O                        |
| Amino group          | NH$_2$^-$                    |
| Chloride ion         | Cl$^-$                        |
| Bromide ion          | Br$^-$                        |
| Ammonia              | NH$_3$                        |

#### 3. Leaving Group (LG)

The halogen atom (X) of the alkyl halide is called the *leaving group*. The leaving group departs with an unshared pair of electrons, essentially acting as a nucleophile itself. For a substitution reaction to proceed effectively, the incoming nucleophile must be stronger than the departing leaving group.

*   **Good Leaving Groups:** Cl$^-$, Br$^-$, I$^-$, HSO$_4$^-$.
*   **Poor Leaving Groups:** OH$^-$, OR, and NH$_2$^-$.
*   *Note:* The iodide ion (I$^-$) is both a good nucleophile and a good leaving group.

The nature of the leaving group influences the reactivity of alkyl halides in nucleophilic substitution reactions.

### Fundamental Events in Nucleophilic Substitution

There are two primary events that occur during a nucleophilic substitution reaction:

1.  **Breaking of the σ bond** between carbon and the halogen (leaving group).
2.  **Formation of a new σ bond** between carbon and the attacking nucleophile.

### Factors Influencing Reaction Mechanism

The specific pathway or mechanism of a nucleophilic substitution reaction is greatly influenced by:

*   The nature of the **attacking nucleophile**.
*   The nature of the **substrate** (alkyl halide).
*   The nature of the **leaving group**.

Depending on these factors, especially the *steric hindrance* around the electrophilic carbon, these reactions follow two different mechanisms: **S$_N$1** and **S$_N$2**.

*   In *primary* or *secondary* alkyl halides, the attack by the incoming nucleophile and displacement of the leaving group often occur in one concerted step (S$_N$2).
*   In *bulky tertiary* alkyl halides, the attack by the incoming nucleophile and displacement of the leaving group occur in two distinct steps (S$_N$1).

### Carbocations and their Stability

A **carbocation** is a carbon atom that is attached to three other atoms or groups of atoms and possesses a unit positive charge. Carbocations are important intermediates in many organic reactions, especially the S$_N$1 mechanism.

#### Stability Order of Carbocations

The general stability order of simple alkyl carbocations is:

$\text{tertiary} (3^\circ) > \text{secondary} (2^\circ) > \text{primary} (1^\circ) > \text{methyl}$

<CaptionedImage src="kg20s4c47q5qg809vfpetjsgq18dgydt" alt="Stability order of carbocations" caption="Fig. 9.1: Stability order of carbocations" />

This stability order is primarily due to:

*   **Hyperconjugation:** Alkyl groups can donate electron density to the electron-deficient carbon through hyperconjugation (overlap of sigma bonds with the empty p-orbital of the carbocation). More alkyl groups mean more hyperconjugative interactions and greater stability.
*   **Inductive Effect:** Alkyl groups are weak electron-donating groups. They can stabilize the positive charge on the carbon through a positive inductive effect (+I effect). An increase in alkyl groups leads to a greater positive inductive effect and thus increased stability.

### S$_N$1 Mechanism

The **S$_N$1 reaction** is a **unimolecular nucleophilic substitution reaction**. It is termed "unimolecular" because the rate-determining step involves only one molecule (the substrate).

It is a **two-step reaction mechanism**:

**Step 1: Ionization of the Substrate (Slow and Rate-Determining)**

The substrate alkyl halide (R-X) first ionizes reversibly to form a carbocation (R$^+$) and a halide ion (X$^-$). This step involves the breaking of the C-X bond and is the slowest step, hence it is the *rate-determining step* of the reaction.

$R-X \xrightleftharpoons[\text{Slow, Rate-determining}]{\text{Ionization}} R^+ + X^-$

This step shows the formation of a trigonal planar carbocation (R$^+$).

**Step 2: Nucleophilic Attack (Fast)**

The carbocation, being highly reactive and electron-deficient, is rapidly attacked by the nucleophile (Nu$^-$). Since the carbocation is planar, the nucleophile can attack from either face (top or bottom), leading to a racemic mixture if the carbon atom is chiral.

$R^+ + Nu^- \xrightarrow{\text{Fast}} R-Nu$

The overall reaction rate for an S$_N$1 reaction depends only on the concentration of the substrate:

$\text{Rate} = k[R-X]$

### S$_N$2 Mechanism

The **S$_N$2 reaction** is a **bimolecular nucleophilic substitution reaction**. It occurs in a single concerted step where bond breaking and bond making happen simultaneously.

**Characteristics:**
*   **Bimolecular:** The rate depends on both the substrate and the nucleophile concentration: $\text{Rate} = k[R-X][Nu^-]$.
*   **Stereochemistry:** It results in the complete inversion of configuration, known as **Walden Inversion**.
*   **Substrate Preference:** Primary alkyl halides are most reactive due to low steric hindrance.

### Possible Questions and Answers

**Q:** What is the defining characteristic of an S$_N$1 reaction regarding its rate-determining step?

**A:** The rate-determining step involves only the substrate molecule, making it a unimolecular reaction. It is the slow ionization of the alkyl halide to form a carbocation.

**Q:** Why are tertiary alkyl halides more reactive towards S$_N$1 reactions than primary alkyl halides?

**A:** Tertiary alkyl halides form more stable tertiary carbocations (3°) in the rate-determining step. Carbocation stability follows the order 3° > 2° > 1°, making the formation of a 3° carbocation energetically more favorable.

**Q:** Provide two examples of good leaving groups and two examples of poor leaving groups.

**A:** Good leaving groups: Cl$^-$, Br$^-$, I$^-$. Poor leaving groups: OH$^-$, NH$_2$^-$.

# 9.6 Alkyl Halides as Synthetic Intermediates

Alkyl halides, also known as haloalkanes, are highly versatile starting materials in organic chemistry. Their utility stems from the polarized carbon-halogen bond, which makes the carbon atom electrophilic and the halogen a good leaving group. This allows them to participate in a wide variety of reactions, particularly nucleophilic substitution and elimination reactions, to produce a diverse range of organic compounds.

### Nucleophilic Substitution Reaction of Alkyl Halides

In a nucleophilic substitution reaction involving a haloalkane, a nucleophile ($\mathrm{Nu}^-$) attacks the electrophilic carbon atom bonded to the halogen ($\mathrm{X}$). As the nucleophile forms a bond with the carbon, the carbon-halogen bond breaks, and the halogen atom departs as a halide ion ($\mathrm{X}^-$), which is a stable leaving group.

The general reaction can be represented as:

$\mathrm{R}-\mathrm{X} + \mathrm{Nu}^- \longrightarrow \mathrm{R}-\mathrm{Nu} + \mathrm{X}^-$

Here:

- $\mathrm{R}-\mathrm{X}$ represents the alkyl halide (substrate).
- $\mathrm{Nu}^-$ represents the nucleophile.
- $\mathrm{R}-\mathrm{Nu}$ represents the new organic product.
- $\mathrm{X}^-$ represents the halide leaving group.

### Retrosynthesis: Working Backwards

Retrosynthesis is a powerful strategy used in organic synthesis to design reaction pathways. Instead of starting from reagents and predicting products, retrosynthesis involves working backward from the desired product (target molecule) to identify suitable starting materials and intermediate steps. This approach helps in systematically breaking down a complex synthesis problem into simpler, manageable steps.

**Conceptual Breakdown for Retrosynthesis of Alkyl Halides**

When considering a product $\mathrm{R}-\mathrm{Nu}$ that could have been formed via nucleophilic substitution, the retrosynthetic analysis involves identifying the nucleophile ($\mathrm{Nu}^-$) that was added and the corresponding electrophilic carbon fragment ($\mathrm{R}^+$) from which it originated.

The general conceptual cleavage of an alkyl halide for analysis is:

$\mathrm{R}-\mathrm{X} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathrm{R}^+ \quad + \quad \mathrm{X}^-$

In retrosynthetic terminology, the idealized fragments $\mathrm{R}^+$ and $\mathrm{X}^-$ are called **synthons**. Since synthons often cannot be used directly as reagents, we use **synthetic equivalents** (actual chemical reagents).

**Retrosynthesis Example: Synthesis of Ethyl Alcohol**

Let's apply the retrosynthesis approach to prepare ethyl alcohol ($\mathrm{CH_3}-\mathrm{CH_2}-\mathrm{OH}$).

1.  **Identify the Target Molecule:** The desired product is $\mathrm{CH_3}-\mathrm{CH_2}-\mathrm{OH}$.
2.  **Retrosynthetic Step (Working Backwards from Product):**
    We imagine the formation of the $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{O}$ bond. This suggests that the $-\mathrm{OH}$ group came from a nucleophilic synthon ($\mathrm{OH}^-$), and the $\mathrm{CH_3}-\mathrm{CH_2}-$ fragment was the electrophilic synthon.
    $\mathrm{CH_3}-\mathrm{CH_2}-\mathrm{OH} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathrm{CH_3}-\mathrm{CH_2}^+ \quad + \quad \mathrm{OH}^-$
3.  **Identify the Starting Material Precursor:**
    To get the $\mathrm{CH_3}-\mathrm{CH_2}^+$ fragment (electrophilic synthon), we need a synthetic equivalent like an alkyl halide. A common choice is bromoethane:
    $\mathrm{CH_3}-\mathrm{CH_2}-\mathrm{Br}$

4.  **Forward Reaction (Verification):**
    Now, we write the forward reaction to confirm our retrosynthetic pathway:
    $\mathrm{CH_3}-\mathrm{CH_2}-\mathrm{Br} \quad + \quad \mathrm{OH}^- \quad \longrightarrow \quad \mathrm{CH_3}-\mathrm{CH_2}-\mathrm{OH} \quad + \quad \mathrm{Br}^-$

**Retrosynthesis Example: Synthesis of Methylamine**

To synthesize methylamine ($\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$) using a haloalkane, the retrosynthetic analysis is as follows:

-   **Retrosynthetic Step:** $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2} \Rightarrow \mathrm{CH_3}^+ + \mathrm{NH_2}^-$
-   **Starting Haloalkane:** Methyl halide, e.g., $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$ (chloromethane) or $\mathrm{CH_3Br}$ (bromomethane).
-   **Nucleophile:** Ammonia ($\mathrm{NH_3}$) is the synthetic equivalent for the $\mathrm{NH_2}^-$ synthon.
-   **Forward Reaction:** $\mathrm{CH_3Cl} + 2\mathrm{NH_3} \longrightarrow \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} + \mathrm{NH_4Cl}$
