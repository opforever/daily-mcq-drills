# 19.1 Main Components of Chromatography

Chromatography is a laboratory technique for the separation of a mixture. The mixture is dissolved in a fluid called the **mobile phase**, which carries it through a structure holding another material called the **stationary phase**. The various constituents of the mixture travel at different speeds, causing them to separate.

## 1. Stationary Phase

The **stationary phase** is the component of the chromatographic system that remains fixed in place. The mobile phase moves over it, and the separation occurs based on the differential interactions of the mixture's components with this phase.

- **Nature:** It can be a solid (e.g., silica, glass) or a liquid coated onto the surface of a solid support.
- **Function:** In column chromatography, the stationary phase is typically packed into a column. As the mobile phase flows through, components that interact more strongly with the stationary phase move more slowly, leading to separation.

<CaptionedImage src="kg296g7ct832n60ag365at06b58dh4tk" alt="Stationary and mobile phase" caption="Figure 19.2: Stationary and mobile phase in column chromatography." />

### Support
In many chromatographic techniques, the stationary phase is held by a **support**. This is an inert material that provides a surface for the stationary phase to adhere to without reacting with the solutes.

## 2. Mobile Phase

The **mobile phase** is the solvent (liquid or gas) that flows through the stationary phase, carrying the components of the mixture with it.

- **Liquid Chromatography (LC):** Uses a liquid mobile phase.
- **Gas Chromatography (GC):** Uses a gas mobile phase.
- **Function:** The mobile phase dissolves the sample and carries it through the column. The separation depends on how the components partition between the mobile and stationary phases. There is a dynamic equilibrium as components constantly move between the two phases.

For more on selecting appropriate mobile and stationary phases, see <InlineNoteTag label="Selection of Mobile and Stationary Phase" notePath="chemistry-12/194-selection-of-mobile-and-stationary-phase" />.

## 3. Retardation Factor ($R_f$) Value

The **Retardation Factor ($R_f$)** is a dimensionless quantity used in planar chromatography (like paper or thin-layer chromatography) to identify compounds. It represents the ratio of the distance traveled by a solute (compound) to the distance traveled by the solvent front.

- The $R_f$ value is characteristic of a specific compound under a given set of conditions (stationary phase, mobile phase, temperature).
- It is calculated using the following formula:

$
R_f = \frac{\text{Distance from baseline travelled by solute}}{\text{Distance from baseline travelled by solvent front}}
$

<CaptionedImage src="kg24fvbp9eqy1djst8yfap9nhs8dgym8" alt="Rf value calculation" caption="Figure 19.3: Calculation of Rf values for different spots." />

For detailed interpretation of $R_f$ values, refer to <InlineNoteTag label="Interpretation of Rf Value" notePath="chemistry-12/193-interpretation-of-mathrm-r-mathrm-f-value" />.

## 4. Solvent Front

The **solvent front** is the furthest point reached by the mobile phase (solvent) as it moves up the stationary phase (e.g., chromatography paper). This distance is used as the denominator in the $R_f$ calculation.

## 5. The Baseline

The **baseline** is the starting line where the initial sample mixture is applied (spotted) on the stationary phase. All distance measurements for calculating the $R_f$ value begin from this reference point.

<CaptionedImage src="kg2erndvzeefqt45wfn7f72cad8dggnv" alt="Paper chromatogram" caption="Figure 19.4: A developed paper chromatogram showing the baseline, spots, and solvent front." />

## Applications of Chromatography

- **Biochemical Research:** Used for the separation and identification of complex biological compounds like proteins, amino acids, and nucleic acids.
- **Petroleum Industry:** Employed to analyze complex mixtures of hydrocarbons in crude oil and refined products.
- **Pharmaceuticals:** Essential for quality control, drug discovery, and ensuring the purity of medicines.
- **Environmental Science:** Used to detect and quantify pollutants in air, water, and soil samples.

# 19.2 Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC)

## What is TLC?

**Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC)** is a simple, fast, and inexpensive chromatographic technique used to separate, identify, and analyse the components of a mixture. It is widely used in forensic chemistry, pharmaceutical analysis, and the identification of unknown materials.

In TLC, separation is based on the principle of **differential adsorption**, different components of a mixture adsorb onto the stationary phase to different extents and therefore travel different distances when carried by the mobile phase.

---

## Components of a TLC System

### Stationary Phase
The stationary phase is a **thin layer of adsorbent material** coated uniformly on a flat, rigid support (glass, aluminium, or plastic plate). The most common adsorbents are:

| Adsorbent | Formula | Polarity |
|---|---|---|
| Silica gel | $\text{SiO}_2$ | Polar |
| Alumina | $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ | Polar |

Because silica gel and alumina are **polar**, they strongly adsorb polar compounds and allow non-polar compounds to travel further up the plate.

### Mobile Phase (Eluent)
The mobile phase is a **liquid solvent or solvent mixture** that travels up the TLC plate by capillary action, carrying the sample components with it. The choice of mobile phase is critical:

- A **more polar solvent** will carry polar compounds further (higher $R_f$).
- A **less polar (non-polar) solvent** will carry non-polar compounds further on a polar stationary phase.

The polarity of the mobile phase must be matched to the nature of the compounds being separated to achieve good resolution.

---

## Procedure

1. **Prepare the plate**, draw a pencil baseline about 1 cm from the bottom.
2. **Spot the sample**, apply a small, concentrated spot of the sample solution onto the baseline using a capillary tube.
3. **Prepare the developing chamber**, pour a small volume of the chosen solvent into a beaker or jar. **The solvent level must be below the baseline** so that the spots are not submerged and dissolved away.
4. **Saturate the atmosphere**, place a lid on the chamber to saturate the air with solvent vapour. This prevents uneven evaporation from the plate and ensures consistent results.
5. **Develop the plate**, place the TLC plate in the chamber and allow the solvent to rise by capillary action.
6. **Remove and mark**, when the solvent front is near the top, remove the plate and immediately mark the solvent front with a pencil.
7. **Visualise the spots**, identify the positions of the separated components.

---

## Visualisation of Colourless Spots

Many compounds are colourless and invisible to the naked eye. Several methods are used to locate them:

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| **UV light** | Plates containing a fluorescent indicator glow under UV; spots appear as dark patches |
| **Iodine vapour** | Iodine adsorbs onto organic compounds, producing brown spots |
| **Ninhydrin spray** | Reacts with amino acids to give a characteristic **purple/blue** colour |
| **Potassium permanganate** | Oxidises organic compounds, producing yellow spots on a purple background |

---

## The Retention Factor ($R_f$)

The **$R_f$ value** (Retention Factor or Retardation Factor) is a characteristic value used to identify compounds under specific conditions:

$R_f = \frac{\text{Distance moved by substance}}{\text{Distance moved by solvent front}}$

**Key points:**
- $R_f$ is always $\leq 1$ because a substance can never travel further than the solvent front.
- A **high $R_f$** means the compound has low affinity for the stationary phase (travels far).
- A **low $R_f$** means the compound has high affinity for the stationary phase (travels a short distance).
- $R_f$ values are **dimensionless** (no units).

### Worked Example
In a TLC experiment, the solvent front moves **10 cm** and a sample spot moves **4 cm**.

$R_f = \frac{4}{10} = 0.4$

---

## Factors Affecting Movement on the TLC Plate

The speed at which a component moves up the plate depends on its **relative affinity** for:
- The **stationary phase** (adsorption): stronger adsorption → slower movement → lower $R_f$
- The **mobile phase** (solubility): greater solubility → faster movement → higher $R_f$

On a **polar stationary phase** (e.g., silica gel):
- **Non-polar compounds** → weak adsorption → high $R_f$
- **Polar compounds** → strong adsorption → low $R_f$

---

## Importance of Phase Selection

Selecting the correct stationary and mobile phases is critical for effective separation:

- If the mobile phase is **too polar**, all compounds will travel to the solvent front ($R_f \approx 1$) and will not be separated.
- If the mobile phase is **too non-polar**, all compounds will remain at the baseline ($R_f \approx 0$) and will not be separated.
- The ideal mobile phase gives $R_f$ values between **0.2 and 0.8** for good separation.

---

## Applications in Forensic Chemistry

TLC is particularly valuable in forensic science for:

1. **Drug identification**, identifying illegal substances in seized samples by comparing $R_f$ values with known standards.
2. **Ink and dye analysis**, separating pigments in inks to compare documents or fibres.
3. **Food adulteration**, detecting the presence of unauthorised additives.
4. **Analysis of unknown materials**, a quick preliminary screen before more advanced techniques (e.g., GC-MS) are applied.
5. **Amino acid analysis**, identifying amino acids in biological samples using ninhydrin as a locating agent.

Because many forensic samples are colourless, TLC combined with appropriate visualisation techniques (UV, ninhydrin, iodine) is especially powerful.

# 19.3 Interpretation of $R_f$ Value

## What is the $R_f$ Value?

The **$R_f$ value** (Retention Factor or Retardation Factor) is a numerical value used in chromatography to characterise how far a particular substance travels relative to the solvent front.

$R_f = \frac{\text{Distance moved by the substance from the baseline}}{\text{Distance moved by the solvent front from the baseline}}$

Because the solute can never travel further than the solvent front, the $R_f$ value is always:

$0 \leq R_f \leq 1$

---

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Baseline** | The pencil line drawn near the bottom of the TLC plate/paper where the sample spots are applied |
| **Solvent front** | The furthest point reached by the mobile phase (solvent) during the chromatography run |
| **Stationary phase** | The material that does not move (e.g. silica gel on a TLC plate, or paper) |
| **Mobile phase** | The solvent that moves through the stationary phase, carrying the components of the mixture |

---

## Worked Example

In a TLC experiment:
- Distance moved by solvent front from baseline = **10 cm**
- Distance moved by substance X from baseline = **4 cm**

$R_f = \frac{4}{10} = 0.4$

The $R_f$ value of substance X is **0.4** (dimensionless).

---

## Effect of Polarity on $R_f$ Value

In **normal-phase TLC** (polar stationary phase such as silica gel, non-polar mobile phase):

- **More polar solutes** → stronger attraction to the polar stationary phase → travel **less distance** → **lower $R_f$**
- **Less polar solutes** → weaker attraction to the stationary phase → travel **further** with the mobile phase → **higher $R_f$**

> **Rule of thumb:** In normal-phase TLC, polarity and $R_f$ value are inversely related.

---

## Factors Affecting $R_f$ Value

The $R_f$ value is **not an absolute constant**, it depends on experimental conditions:

1. **Nature of the stationary phase** (e.g. silica gel vs. alumina)
2. **Composition of the mobile phase** (solvent polarity)
3. **Temperature** of the system
4. **Purity of the solvent**
5. **Thickness and activity of the stationary phase layer**

> **Note:** The *length* of the plate does NOT affect the $R_f$ value, because it is a ratio of distances.

---

## Using $R_f$ Values for Identification

$R_f$ values can be used to **compare** an unknown substance with a known reference standard, but only when both are run under **identical conditions** (same solvent, same stationary phase, same temperature).

- If the unknown and the reference have the **same $R_f$ value** in one solvent system → possible match
- If they match across **multiple different solvent systems** → strong evidence they are the same compound
- $R_f$ values **cannot** be used for absolute identification on their own

# 19.4 Selection of Mobile and Stationary Phases in Chromatography

This section outlines the critical factors and characteristics to consider when choosing the stationary and mobile phases for a chromatographic separation to ensure efficiency, resolution, and accuracy.

### 19.4.1 Selection of Stationary Phase

In chromatography, the stationary phase is typically a solid adsorbent that remains fixed in the column or on a plate. Its primary role is to interact with the components of the sample mixture, causing them to separate based on differences in their affinity for the phase.

#### Characteristics of an Ideal Stationary Phase

An effective stationary phase should possess the following traits:

1. **High and Selective Adsorption Capacity:** It must be able to adsorb the sample components effectively and show different affinities for different components to enable separation.
2. **Large Surface Area:** The stationary phase should be finely divided to maximize the surface area available for interaction with the sample, leading to better separation efficiency.

3. **High Mechanical Stability:** It must be physically robust to withstand the pressure and flow of the mobile phase without breaking down and forming fine dust, which can clog the system.

4. **Chemical Inertness:** The stationary phase should not react chemically with the sample components or the mobile phase (eluting solvents).
5. **High Purity:** It must be free from impurities that could interfere with the separation or bleed from the column, creating noise in the detector signal.
6. **Availability and Cost-Effectiveness:** The material should be readily and economically available.

#### Principle of Separation

The stationary phase is chosen so that the components of the sample mixture have different affinities (e.g., solubilities, adsorption strengths) for it. This difference causes each component to move through the stationary phase at a different rate when carried by the mobile phase, resulting in their separation.

#### Achieving High Resolution

To separate a mixture of closely related compounds with high resolution, specific choices are made:

*   **Stationary Phase:** Use a stationary phase with very small and uniform particles. This minimizes the diffusion paths for the analytes and provides more interaction sites.
*   **Mobile Phase:** Use a mobile phase with low viscosity and a high, stable flow rate. This reduces band broadening (diffusion) and ensures that the components move through the column efficiently, resulting in sharper peaks and better separation.

### 19.4.2 Selection of Mobile Phase

The mobile phase (or eluent) is the solvent or gas that moves the sample mixture through the stationary phase. The choice of mobile phase is just as critical as the stationary phase for achieving a successful separation.

#### Solvent Purity

It is crucial to use high-purity solvents to avoid introducing contaminants that can interfere with the analysis.

*   **Grade:** Use HPLC (High-Performance Liquid Chromatography) or MS (Mass Spectrometry) grade solvents. These are specifically purified to minimize impurities, dissolved gases, and particulate matter.
*   **Consistency:** Sourcing solvents from reputable manufacturers with strong quality control ensures batch-to-batch consistency, leading to more reproducible experimental results.

#### Key Solvent Properties

Several physical and chemical properties of the solvent must be considered:

*   **Polarity:** The polarity of the mobile phase should be matched to the polarity of the analytes and the stationary phase to achieve optimal interaction and elution. In normal-phase chromatography, a non-polar mobile phase is used with a polar stationary phase. In reverse-phase chromatography, a polar mobile phase is used with a non-polar stationary phase.

*   **Viscosity:** Solvents with low viscosity are preferred as they allow for lower back-pressure and better efficiency at higher flow rates.
*   **Chemical Reactivity:** The mobile phase must be chemically inert and not react with or degrade the analytes or the stationary phase.
*   **UV Transparency:** If a UV-Vis detector is being used, the mobile phase must be transparent (i.e., not absorb light) at the specific wavelength used for detection. This is often referred to as the UV cutoff wavelength. For more on detection methods, see <InlineNoteTag label="IR Spectroscopy" notePath="chemistry-12/18.3-ir-spectroscopy" />.

*   **pH:** For separations involving ionizable compounds (acids or bases), controlling the pH of the mobile phase with a buffer is essential to ensure that the analytes are in a consistent, non-charged, or charged state, which affects their retention and peak shape.

### Summary Table

| Phase | Key Considerations | Goal |
|:--- |:--- |:--- |
| **Stationary Phase** | Particle size, surface area, chemical nature (polarity), mechanical stability, purity. | To provide a medium that interacts differently with each analyte. |
| **Mobile Phase** | Polarity, viscosity, purity (grade), chemical inertness, pH, UV transparency. | To carry analytes through the stationary phase at different rates for separation. |

# 19.5 Use of Mass Spectrometry in Forensic Analysis

Mass spectrometry is a powerful analytical tool used across various scientific fields, including chemistry, biochemistry, medicine, and environmental science. Its primary function is to identify unknown substances, analyze their molecular composition, and quantify known compounds. In forensic science, it is crucial for analyzing trace evidence found at crime scenes, such as drugs, toxins, explosives, and other chemical residues.

---

## 19.5.1 How Mass Spectrometry Works in Forensic Science

In mass spectrometry, a sample is analyzed to determine its molecular composition. This is achieved by generating a spectrum that reveals the mass-to-charge ratio of its component parts. The process can be broken down into five key steps:

1.  **Sample Injection and Separation:** The sample is introduced into the mass spectrometer. Often, it first passes through a separation instrument like a gas chromatograph (GC) to separate the mixture into its individual components before analysis.
2.  **Ionization:** The separated components enter an ionization chamber. A common method used is Electron Ionization (EI), where high-energy electrons are fired at the sample molecules. This knocks one or more electrons off the molecules, creating positively charged ions (cations).
    $M + e^- \rightarrow M^{+\bullet} + 2e^-$
    *Where $M$ is the sample molecule and $M^{+\bullet}$ is the resulting molecular ion.*
3.  **Acceleration and Separation:** The newly formed ions are accelerated into a magnetic field within a mass analyzer. The magnetic field deflects the ions based on their **mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$)**. Lighter ions or more highly charged ions are deflected more than heavier or less charged ions.
4.  **Detection:** The separated ion beams travel to a detector. The detector generates an electrical signal that is proportional to the number of ions hitting it at each specific $m/z$ value.
5.  **Spectrum Generation:** The data from the detector is plotted as a **mass spectrum**, which shows the relative abundance of ions at each $m/z$ ratio. A forensic analyst can interpret this unique "fingerprint" to identify the exact compounds present in the original sample by comparing it to a library of known spectra.

---

## 19.5.2 Applications of Mass Spectrometry

Mass spectrometry is an efficient method to elucidate the chemical composition of a sample. Its key applications in forensics include:

*   **Drug Testing:** Identifying illegal substances in blood, urine, or tissue samples.
*   **Toxicology:** Determining the presence of poisons, toxins, or drug overdoses as a cause of death.
*   **Explosive Residue Detection:** Analyzing trace amounts of material from a suspected explosion site to identify the type of explosive used.
*   **Arson Investigation:** Identifying accelerants (like gasoline) from fire debris.
*   **Quality Control:** Ensuring the purity and composition of drugs, foods, and other commercial products.

---

## 19.5.3 Advantages and Disadvantages of Mass Spectrometry

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|:--- |:--- |
| **High Sensitivity:** Can detect substances at very low concentrations (parts per million or even billion). | **Poor Isomer Separation:** Cannot distinguish between optical and geometric isomers on its own. |
| **High Accuracy:** Accurately determines the molecular mass and atomic composition of a sample. | **Limited for Similar Ions:** Less effective for identifying hydrocarbons that produce similar-mass ions. |
| **Versatility:** Can analyze a wide range of compounds, including complex mixtures. | **High Cost and Specialization:** The equipment is expensive and requires highly trained personnel to operate. |

To overcome some disadvantages, mass spectrometry is often combined with separation techniques like **Gas Chromatography (GC-MS)** to improve the separation and identification of compounds in a mixture. This is often compared with other spectroscopic techniques like <InlineNoteTag label="IR Spectroscopy" notePath="chemistry-12/18.3-ir-spectroscopy" /> for comprehensive analysis.
