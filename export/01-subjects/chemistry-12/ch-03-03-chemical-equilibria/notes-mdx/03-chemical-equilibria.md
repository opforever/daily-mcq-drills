# 3.1 Strength of Acids and Bases

The strength of an acid or base depends on the **extent to which it ionizes** in water. This is quantified by the **acid dissociation constant ($K_a$)** and the **base dissociation constant ($K_b$)**.

---

## Strong vs Weak Acids

### Strong Acids
A **strong acid** ionizes **completely** in water. The degree of ionization is approximately **1 (100%)**.

$HA \rightarrow H^+ + A^-$

Examples: $HCl$, $HNO_3$, $H_2SO_4$, $HClO_4$

For strong acids, $K_a$ is very large (effectively $\gg 1$), so the equilibrium lies entirely to the right.

### Weak Acids
A **weak acid** ionizes only **partially** in water, establishing a dynamic equilibrium:

$HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-$

The **acid dissociation constant** is:

$K_a = \frac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$

A **larger $K_a$** means greater ionization → **stronger acid**.

Examples:
| Acid | $K_a$ | $pK_a$ |
|------|--------|--------|
| Acetic acid ($CH_3COOH$) | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | 4.76 |
| Hydrofluoric acid ($HF$) | $6.3 \times 10^{-4}$ | 3.2 |
| Carbonic acid ($H_2CO_3$) | $4.3 \times 10^{-7}$ | 6.37 |

### The $pK_a$ Scale
$pK_a = -\log_{10}(K_a)$

- **Lower $pK_a$** → larger $K_a$ → **stronger acid**
- A difference of 1 $pK_a$ unit = 10-fold difference in $K_a$

---

## Strong vs Weak Bases

### Strong Bases
A **strong base** dissociates **completely** in water. Degree of ionization ≈ 1.

$MOH \rightarrow M^+ + OH^-$

Examples: $NaOH$, $KOH$, $Ca(OH)_2$

### Weak Bases
A **weak base** ionizes only **partially** in water:

$B + H_2O \rightleftharpoons BH^+ + OH^-$

The **base dissociation constant** is:

$K_b = \frac{[BH^+][OH^-]}{[B]}$

A **larger $K_b$** means greater ionization → **stronger base**.

Example: Ammonia ($NH_3$), $K_b = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$

$NH_3 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+ + OH^-$

### The $pK_b$ Scale
$pK_b = -\log_{10}(K_b)$

- **Lower $pK_b$** → larger $K_b$ → **stronger base**

---

## Summary: Comparing Acid/Base Strength

| Property | Strong Acid/Base | Weak Acid/Base |
|----------|-----------------|----------------|
| Degree of ionization | ~100% (complete) | Partial (≪100%) |
| $K_a$ or $K_b$ | Very large ($\gg 1$) | Small ($\ll 1$) |
| $pK_a$ or $pK_b$ | Very negative or very small | Larger positive value |
| Equilibrium position | Far right | Left (mostly un-ionized) |

---

## Worked Example

**Q:** Ethanoic acid has $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$. Calculate its $pK_a$ and classify it.

**Solution:**
$pK_a = -\log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) = -(-4.74) = 4.74$

Since $K_a \ll 1$ and $pK_a > 0$, ethanoic acid is a **weak acid** with low degree of ionization.

# 3.2 Buffer Solutions and their Applications

A buffer solution is a solution whose pH does not change significantly when a small amount of acid or base is added to it. Such a solution maintains a constant pH over time.

## Types of Buffer Solutions

Buffer solutions can be broadly classified into two types based on their pH:

1.  **Acidic Buffers:**
    *   Formed by mixing a **weak acid** and a salt of that weak acid with a strong base.
    *   These solutions have a pH less than 7.
    *   *Example:* Acetic acid ($\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$) and Sodium Acetate ($\mathrm{CH_3COONa}$).
2.  **Basic Buffers:**
    *   Formed by mixing a **weak base** and a salt of that weak base with a strong acid.
    *   These solutions have a pH greater than 7.
    *   *Example:* Ammonium hydroxide ($\mathrm{NH_4OH}$) and Ammonium chloride ($\mathrm{NH_4Cl}$).

## Buffer Action

Let's consider a typical acidic buffer solution containing acetic acid ($\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$) and sodium acetate ($\mathrm{CH_3COONa}$). The principle of buffer action relies on the **common ion effect**.

*   **Components and Equilibria:**
    *   Acetic acid is a weak electrolyte and dissociates partially:
        $ \mathrm{CH_3COOH}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{H_2O}_{(\mathrm{l})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3COO^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{H_3O^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} $
    *   Sodium acetate is a strong electrolyte and dissociates completely:
        $ \mathrm{CH_3COONa}_{(\mathrm{s})} \longrightarrow \mathrm{CH_3COO^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{Na^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} $
    *   The common ion $\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}$ from the salt suppresses the dissociation of the weak acid, shifting its equilibrium to the left.

*   **Action Upon Addition of Acid (e.g., HCl):**
    When a small amount of a strong acid ($\mathrm{HCl}$) is added, it introduces $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions into the solution:
    $ \mathrm{HCl}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \longrightarrow \mathrm{H^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{Cl^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} $
    The added $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions are consumed by the acetate ions ($\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}$) present in abundance from the salt, forming undissociated weak acetic acid:
    $ \mathrm{CH_3COO^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{H^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3COOH}_{(\mathrm{aq})} $
    This reaction effectively neutralizes the added acid, preventing a significant change in pH.

*   **Action Upon Addition of Base (e.g., NaOH):**
    When a small amount of a strong base ($\mathrm{NaOH}$) is added, it introduces $\mathrm{OH^-}$ ions into the solution:
    $ \mathrm{NaOH}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Na^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{OH^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} $
    The added $\mathrm{OH^-}$ ions are neutralized by the $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions produced by the weak acid, forming water. To replenish the consumed $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions, the weak acetic acid dissociates further:
    $ \mathrm{H^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{OH^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2O}_{(\mathrm{l})} $
    The overall reaction where the weak acid neutralizes the added base is:
    $ \mathrm{CH_3COOH}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{OH^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3COO^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{H_2O}_{(\mathrm{l})} $
    This reaction consumes the added base, preventing a significant change in pH.

## Calculation of pH of Buffer Solution

The pH of a buffer solution can be calculated by assuming that the concentration of the conjugate base is predominantly supplied by the strong electrolyte (salt), and the original concentration of the weak acid (or base) can be approximated as its equilibrium concentration. This approach is the basis of the **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation**.

For a weak acid/conjugate base buffer:
$ K_a = \frac{[\text{Conjugate Base}][\mathrm{H^+}]}{[\text{Weak Acid}]} $
Which can be rearranged to:
$ [\mathrm{H^+}] = K_a \frac{[\text{Weak Acid}]}{[\text{Conjugate Base}]} $
And then $\mathrm{pH} = -\log[\mathrm{H^+}]$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 3.2: pH Calculation of an Acetic Acid-Sodium Acetate Buffer

**(a) Calculate the pH of an acetic acid-sodium acetate buffer solution containing 1.0 moles of each component.**

1.  **Given Values:**
    *   $[\mathrm{CH_3CO_2H}] = 1.0\,\mathrm{M}$ (concentration of weak acid)
    *   $[\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}] = 1.0\,\mathrm{M}$ (concentration of conjugate base, from salt)
    *   $K_a$ for acetic acid = $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$

2.  **Apply the Formula (Acid Dissociation Constant):**
    The dissociation of acetic acid is:
    $ \mathrm{CH_3CO_2H} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3COO^-} + \mathrm{H^+} $
    The equilibrium constant expression is:
    $ K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}][\mathrm{H^+}]}{[\mathrm{CH_3CO_2H}]} $

3.  **Show Calculation:**
    Substitute the given values:
    $ 1.8 \times 10^{-5} = \frac{[1.0\,\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{H^+}]}{[1.0\,\mathrm{M}]} $
    Therefore, $[\mathrm{H^+}] = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{M}$.
    Calculate pH:
    $ \mathrm{pH} = -\log[\mathrm{H^+}] $
    $ \mathrm{pH} = -\log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) $
    $ \mathrm{pH} = 4.745 $
    *The pH of the initial buffer solution is 4.745.*

**(b) What will be the pH of this solution after the addition of 0.01 mole of hydrochloric acid to $1\,\mathrm{dm}^3$ of the solution? Assume that the volume of the solution remains unchanged with the addition of hydrochloric acid.**

4.  **Given Values:**
    *   Initial moles of $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$ = 1.0 mole
    *   Initial moles of $\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}$ = 1.0 mole
    *   Moles of $\mathrm{HCl}$ added = 0.01 mole
    *   Volume of solution = $1\,\mathrm{dm}^3$ (or 1 L)
    *   $K_a$ for acetic acid = $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$

5.  **Impact of HCl Addition:**
    $\mathrm{HCl}$ is a strong acid and dissociates completely, producing 0.01 mole of $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions:
    $ \mathrm{HCl}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \longrightarrow \mathrm{H^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{Cl^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} $
    These $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions react with the conjugate base ($\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}$) present in the buffer:
    $ \mathrm{CH_3COO^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{H^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \longrightarrow \mathrm{CH_3CO_2H}_{(\mathrm{aq})} $
    Each 0.01 mole of $\mathrm{H^+}$ will consume 0.01 mole of $\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}$ and produce 0.01 mole of $\mathrm{CH_3CO_2H}$.

6.  **Calculate New Moles/Concentrations:**
    *   Moles of $\mathrm{CH_3CO_2H}$ after $\mathrm{HCl}$ addition:
        $ (1.0 + 0.01)\,\mathrm{mole} = 1.01\,\mathrm{mole} $
    *   Moles of $\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}$ after $\mathrm{HCl}$ addition:
        $ (1.0 - 0.01)\,\mathrm{mole} = 0.99\,\mathrm{mole} $
    Since the volume is $1\,\mathrm{dm}^3$, these values also represent the new concentrations:
    *   $[\mathrm{CH_3CO_2H}] = 1.01\,\mathrm{M}$
    *   $[\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}] = 0.99\,\mathrm{M}$

7.  **Show Calculation for New pH:**
    Rearrange the $K_a$ expression to solve for $[\mathrm{H^+}]$:
    $ [\mathrm{H^+}] = K_a \frac{[\mathrm{CH_3CO_2H}]}{[\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}]} $
    Substitute the new concentrations:
    $ [\mathrm{H^+}] = \frac{(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) \times (1.01\,\mathrm{M})}{0.99\,\mathrm{M}} $
    $ [\mathrm{H^+}] = 1.836 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{M} $
    Calculate pH:
    $ \mathrm{pH} = -\log[\mathrm{H^+}] $
    $ \mathrm{pH} = -\log(1.836 \times 10^{-5}) $
    $ \mathrm{pH} = 4.736 $
    *The pH after adding HCl is 4.736.*

## Applications of Buffer Solutions

Buffers are vital in maintaining stable pH environments across various fields.

### The Buffer System in Blood Plasma

*   The **bicarbonate buffer system** is crucial for maintaining blood pH within a narrow range of 7.35-7.45. Deviation outside 6.8-7.8 can be fatal.
*   **Formation of Carbonic Acid:** Carbon dioxide reacts with water to form carbonic acid. This reaction is catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells.
    $ \mathrm{CO}_{2(\mathrm{g})} + \mathrm{H_2O}_{(\mathrm{l})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2CO}_{3(\mathrm{aq})} $
*   **Decomposition of Carbonic Acid:** Carbonic acid then dissociates into bicarbonate ions and hydrogen ions.
    $ \mathrm{H_2CO}_{3(\mathrm{aq})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{HCO}_{3(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} + \mathrm{H^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} $
*   **Neutralization of Acids:** If blood becomes too acidic (excess $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions), bicarbonate acts as a base to neutralize them:
    $ \mathrm{HCO}_{3(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} + \mathrm{H^{+}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2CO}_{3(\mathrm{aq})} $
*   **Neutralization of Bases:** If blood becomes too basic (excess $\mathrm{OH^-}$ ions), carbonic acid donates $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions to neutralize the base:
    $ \mathrm{OH^{-}}_{(\mathrm{aq})} + \mathrm{H_2CO}_{3(\mathrm{aq})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{HCO}_{3(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} + \mathrm{H_2O}_{(\mathrm{l})} $

### Household Cleaning Products

*   **Detergents:** Contain buffers to maintain a stable pH, ensuring effective cleaning without damaging surfaces or irritating skin.

### Personal Care Products

*   **Shampoos and Soaps:** Buffers are used to maintain pH, preventing skin irritation and ensuring product gentleness.

### Swimming Pools

*   **Water Treatment:** Buffers help maintain a stable pH level, preventing corrosion of pool equipment and ensuring comfort for swimmers.

### Food and Beverage Industry

*   **Food Preservation:** Used to control acidity, preserve flavors, and maintain the stability of food products.
*   **Beverage Production:** Help control pH in beverages for consistent taste and to prevent spoilage.

### Photography

*   **Developer Solutions:** Buffers maintain a stable pH for controlled and consistent development of film or prints.

# 3.3 Solubility Product and Precipitation Reactions

## Introduction

Many salts that are considered "insoluble" actually dissolve to a very small extent in water. These are called **sparingly soluble salts**. The equilibrium established between the undissolved solid and its ions in solution is described by the **Solubility Product**, $K_{sp}$.

---

## The Solubility Product ($K_{sp}$)

For a sparingly soluble salt $A_xB_y$ dissolving in water:

$A_xB_y(s) \rightleftharpoons xA^{y+}(aq) + yB^{x-}(aq)$

The solubility product expression is:

$K_{sp} = [A^{y+}]^x[B^{x-}]^y$

> **Note:** The concentration of the pure solid $A_xB_y$ is constant and is incorporated into $K_{sp}$, so it does not appear in the expression.

### Key Points
- $K_{sp}$ is a constant at a given temperature.
- A **smaller** $K_{sp}$ means the salt is **less soluble**.
- $K_{sp}$ only applies to **saturated solutions** of sparingly soluble salts.

### Examples of $K_{sp}$ Expressions

| Salt | Equilibrium | $K_{sp}$ Expression |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| $AgCl$ | $AgCl \rightleftharpoons Ag^+ + Cl^-$ | $K_{sp} = [Ag^+][Cl^-]$ |
| $CaF_2$ | $CaF_2 \rightleftharpoons Ca^{2+} + 2F^-$ | $K_{sp} = [Ca^{2+}][F^-]^2$ |
| $Ag_2CrO_4$ | $Ag_2CrO_4 \rightleftharpoons 2Ag^+ + CrO_4^{2-}$ | $K_{sp} = [Ag^+]^2[CrO_4^{2-}]$ |
| $Ca_3(PO_4)_2$ | $Ca_3(PO_4)_2 \rightleftharpoons 3Ca^{2+} + 2PO_4^{3-}$ | $K_{sp} = [Ca^{2+}]^3[PO_4^{3-}]^2$ |

---

## Molar Solubility and $K_{sp}$

**Molar solubility** ($S$) is the number of moles of a salt that dissolves per litre of solution to form a saturated solution.

### Worked Example 1: $AgCl$

$AgCl(s) \rightleftharpoons Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq)$

If solubility = $S$: $[Ag^+] = S$, $[Cl^-] = S$

$K_{sp} = S \times S = S^2 \implies S = \sqrt{K_{sp}}$

### Worked Example 2: $Ag_2CrO_4$

$Ag_2CrO_4(s) \rightleftharpoons 2Ag^+(aq) + CrO_4^{2-}(aq)$

If solubility = $S$: $[Ag^+] = 2S$, $[CrO_4^{2-}] = S$

$K_{sp} = (2S)^2(S) = 4S^3 \implies S = \left(\frac{K_{sp}}{4}\right)^{1/3}$

### Worked Example 3: $MX_2$ type salt

Given $K_{sp} = 4 \times 10^{-12}$:

$K_{sp} = 4S^3 \implies S^3 = \frac{4 \times 10^{-12}}{4} = 10^{-12} \implies S = 10^{-4}\,mol\,dm^{-3}$

---

## The Ionic Product ($Q_{sp}$) and Precipitation

The **Ionic Product** $Q_{sp}$ is calculated the same way as $K_{sp}$, but using the **actual concentrations** of ions in any solution (not necessarily saturated).

### Precipitation Rules

| Condition | Meaning | Result |
|-----------|---------|--------|
| $Q_{sp} < K_{sp}$ | Unsaturated | No precipitate; more salt can dissolve |
| $Q_{sp} = K_{sp}$ | Saturated | Equilibrium; no change |
| $Q_{sp} > K_{sp}$ | Supersaturated | **Precipitate forms** |

### Worked Example: Will a precipitate form?

If $[Ba^{2+}] = 0.001\,mol\,dm^{-3}$ and $[SO_4^{2-}] = 0.002\,mol\,dm^{-3}$ are mixed, and $K_{sp}(BaSO_4) = 1.1 \times 10^{-10}$:

$Q_{sp} = [Ba^{2+}][SO_4^{2-}] = (0.001)(0.002) = 2 \times 10^{-6}$

Since $Q_{sp} = 2 \times 10^{-6} \gg K_{sp} = 1.1 \times 10^{-10}$, a precipitate of $BaSO_4$ **will form**.

---

## The Common Ion Effect on Solubility

When a soluble salt sharing a common ion with a sparingly soluble salt is added to the solution, the solubility of the sparingly soluble salt **decreases**. This is the **Common Ion Effect**, explained by Le Chatelier's Principle.

### Example: $AgCl$ in $NaCl$ solution

$AgCl(s) \rightleftharpoons Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq)$

Adding $NaCl$ increases $[Cl^-]$. By Le Chatelier's Principle, the equilibrium shifts **left**, reducing the dissolution of $AgCl$. Therefore, the solubility of $AgCl$ decreases.

### Quantitative Treatment

If $AgCl$ is dissolved in $0.1\,mol\,dm^{-3}$ $NaCl$ solution ($K_{sp}(AgCl) = 1.8 \times 10^{-10}$):

$K_{sp} = [Ag^+][Cl^-] = S(0.1 + S) \approx S \times 0.1$

$S = \frac{K_{sp}}{0.1} = \frac{1.8 \times 10^{-10}}{0.1} = 1.8 \times 10^{-9}\,mol\,dm^{-3}$

Compare this to the solubility in pure water: $S = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-10}} = 1.34 \times 10^{-5}\,mol\,dm^{-3}$. The common ion reduces solubility by a factor of ~7000.

## Applications of Solubility Product

### 1. Barium Meal in Medical Imaging

$BaSO_4$ has an extremely low $K_{sp}$ ($\approx 1.1 \times 10^{-10}$), making it virtually insoluble in body fluids. Although barium ions ($Ba^{2+}$) are toxic, the concentration of dissolved $Ba^{2+}$ from $BaSO_4$ is negligibly small and harmless. Its high atomic mass makes it opaque to X-rays, providing excellent contrast for imaging the gastrointestinal tract.

### 2. Qualitative Analysis (Selective Precipitation)

In qualitative analysis, specific reagents are added to selectively precipitate certain ions while leaving others in solution. For example:
- Adding $H_2S$ in acidic conditions precipitates Group II cations (e.g., $PbS$, $CuS$) because their $K_{sp}$ values are extremely small.
- Group III cations (e.g., $ZnS$) have larger $K_{sp}$ values and only precipitate in basic conditions.

### 3. Water Treatment

Phosphate ions ($PO_4^{3-}$) in wastewater can be removed by adding $Ca^{2+}$ ions, precipitating insoluble $Ca_3(PO_4)_2$:

$3Ca^{2+}(aq) + 2PO_4^{3-}(aq) \rightarrow Ca_3(PO_4)_2(s)$

### 4. Salt Purification (Common Ion Effect)

Impure $NaCl$ dissolved in water can be purified by passing $HCl$ gas through the solution. The common ion $Cl^-$ from $HCl$ causes $NaCl$ to precipitate out, leaving more soluble impurities in solution.

# 3.4 Common Ion Effect

The common ion effect is a crucial concept in chemistry, particularly when dealing with the equilibrium of weak electrolytes and sparingly soluble salts. It describes how the presence of a common ion in a solution can suppress the ionization or solubility of another electrolyte.

## 1. Definition of the Common Ion Effect

The common ion effect describes the behavior of a solution in which the same ion is produced by two different compounds. Specifically, it is defined as:

> "The phenomenon in which the degree of ionization or solubility of an electrolyte is suppressed by the addition of a highly soluble electrolyte containing a common ion."

This effect is a direct consequence of **Le Chatelier's principle**.

## 2. Mechanism: Weak Electrolyte and its Salt

Consider a weak electrolyte, such as hydrofluoric acid ($\mathrm{HF}$), which partially dissociates in water, and its salt, sodium fluoride ($\mathrm{NaF}$), which is a strong electrolyte.

**Dissociation of Hydrofluoric Acid (Weak Electrolyte):**
$ \mathrm{HF}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{+} + \mathrm{F}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} $
(Here, $K_a = 7.2 \times 10^{-4}$, indicating weak dissociation.)

**Dissociation of Sodium Fluoride (Strong Electrolyte):**
$ \mathrm{NaF}_{(\mathrm{s})} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{+} + \mathrm{F}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} $
(NaF dissociates completely.)

In this scenario, $\mathrm{F^-}$ is the common ion produced by both compounds. When NaF is added to a solution of HF, the concentration of $\mathrm{F^-}$ ions significantly increases. According to Le Chatelier's principle, the equilibrium of the weak acid (HF) will shift to the **left** to consume the excess $\mathrm{F^-}$ ions. This shift results in:

- A **decrease** in the dissociation of HF.
- An **increase** in the concentration of undissociated HF.
- A **decrease** in the concentration of $\mathrm{H}^{+}$ ions.

Thus, the presence of the common ion suppresses the ionization of the weak electrolyte. This principle is directly related to buffer solutions.

## 3. Mechanism: Sparingly Soluble Salt and Highly Soluble Salt

Similarly, the common ion effect can decrease the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt. When a highly soluble salt is added to a saturated solution of a less soluble salt containing a common ion, the degree of dissociation of the less soluble salt decreases, causing a decrease in its solubility and often leading to precipitation.

## 4. Applications of the Common Ion Effect

The common ion effect has significant applications in various chemical processes:

- **Separation and Identification of Cations:** In qualitative chemical analysis, the separation and identification of cations into analytical groups are often based on the solubility product principle and the common ion effect. By carefully controlling the concentration of a common ion, specific ions can be selectively precipitated.
- **Controlled Precipitation:** Any procedure that involves controlled precipitation (e.g., in industrial processes or laboratory synthesis) often utilizes these principles to maximize the yield of a desired product or remove impurities.

## Worked Examples

### Example i: Potassium Perchlorate Precipitation

1. **Given:** Potassium perchlorate ($\mathrm{KClO_4}$) is moderately soluble in water. Potassium chloride ($\mathrm{KCl}$) is highly soluble.
2. **Scenario:** Highly soluble $\mathrm{KCl}$ is added to a saturated solution of $\mathrm{KClO_4}$.
3. **Chemical Reactions:**
   - Dissociation of potassium perchlorate (sparingly soluble):
     $ \mathrm{KClO}_{4(\mathrm{s})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{K}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{+} + \mathrm{ClO}_{4(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} $
   - Dissociation of potassium chloride (highly soluble):
     $ \mathrm{KCl}_{(\mathrm{s})} \rightarrow \mathrm{K}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{+} + \mathrm{Cl}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} $
4. **Explanation:** The addition of $\mathrm{KCl}$ significantly increases the concentration of $\mathrm{K}^{+}$ ions, which is a common ion with $\mathrm{KClO_4}$. According to Le Chatelier's principle, the equilibrium of $\mathrm{KClO_4}$ shifts to the **left**, favoring the formation of solid $\mathrm{KClO_4}$. This suppresses the ionization (and thus the solubility) of $\mathrm{KClO_4}$, causing it to precipitate out of the solution.

### Example ii: Sodium Chloride Precipitation from Brine

1. **Given:** A saturated solution of sodium chloride (brine). Hydrogen chloride gas ($\mathrm{HCl}$) is passed through it.
2. **Scenario:** $\mathrm{HCl}$ gas is passed through a saturated solution of $\mathrm{NaCl}$.
3. **Chemical Reactions:**
   - Dissociation of sodium chloride (in saturated solution):
     $ \mathrm{NaCl}_{(\mathrm{s})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Na}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{+} + \mathrm{Cl}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} $
   - Dissociation of hydrogen chloride (strong acid):
     $ \mathrm{HCl}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \rightarrow \mathrm{H}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{+} + \mathrm{Cl}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} $
4. **Explanation:** When $\mathrm{HCl}$ gas is passed into the solution, it dissolves and dissociates completely, increasing the concentration of $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ions. $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ is a common ion with $\mathrm{NaCl}$. According to Le Chatelier's principle, the increased $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ concentration shifts the equilibrium of $\mathrm{NaCl}$ to the **left**, causing pure $\mathrm{NaCl}$ to precipitate out. This method is used for purifying common salt.

## Possible Questions/Answers

**Q1:** Ammonium chloride ($\mathrm{NH_4Cl}$) is a water-soluble salt. What will happen if this salt is added to a solution containing ammonium hydroxide ($\mathrm{NH_4OH}$)?
$ \mathrm{NH_4OH}_{(\mathrm{aq})} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{NH}_{4(\mathrm{aq})}^{+} + \mathrm{OH}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{-} $

**A1:** Ammonium hydroxide ($\mathrm{NH_4OH}$) is a weak base that partially dissociates in water. Ammonium chloride ($\mathrm{NH_4Cl}$) is a strong electrolyte that completely dissociates, producing $\mathrm{NH_4}^{+}$ ions. These $\mathrm{NH_4}^{+}$ ions are common ions. According to Le Chatelier's principle, the addition of $\mathrm{NH_4}^{+}$ ions will shift the equilibrium of $\mathrm{NH_4OH}$ to the **left**, suppressing its ionization and decreasing the concentration of $\mathrm{OH^-}$ ions, making the solution less basic.

**Q2:** Carbonic acid is a weak acid. It ionizes in water as follows:
$ \mathrm{H_2CO}_{3(\mathrm{aq})} \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{H}_{(\mathrm{aq})}^{+} + \mathrm{CO}_{3(\mathrm{aq})}^{2-} $
What will happen if a strong electrolyte such as $\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}$ is added to a solution containing carbonic acid?

**A2:** Carbonic acid ($\mathrm{H_2CO_3}$) is a weak acid that partially ionizes. Sodium carbonate ($\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}$) is a strong electrolyte that completely dissociates, producing $\mathrm{CO_3}^{2-}$ ions. These $\mathrm{CO_3}^{2-}$ ions are common ions. According to Le Chatelier's principle, the addition of $\mathrm{CO_3}^{2-}$ ions will shift the equilibrium of $\mathrm{H_2CO_3}$ to the **left**, suppressing its ionization and decreasing the concentration of $\mathrm{H}^{+}$ ions, making the solution less acidic.

# The Partition Coefficient and Distribution Law

## 3.5 The Partition Coefficient

When a solute is introduced to a system containing two immiscible liquids, it distributes itself between the two solvents. This distribution reaches a dynamic equilibrium, where the rate at which the solute moves from the first solvent to the second equals the rate at which it moves from the second to the first.

Consider two immiscible liquids, such as ether (less dense, forms the upper layer) and water. If a solute $X$ that dissolves in both liquids is added to this system and shaken vigorously, the solute will distribute itself between the two phases. At equilibrium, the concentration of solute $X$ in each layer becomes constant.

The distribution of solute $X$ between water and ether can be represented by the following equilibrium:

$
\mathrm{X} \text{ (in water)} \quad \rightleftharpoons\quad \mathrm{X} \text{ (in ether)}
$

The equilibrium constant for this distribution is known as the **partition coefficient** and is denoted by $K_{pc}$ or $K_p$. It is defined as the ratio of the concentration of the solute in the organic (ether) layer to its concentration in the aqueous (water) layer.

$
\mathrm{K}_{\mathrm{PC}}=\frac{[\mathrm{X} \text{ in ether}]}{[\mathrm{X} \text{ in water}]}
$

### Nernst Distribution Law

The concept governing the distribution of a solute between two immiscible solvents is known as the **distribution law** or **Nernst distribution law**. This law states that at a constant temperature, a solute distributes itself between two immiscible solvents in contact with each other in such a way that the ratio of its concentrations in the two solvents is constant, provided the solute is in the same molecular state in both solvents.

The partition coefficient ($K_{pc}$) is therefore defined as:
*The ratio of the concentrations of a solute in two different immiscible solvents in contact with each other when equilibrium has been established at a particular temperature.*

Since the partition coefficient is a ratio of two concentrations (e.g., $\mathrm{mol\,dm}^{-3} / \mathrm{mol\,dm}^{-3}$), it is a **dimensionless quantity** and thus has **no units**. Its value is constant at a constant temperature.

### 3.5.1 Calculation of Partition Coefficient

The partition coefficient can be calculated using the equilibrium expression when the solute exists in the same physical state in both solvents. This principle is widely used in solvent extraction, a technique often used to separate organic products from aqueous reaction mixtures.

## Worked Examples

### ACTIVITY 3.1: Calculating the partition coefficient

**Procedure:**

1. Measure $100\ \mathrm{cm}^3$ of a $0.150\ \mathrm{M}$ solution of aqueous methylamine ($\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$) and add it into a separatory funnel.
2. Add $75\ \mathrm{cm}^3$ of an organic solvent into the separatory funnel.
3. Shake the separatory funnel gently but thoroughly to allow the solute to distribute between the two solvents. Allow the system to reach equilibrium (about 5-10 minutes).
4. Let the layers settle and separate the two solvents into different beakers.
5. Measure the concentration of the solute in each solvent.

**Calculations:**

$100\ \mathrm{cm}^3$ of a $0.150\ \mathrm{mol\,dm}^{-3}$ solution of aqueous methylamine ($\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$) is shaken with $75.0\ \mathrm{cm}^3$ of an organic solvent. After equilibrium, $25\ \mathrm{cm}^3$ of the aqueous layer is run off and titrated against $0.225\ \mathrm{M\ HCl}$. $7.05\ \mathrm{cm}^3$ of $\mathrm{HCl}$ was used.

**Data for calculations:**

6. Volume of aqueous methylamine solution: $100\ \mathrm{cm}^3$
7. Initial concentration of methylamine: $0.150\ \mathrm{mol\,dm}^{-3}$
8. Volume of organic solvent: $75.0\ \mathrm{cm}^3$
9. Volume of aqueous layer titrated: $25.0\ \mathrm{cm}^3$
10. Volume of $\mathrm{HCl}$ used for titration: $7.05\ \mathrm{cm}^3$
11. Concentration of $\mathrm{HCl}$: $0.225\ \mathrm{mol\,dm}^{-3}$

**Goal:** Calculate the partition coefficient of methylamine in the organic solvent and water.

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Write down the equilibrium equation.**

$
\mathrm{CH_3NH_{2(aq)}} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CH_3NH_2 \text{ (organic solvent)}}
$

**Step 2: Write down the $\mathrm{K}_{\mathrm{pc}}$ expression.**

$
\mathrm{K}_{\mathrm{pc}}=\frac{[\mathrm{CH_3NH_2 \text{ (organic layer)}}]}{[\mathrm{CH_3NH_{2(aq)}}]}
$

**Step 3: Determine the total moles of methylamine in the original solution.**

12. **Given Values:**
   - Initial concentration of $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2} = 0.150\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$
   - Initial volume of $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ solution $= 100\ \mathrm{cm^3} = 0.100\ \mathrm{dm^3}$

13. **Apply Formula:** Moles ($n$) = Concentration ($M$) $\times$ Volume ($V_{dm^3}$)

14. **Calculation:**
   $
   \text{Total moles } \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} = 0.150\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \times 0.100\ \mathrm{dm^3} = 0.015\ \mathrm{mol}
   $

**Step 4: Determine the number of moles of methylamine in the aqueous layer after equilibrium.**

The reaction during titration is:
$
\mathrm{CH_3NH_{2(aq)}} + \mathrm{HCl_{(aq)}} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH_3NH_3Cl_{(aq)}}
$
The stoichiometry is $1\ \mathrm{mol}$ of $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ reacts with $1\ \mathrm{mol}$ of $\mathrm{HCl}$.

15. **Calculation:**
   - Moles of $\mathrm{HCl}$ reacted = $0.225\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \times 0.00705\ \mathrm{dm^3} = 0.00158625\ \mathrm{mol}$
   - Moles of $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ in $25\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ aqueous layer $= 0.00158625\ \mathrm{mol}$.
   - Moles of $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ in the *entire* $100\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ aqueous layer (after equilibrium) =
     $
     \frac{0.00158625\ \mathrm{mol}}{25\ \mathrm{cm^3}} \times 100\ \mathrm{cm^3} = 0.006345\ \mathrm{mol}
     $

**Step 5: Determine the number of moles of $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ present in the organic layer.**

$
\text{Moles } \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} \text{ (organic layer)} = 0.015\ \mathrm{mol} - 0.006345\ \mathrm{mol} = 0.008655\ \mathrm{mol}
$

**Step 6: Change the number of moles into concentrations.**

- Concentration ($\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ in aqueous layer) = $\frac{0.006345\ \mathrm{mol}}{0.100\ \mathrm{dm^3}} = 0.06345\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$
- Concentration ($\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ in organic layer) = $\frac{0.008655\ \mathrm{mol}}{0.075\ \mathrm{dm^3}} = 0.1154\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$

**Step 7: Substitute the values into the $\mathrm{K}_{\mathrm{pc}}$ expression.**

$
K_{PC} = \frac{0.1154\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}}{0.06345\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}} \approx 1.82
$
Since the value of $K_{pc}$ is larger than 1, methylamine is more soluble in the organic solvent than in water.

### 3.5.2 Factors Affecting the Numerical Value of Partition Coefficient

The partition coefficient ($K_{pc}$) quantifies how a solute distributes between two immiscible phases. Several factors can influence its numerical value:

1. **The Polarity of the Solute:**
   - Polar solutes generally have a greater affinity for polar phases (e.g., water) due to favorable dipole-dipole interactions or hydrogen bonding. Conversely, non-polar solutes prefer non-polar phases.

2. **The Solvent Polarity:**
   - The nature of the two immiscible solvents significantly determines the partitioning. This is explained by the "like dissolves like" principle.

3. **Temperature:**
   - Temperature influences the solubility and kinetic energy of the solute in both solvents. Generally, changing temperature alters the $K_{pc}$ value.

4. **Molecular Structure and Size:**
   - The size and specific functional groups within a solute molecule affect its interactions with solvents. Larger molecules or those with complex structures can experience increased steric hindrance or different solvation energies.

## Possible Questions/Answers

- **Q:** When 2 grams of a solute is shaken with a mixture of $150\ \mathrm{cm}^3$ of water and $20\ \mathrm{cm}^3$ of chloroform. After shaking, $1.5$ grams of the solute is found in the chloroform layer. Calculate the partition coefficient.
  - **A:**
    1. **Mass in water:** $2\ \mathrm{g} - 1.5\ \mathrm{g} = 0.5\ \mathrm{g}$
    2. **Concentration in chloroform:** $\frac{1.5\ \mathrm{g}}{20\ \mathrm{cm}^3} = 0.075\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$
    3. **Concentration in water:** $\frac{0.5\ \mathrm{g}}{150\ \mathrm{cm}^3} = 0.00333\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$
    4. **$K_{PC}$:** $\frac{0.075}{0.00333} \approx 22.5$

## Significance of Partition Coefficient

Partition coefficients are crucial in various chemical and biological processes, including:
* **Liquid-liquid extraction:** For separating compounds in the lab.
* **Drug distribution:** Predicting how pharmaceuticals will be absorbed and distributed in the body (e.g., crossing cell membranes).
* **Chromatography:** In paper or column chromatography, the distribution of a solute between the stationary and mobile phases is governed by $K_{pc}$.
