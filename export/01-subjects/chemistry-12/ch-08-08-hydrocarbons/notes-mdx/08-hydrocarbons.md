# Nomenclature of Benzene

This section details the systematic naming conventions for benzene derivatives according to IUPAC rules, covering monosubstituted, disubstituted, and polysubstituted benzenes. Understanding these rules is crucial for correctly identifying and communicating about aromatic compounds in organic chemistry.

## General IUPAC Conventions for Naming Organic Compounds

According to IUPAC conventions:
* All letters in the names of compounds must be written in lowercase, except when they appear at the beginning of a sentence.
* The first letter of element symbols is always uppercase (e.g., H for hydrogen, Br for bromine).

## Monosubstituted Benzenes

Monosubstituted benzenes have one substituent attached to the benzene ring. The IUPAC system retains some common names for simple monosubstituted aromatic compounds.

**Examples of Monosubstituted Benzenes (Common and Systematic Names):**

| Common Name | Systematic Name |
|-------------|------------------|
| Toluene | Methylbenzene |
| Ethylbenzene | Ethylbenzene |
| Styrene | Vinylbenzene |
| Bromobenzene | Bromobenzene |
| Benzoic acid | Benzenecarboxylic acid |
| Benzaldehyde | Benzenecarbaldehyde |
| Anisole | Methoxybenzene |
| Cumene | Isopropylbenzene |
| Nitrobenzene | Nitrobenzene |
| Phenol | Hydroxybenzene |

## Disubstituted Benzenes

Disubstituted benzenes have two substituents attached to the benzene ring and can exhibit three positional isomers. For showing the positions of substituents, numbers or Greek prefixes can be used.

**Positional Isomers:**

* **ortho (o-):** Refers to substituents on adjacent carbons (positions 1,2). The word "ortho" means "straight".
* **meta (m-):** Refers to substituents separated by one carbon (positions 1,3). The word "meta" means "after".
* **para (p-):** Refers to substituents on opposite carbons (positions 1,4). The word "para" means "beyond".

**Examples of Disubstituted Benzenes (Using both numbering and Greek prefixes):**

*Dihydroxybenzenes:*

* 1,2-dihydroxybenzene (Catechol)
* 1,3-dihydroxybenzene (Resorcinol)
* 1,4-dihydroxybenzene (Hydroquinone)

**Derivatives of Parent Molecules:**

When one of the two substituents on the ring gives a special name to the compound (e.g., toluene, phenol, aniline), the compound is named as a derivative of that parent molecule. In this case, the special substituent occupies position 1 on the ring.

The IUPAC system retains the common name **xylene** for the three isomeric dimethylbenzenes:

* 1,2-dimethylbenzene (*o*-xylene)
* 1,3-dimethylbenzene (*m*-xylene)
* 1,4-dimethylbenzene (*p*-xylene)

**Alphabetical Order (When no special name is given):**

When neither group imparts a special name to the compound, the substituents are named in alphabetical order, followed by the word "benzene". The positions are numbered to give the lowest possible set of numbers.

* 1-chloro-4-ethylbenzene (*p*-ethylchlorobenzene)
* 1-bromo-3-chlorobenzene (*m*-chlorobromobenzene)

## Polysubstituted Benzenes (Three or More Substituents)

When three or more substituents are present on a benzene ring, their locations are specified by numbers.

**Derivatives of Parent Molecules:**

If one of the substituents imparts a special name to the molecule, it is named as a derivative of that parent molecule, with the special substituent occupying position 1. The remaining substituents are then numbered to give the smallest possible set of numbers and listed in alphabetical order.

The priority order for choosing the parent molecule is generally:
$-COOH > -CHO > -COCH_3 > -OH > -NH_2 > -R$

* 4-bromo-2-nitrotoluene
* 2-bromo-4-ethylphenol

**Alphabetical Order and Smallest Number Set (When no special name is given):**

If none of the substituents imparts a special name, the carbon atoms are numbered to give the smallest possible set of numbers to the substituents. The substituents are then listed in alphabetical order, followed by the word "benzene".

Example: A compound with three different substituents, e.g., 1-bromo-2-chloro-4-nitrobenzene.

## Possible Questions and Answers

**Q:** What is the systematic IUPAC name for toluene?
**A:** Methylbenzene.

**Q:** Explain the difference between *ortho*, *meta*, and *para* isomers in disubstituted benzenes.
**A:** *ortho* (1,2-positions), *meta* (1,3-positions), and *para* (1,4-positions).

**Q:** How are substituents named when there are three or more on a benzene ring, and one substituent (e.g., hydroxyl) gives the compound a special name (e.g., phenol)?
**A:** The carbon bearing the special substituent is designated as position 1. The other substituents are then numbered to achieve the lowest possible set of numbers and listed alphabetically before the parent name.

**Q:** What common name does IUPAC retain for the three isomeric dimethylbenzenes?
**A:** Xylene.

# 8.2 The Structure of Benzene

## Initial Challenges in Determining Benzene's Structure

After its discovery in 1835, the structure of benzene ($C_6H_6$) remained a puzzle for chemists for forty years. Elemental analysis revealed an unusually low hydrogen-to-carbon ratio (H:C = 1:1), indicating a high degree of unsaturation. Many chemists initially proposed *open-chain structures* containing carbon-carbon double and triple covalent bonds.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/chemistry/Pasted image 20250923192752.webp" alt="Proposed open-chain structures for benzene" caption="Proposed open-chain structures for benzene" />

However, these structures were inconsistent with the observed high stability and unique chemical properties of benzene.

## Kekulé's Structure of Benzene

In 1865, the German chemist August Kekulé proposed the first *ring (cyclic) structure* for benzene. This idea came to him in a dream, where he envisioned atoms forming chains that then transformed into rings, resembling a snake biting its tail.

*   **Key Features of Kekulé's Model:**
    *   Benzene is a cyclic molecule with six carbon atoms arranged in a ring.
    *   Each carbon atom is bonded to one hydrogen atom.
    *   The ring contains three alternating carbon-carbon double bonds.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/chemistry/Pasted image 20250923192833.webp" alt="Kekulé's Structure" caption="Kekulé's Structure" />

Kekulé further proposed that the double bonds in benzene rapidly alternate their positions, making the two possible structures indistinguishable. This rapid alternation suggested that the pi ($\pi$) electrons are free to move over all six carbon atoms.

## Limitations of Kekulé's Structure

Despite its evolutionary nature, Kekulé's model faced several challenges due to its inability to fully explain benzene's observed properties:

1.  **Reactivity with Alkenes:** If benzene contains double bonds like alkenes, why does it not react in the same way (e.g., undergoing addition reactions readily)?
2.  **Bromine Addition:** Why cannot benzene add three moles of bromine, as would be expected for a molecule with three distinct double bonds?
3.  **Reaction Preference:** Why does benzene favor *substitution reactions* over *addition reactions*?

These inconsistencies led chemists to seek more accurate models for the structure of benzene.

## Resonance Model of Benzene

The concept of *resonance* provides a more accurate description of benzene's structure, addressing the limitations of Kekulé's model. Linus Pauling, in the 1930s, significantly developed the theory of resonance and applied it to organic molecules.

*   **Definition of Resonance:** Resonance is the spreading or *delocalization* of electron pairs (either lone pairs or pi electron pairs) across several adjacent atoms within a molecule or ion.
*   **Resonance Hybrid:** When a single Lewis structure cannot accurately represent the true distribution of electrons, the molecule is said to exhibit resonance. Its actual structure is a *resonance hybrid* - an average of all possible contributing Lewis structures.
*   **Canonical Structures:** The different possible Lewis structures that contribute to the resonance hybrid are called *resonance contributing structures* or *canonical structures*. These are hypothetical and do not exist independently; only the resonance hybrid is real.

For benzene, the true structure is an average of the two Kekulé structures, often represented by a hexagon with an inscribed circle. The circle symbolizes the six delocalized pi ($\pi$) electrons shared over all six carbon atoms.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/chemistry/Pasted image 20250923192855.webp" alt="Resonance Hybrid of Benzene" caption="Resonance Hybrid of Benzene" />

*   **Why Kekulé structures are still used:**
    1.  They justify the tetravalent nature of carbon and the electron count.
    2.  They easily represent the movement of electrons in reaction mechanisms.

## Atomic/Molecular Orbital Model of Benzene

The atomic/molecular orbital model, also developed with concepts of hybridization and resonance by Linus Pauling, provides the most comprehensive explanation for benzene's structure. Each of the six carbon atoms in the benzene ring exhibits $\text{sp}^2$ hybridization: each carbon forms two $\sigma$ bonds with adjacent carbon atoms via $\text{sp}^2-\text{sp}^2$ orbital overlap, and one $\sigma$ bond with a hydrogen atom via $\text{sp}^2-1\text{s}$ orbital overlap. The carbon skeleton forms a regular hexagon, with all C-C-C and H-C-C bond angles at $120^\circ$, indicating a planar structure.

Experimental measurements show that the carbon-carbon bond length in benzene is $1.39 \, \text{Å}$, a value intermediate between a typical carbon-carbon single bond ($1.54 \, \text{Å}$) and a carbon-carbon double bond ($1.30 \, \text{Å}$). This confirms that the C-C bonds in benzene are neither purely single nor purely double, but possess *partial double bond character*.

This partial character comes from the ring's delocalized $\pi$ electron system. Each $\text{sp}^2$-hybridized carbon atom also possesses one unhybridized $2p$ orbital; these six $2p$ orbitals are perpendicular to the plane of the carbon ring and each contains one electron. They overlap *laterally and continuously* around the entire ring, forming a delocalized $\pi$ electron cloud that creates two torus-shaped (doughnut-shaped) regions of electron density, one above the plane of the ring and another below it.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/chemistry/Pasted image 20250923192951.webp" alt="Formation of sigma and pi bonds in benzene" caption="Formation of σ and π bonds in benzene" />

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/chemistry/Pasted image 20250923193030.webp" alt="Delocalized pi electron cloud (torus shape)" caption="Delocalized π electron cloud (torus shape)" />

## Resonance Energy and Exceptional Stability of Benzene

The delocalization of $\pi$-electrons in benzene contributes to its unexpectedly high stability, quantified as *resonance energy* (or delocalization energy).

*   **Definition:** Resonance energy is the difference in energy between the actual benzene molecule and a hypothetical structure with localized double bonds (like 1,3,5-cyclohexatriene).
*   **Measurement:** Resonance energy is typically determined by comparing the experimental enthalpy of hydrogenation of benzene with the theoretical enthalpy calculated for a hypothetical cyclohexatriene.

<CaptionedImage src="/content/assets/class-12/chemistry/Pasted image 20250923193052.webp" alt="Hydrogenation of Cyclohexene" caption="Hydrogenation of Cyclohexene" />

## Worked Examples

### Calculation of Benzene's Resonance Energy

To calculate the resonance energy of benzene, we compare the enthalpies of hydrogenation:

1.  **Hydrogenation of Cyclohexene:**
    *   The experimental enthalpy of hydrogenation for cyclohexene (containing one double bond) is:
        $ \Delta H_{\text{cyclohexene}} = -120 \, \text{kJ/mol} $

2.  **Theoretical Hydrogenation of Hypothetical 1,3,5-Cyclohexatriene:**
    *   If benzene were a simple 1,3,5-cyclohexatriene with three isolated double bonds (like Kekulé's initial idea), its enthalpy of hydrogenation would theoretically be three times that of cyclohexene:
        $ \Delta H_{\text{theoretical cyclohexatriene}} = 3 \times (-120 \, \text{kJ/mol}) = -360 \, \text{kJ/mol} $

3.  **Experimental Hydrogenation of Benzene:**
    *   The experimentally observed enthalpy of hydrogenation for benzene to cyclohexane is significantly less exothermic:
        $ \Delta H_{\text{experimental benzene}} = -208 \, \text{kJ/mol} $

4.  **Calculation of Resonance Energy:**
    *   The difference between the theoretical and experimental values represents the resonance energy, which is the extra stability gained from electron delocalization:
        $ \text{Resonance Energy} = \Delta H_{\text{theoretical cyclohexatriene}} - \Delta H_{\text{experimental benzene}} $
        $ \text{Resonance Energy} = (-360 \, \text{kJ/mol}) - (-208 \, \text{kJ/mol}) $
        $ \text{Resonance Energy} = -360 \, \text{kJ/mol} + 208 \, \text{kJ/mol} = -152 \, \text{kJ/mol} $
    *   Often quoted as a positive value, indicating the *amount of energy released* upon hydrogenation is *less* than expected, meaning benzene is *more stable* by that amount. So, the extra stability (resonance energy) is $152 \, \text{kJ/mol}$.

This significant difference of $152 \, \text{kJ/mol}$ highlights the enhanced stability of benzene due to the delocalized $\pi$-electron system.

## Possible Questions and Answers

**Q:** Draw the two Kekulé structures of benzene. How did Kekulé switch to a closed structure of the benzene molecule, although other chemists tried open structures for it?

**A:** The two Kekulé structures show alternating single and double bonds within a six-membered carbon ring.

Kekulé's breakthrough to a cyclic structure was reportedly inspired by a *dream* of a snake biting its tail, symbolizing a ring. This imaginative leap allowed him to move beyond the limitations of proposed open-chain structures, which could not explain benzene's observed stability and unusual chemical inertness toward addition reactions characteristic of alkenes.

**Q:** Define resonance in benzene. Why is benzene exceptionally stable than alkenes?

**A:** *Resonance in benzene* refers to the delocalization of its six $\pi$-electrons over all six carbon atoms in the ring. Instead of fixed alternating single and double bonds, the actual structure is a *resonance hybrid* of two contributing Kekulé structures.

Benzene is exceptionally stable compared to alkenes (like a hypothetical 1,3,5-cyclohexatriene) due to this $\pi$-electron delocalization. This enhanced stability is quantified as *resonance energy*. The delocalized electron cloud provides a lower energy state than if the electrons were localized in discrete double bonds, making the molecule less reactive and more stable.

# Reactivity of Benzene: Substitution, Addition, and Oxidation Reactions

Benzene is a unique organic compound characterized by its highly stable delocalized pi ($\pi$) electron ring. This aromatic stability influences its reactivity, leading to distinct types of reactions compared to typical unsaturated hydrocarbons like alkenes. Benzene primarily undergoes electrophilic substitution reactions, but can also participate in addition and oxidation reactions under specific, often vigorous, conditions.

<InlineNoteTag label="Molecular Orbital Treatment of Benzene" notePath="chemistry-12/8.2-structure-of-benzene" />

---

## 8.3.1 Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) Reactions

Electrophilic aromatic substitution reactions are the most characteristic reactions of benzene. They involve an electrophile attacking the high electron density of the delocalized $\pi$-electron ring, resulting in the replacement of a hydrogen atom on the benzene ring by the electrophile. The delocalized $\pi$-electron system is restored in the final product, preserving the aromatic stability.

**General Mechanism of Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution Reactions:**

EAS reactions involve three main steps:

1. **Generation of the Electrophile:** An electrophile ($\mathrm{E}^+$) is generated from a suitable reagent, often with the help of a Lewis acid catalyst (e.g., $\mathrm{FeX_3}$ or $\mathrm{AlX_3}$).

2. **Attack of Electrophile on Benzene (Formation of Arenium Ion):** The $\pi$-electrons of the benzene ring attack the electrophile, forming a positively charged intermediate known as an *arenium ion* (also called a sigma ($\sigma$) complex). In this step, the aromaticity of the benzene ring is temporarily broken.

3. **Removal of Hydrogen (Deprotonation):** A Lewis base (often the conjugate base of the catalyst or the catalyst itself) removes a hydrogen ion ($\mathrm{H}^+$) from the arenium ion. This step restores the delocalized $\pi$-electron system, regenerating the aromatic ring and forming the substituted benzene product. The catalyst is also regenerated.

**Key Information:**
The delocalized $\pi$-electron system in benzene is far more stable than the localized $\pi$-bonds in alkenes. This is why benzene always requires a catalyst for electrophilic substitution reactions, unlike alkenes which readily undergo addition reactions.

---

### Specific Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution Reactions

#### Halogenation

Halogenation is the reaction where a hydrogen atom on the benzene ring is replaced by a halogen atom (e.g., $-\mathrm{Cl}$, $-\mathrm{Br}$). This reaction typically requires a Lewis acid catalyst, such as iron(III) halide ($\mathrm{FeX_3}$) or aluminum halide ($\mathrm{AlX_3}$).

**General Reaction:**
<CaptionedImage src="kg28qr1d4s6wv9hnv9m4g8y4nx8dh6yg" alt="General halogenation reaction of benzene" caption="Figure 8.3.1: General halogenation reaction of benzene." />

**Example: Bromination of Benzene**
When benzene reacts with bromine in the presence of iron(III) bromide ($\mathrm{FeBr_3}$), bromobenzene is formed.
<CaptionedImage src="kg2fqv9m3fcam8w9wnnyxdsdks8dhvf5" alt="Bromination of benzene" caption="Figure 8.3.2: Bromination of benzene to form bromobenzene." />

**Mechanism of Bromination:**

1. **Generation of Electrophile ($\mathrm{Br}^+$):** Bromine ($\mathrm{Br_2}$) acts as a Lewis base, donating electrons to the Lewis acid $\mathrm{FeBr_3}$, generating the highly reactive bromonium ion ($\mathrm{Br}^+$).

2. **Formation of Arenium Ion ($\sigma$-Complex):** The $\pi$-electrons of the benzene ring attack the bromonium ion ($\mathrm{Br}^+$), forming a carbocation intermediate (arenium ion), which has lost its aromaticity temporarily.

3. **Deprotonation/Regeneration of Catalyst:** The $\mathrm{FeBr_4^-}$ ion (a Lewis base) abstracts a proton ($\mathrm{H}^+$) from the arenium ion, restoring the aromatic ring and regenerating the $\mathrm{FeBr_3}$ catalyst.

**Chlorination of Benzene:**
Similar to bromination, chlorination occurs with chlorine gas in the presence of a catalyst like $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$, producing chlorobenzene and hydrogen chloride.

*Mechanism follows the same three steps as bromination, substituting $\mathrm{Br}$ with $\mathrm{Cl}$ and $\mathrm{FeBr_3}$ with $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$.*

**Halogenation of Alkylarenes (e.g., Methylbenzene):**
Alkyl substituents (like methyl groups) are *ring activating* and *ortho/para directing*. They increase electron density at positions 2 (ortho) and 4 (para) on the benzene ring, making these positions more susceptible to electrophilic attack. Hydroxyl ($-OH$) and amino ($-NH_2$) groups similarly activate the ring.

Example: Chlorination of methylbenzene (toluene) produces a mixture of 2-chlorotoluene and 4-chlorotoluene.

In excess chlorine, multiple substitutions can occur, leading to products like 2,4-dichlorotoluene, 2,6-dichlorotoluene, and 2,4,6-trichlorotoluene.

**Side Chain Halogenation:**
In contrast to ring halogenation, if chlorine reacts with an alkyl side chain of an alkylbenzene in the presence of **ultraviolet (UV) light** (or strong sunlight), a free-radical substitution mechanism occurs on the side chain, not the ring.

With excess chlorine, all hydrogen atoms on the methyl side chain can be replaced by chlorine atoms.

---

#### Nitration

Nitration introduces a nitro group ($-\mathrm{NO_2}$) to the benzene ring. The electrophile for this reaction is the nitronium ion ($\mathrm{NO_2^+}$), generated from a mixture of concentrated nitric acid ($\mathrm{HNO_3}$) and concentrated sulfuric acid ($\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$), known as the 'nitrating mixture'. The reaction is typically carried out by refluxing benzene at $25-60^\circ \mathrm{C}$.

**General Reaction:**
<CaptionedImage src="kg233en0mvy78h47qkhg509t958dhpbm" alt="Nitration of benzene" caption="Figure 8.3.3: General nitration reaction of benzene." />

**Mechanism of Nitration:**

1. **Generation of Nitronium Ion ($\mathrm{NO_2^+}$):** Sulfuric acid protonates nitric acid, which then dehydrates to form the nitronium ion.
$ \mathrm{HNO_3 + H_2SO_4 \rightleftharpoons H_2NO_3^+ + HSO_4^-} $
$ \mathrm{H_2NO_3^+ \rightleftharpoons NO_2^+ + H_2O} $
Or more generally:
<CaptionedImage src="kg22rf01tzq12rqt1e8d9reva98dhr3s" alt="Formation of nitronium ion" caption="Figure 8.3.4: Formation of nitronium ion." />

2. **Formation of Arenium Ion ($\sigma$-Complex):** The benzene $\pi$-electrons attack the nitronium ion, forming the arenium ion intermediate. This intermediate is resonance stabilized.

3. **Deprotonation/Regeneration of Catalyst:** A base (typically $\mathrm{HSO_4^-}$) removes a proton from the arenium ion, restoring the aromaticity and forming nitrobenzene. Sulfuric acid is regenerated.

---

#### Friedel-Crafts Reactions

Discovered by Charles Friedel and James Crafts in 1877, these reactions introduce alkyl or acyl groups to the benzene ring, catalyzed by Lewis acids like aluminum chloride ($\mathrm{AlCl_3}$).

**i. Alkylation (Friedel-Crafts Alkylation)**

Introduction of an alkyl group (e.g., methyl, ethyl) into the benzene ring by reacting benzene with a chloroalkane (alkyl halide) in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst like $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$.

**General Reaction:**
<CaptionedImage src="kg20hc989xaxbvdpc1a0f5m2c58dhy90" alt="Friedel-Crafts alkylation" caption="Figure 8.3.5: General Friedel-Crafts alkylation reaction." />

**Example:**
Reaction of benzene with chloromethane to form methylbenzene (toluene).
<CaptionedImage src="kg2drwvpdve96jq0pcj3ds08718dhhdm" alt="Formation of methylbenzene" caption="Figure 8.3.6: Formation of methylbenzene from benzene." />

**Mechanism of Friedel-Crafts Alkylation:**

1. **Generation of Electrophile ($\mathrm{R}^+$):** The alkyl halide reacts with the Lewis acid catalyst ($\mathrm{AlCl_3}$) to generate a carbocation (alkyl cation), which acts as the electrophile.
Example: Generation of methyl cation ($\mathrm{CH_3^+}$).
$ \mathrm{CH_3Cl + AlCl_3 \rightarrow CH_3^+ + [AlCl_4]^-} $

2. **Formation of Arenium Ion:** The benzene $\pi$-electrons attack the alkyl cation, forming the arenium ion. This ion is resonance stabilized.

3. **Deprotonation/Regeneration of Catalyst:** The $\mathrm{[AlCl_4]^-}$ ion removes a proton from the arenium ion, restoring aromaticity and regenerating $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$.
$ \mathrm{[AlCl_4]^- + H^+ \rightarrow AlCl_3 + HCl} $

**ii. Acylation (Friedel-Crafts Acylation)**

Introduction of an acyl group ($\mathrm{R-CO-}$) into the benzene ring by reacting benzene with an acyl halide ($\mathrm{RCOCl}$) in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst ($\mathrm{AlX_3}$). The product is a phenyl ketone.

**Mechanism of Friedel-Crafts Acylation:**

4. **Generation of Electrophile (Acylium Ion):** The acyl halide reacts with the Lewis acid catalyst ($\mathrm{AlCl_3}$) to generate a resonance-stabilized acylium ion ($\mathrm{R-C \equiv O^+}$), which is the electrophile.
$ \mathrm{RCOCl + AlCl_3 \rightarrow R-C \equiv O^+ + [AlCl_4]^-} $

5. **Formation of Arenium Ion:** The benzene $\pi$-electrons attack the acylium ion, forming the arenium ion intermediate.

6. **Deprotonation/Regeneration of Catalyst:** A base ($\mathrm{[AlCl_4]^-}$) removes a proton from the arenium ion, restoring aromaticity and forming the phenyl ketone. $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$ is regenerated.
$ \mathrm{[AlCl_4]^- + H^+ \rightarrow AlCl_3 + HCl} $

**Key Information:**
Aluminum chloride ($\mathrm{AlCl_3}$) is often referred to as a *halogen carrier* in Friedel-Crafts reactions because it helps generate the electrophilic halogen species.

## 8.3.2 Addition Reactions

Benzene, despite its apparent unsaturation, is generally *reluctant* to undergo addition reactions under normal conditions. This is due to the inherent stability of its delocalized $\pi$-electron aromatic system. Breaking this stable system requires harsh conditions (high temperature, high pressure, strong reagents).

---

### Hydrogenation

Hydrogenation is the addition of hydrogen molecules across the carbon atoms of the benzene ring, converting it into cyclohexane. This reaction breaks the aromaticity and requires specific conditions:

- **Catalysts:** Nickel ($\mathrm{Ni}$), Platinum ($\mathrm{Pt}$), or Palladium ($\mathrm{Pd}$)
- **Temperature:** $150-200^\circ \mathrm{C}$
- **Pressure:** $20-30 \, \mathrm{atm}$

**General Reaction:**
$ \mathrm{C_6H_6 + 3H_2 \xrightarrow{Ni, 150-200^\circ C, 20-30 atm} C_6H_{12}} $

**Role of Catalyst:** The catalyst provides a surface area that breaks hydrogen molecules into individual hydrogen atoms. It also interacts with the benzene molecule, weakening its $\pi$-electron system and making it more reactive. Six hydrogen atoms then add to the six carbon atoms of the benzene molecule, forming cyclohexane and releasing it from the catalyst surface.

**Example:** Hydrogenation of methylbenzene yields methylcyclohexane under similar conditions.
<CaptionedImage src="kg235gdhe7bz8363bc79wfpm918dhp5x" alt="Hydrogenation of methylbenzene" caption="Figure 8.3.7: Hydrogenation of methylbenzene to methylcyclohexane." />

---

### Difference Between Addition Reactions and Electrophilic Substitution Reactions

The fundamental difference lies in the fate of the intermediate carbocation formed after the initial electrophilic attack:

| Feature | Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution | Addition Reactions (e.g., Alkenes) |
| :------------------------ | :---------------------------------- | :--------------------------------- |
| **Initial Attack** | Electrophile attacks $\pi$-system | Electrophile attacks $\pi$-system |
| **Intermediate** | Arenium ion (retains some unsaturation) | Bridged halonium ion or carbocation (localized positive charge) |
| **Second Step** | **Loss of $\mathrm{H^+}$** to restore aromaticity | **Attack by nucleophile** to form a saturated product |
| **Aromaticity** | **Restored** in the final product | **Destroyed** (if aromatic) or leads to *saturated* product |
| **Product** | **Substituted** aromatic compound | **Adduct** (saturated compound) |
| **Conditions for Benzene** | Requires **catalyst**, relatively mild | Requires **harsh conditions** (high T/P, strong reagents) |

---

## 8.3.3 Oxidation Reactions

Benzene and other alkanes are generally resistant to oxidation by common oxidizing agents. However, they can be oxidized under certain conditions. In alkylbenzenes, the aliphatic side chain is often more susceptible to oxidation than the aromatic ring itself, especially under prolonged heating or strong oxidizing conditions.

The presence of the benzene ring *activates* the alkyl side chain towards oxidation. For instance, while alkanes are not easily oxidized by potassium manganate(VII), the methyl side-chain of methylbenzene is readily oxidized by hot alkaline or hot acidic potassium manganate(VII) ($\mathrm{KMnO_4}$) to produce benzoic acid.

The oxidation typically targets the carbon atom of the alkyl side-chain closest to the benzene ring (the benzylic carbon), converting it into a carboxylic acid group ($-\mathrm{COOH}$). Longer alkyl chains will have the benzylic carbon oxidized to a carboxylic acid, and other carbons may be oxidized to carbon dioxide.

**Example: Oxidation of Methylbenzene to Benzoic Acid**
<CaptionedImage src="kg2ekw3d5xwtv8c3s5eam2vknn8dh0bz" alt="Oxidation of methylbenzene" caption="Figure 8.3.8: Oxidation of methylbenzene to benzoic acid." />

For alkyl chains longer than methyl, all carbon atoms in the side chain, except the benzylic carbon (which forms the carboxylic acid group), are typically oxidized to carbon dioxide ($\mathrm{CO_2}$).

**Mechanism of Methylbenzene Oxidation to Benzoic Acid:**

The mechanism involves a series of free-radical and carbocation intermediates:

1. **Hydrogen Abstraction & Benzyl Cation Formation:** Hot alkaline $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ abstracts a benzylic hydrogen atom as a free radical, forming a benzyl radical. This radical then reacts with an odd-electron intermediate of $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ to form a benzyl cation.

2. **Hydroxyl Group Addition:** The benzyl cation abstracts a hydroxyl group ($-OH$) from potassium hydrogenmanganate, forming an unstable alcohol.

3. **Further Oxidation:** The alkaline $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ continues to oxidize the benzylic carbon, first to a diol, then to benzaldehyde, and finally to benzoic acid.

# 8.4 Electrophilic Substitution in Substituted Arenes

When a benzene ring already carries a substituent, that substituent influences **both the rate** and **the position** of any further electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) reaction.

---

## The Sigma Complex (Arenium Ion)

In every EAS reaction the electrophile ($E^+$) attacks the $\pi$-electron cloud of the ring to form a **sigma complex** (also called an arenium ion or Wheland intermediate). This is a carbocation in which one carbon is $sp^3$-hybridised and the positive charge is delocalised over the remaining five carbons. A base then removes a proton to restore aromaticity.

$\text{ArH} + E^+ \longrightarrow [\text{Ar}(H)(E)]^+ \xrightarrow{-H^+} \text{ArE}$

## Directing Effects of Substituents

### Electron-Donating Groups (EDGs), Activating, Ortho/Para Directors

Groups such as $-OH$, $-OR$, $-NH_2$, $-NHR$, $-CH_3$, and $-C_2H_5$ donate electron density into the ring by **resonance** or **hyperconjugation**. This:

- **Activates** the ring (faster than benzene)
- Directs the electrophile to the **ortho** and **para** positions

The sigma complex formed at ortho/para positions is more stable because the positive charge can be delocalised onto the carbon bearing the EDG, which then stabilises it further.

### Electron-Withdrawing Groups (EWGs), Deactivating, Meta Directors

Groups such as $-NO_2$, $-CHO$, $-COR$, $-COOH$, $-CN$, and $-SO_3H$ withdraw electron density from the ring by **inductive** and/or **resonance** effects. This:

- **Deactivates** the ring (slower than benzene)
- Directs the electrophile to the **meta** position

At the meta position the positive charge in the sigma complex is never placed on the carbon bearing the EWG, making it the least destabilised option.

### Halogens, Deactivating but Ortho/Para Directors

Halogens ($-F$, $-Cl$, $-Br$, $-I$) show a dual character:

| Effect | Direction | Result |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| Inductive (electronegativity) | Withdraws $\sigma$ electrons | Deactivates ring |
| Resonance (lone pairs) | Donates into ring at o/p | Directs to ortho/para |

Overall the ring is **less reactive** than benzene, but substitution still occurs predominantly at the **ortho** and **para** positions.

**Example:** Bromination of chlorobenzene gives mainly o-bromochlorobenzene and p-bromochlorobenzene.

## Predicting Major Products

| Substituent | Type | Position |
|-------------|------|----------|
| $-OH$, $-NH_2$, $-OR$ | Activating | ortho/para |
| $-CH_3$, alkyl | Activating | ortho/para |
| $-Cl$, $-Br$ | Deactivating | ortho/para |
| $-NO_2$, $-CHO$, $-COOH$ | Deactivating | meta |

**Example:** Nitration of nitrobenzene gives **1,3-dinitrobenzene** because $-NO_2$ is a meta-director.

## Electrophilic Substitution Reactions of Phenol (SLO C-12-E-27)

Phenol ($C_6H_5OH$) is highly activated toward EAS because the $-OH$ group donates electron density into the ring by resonance, making the ortho and para positions electron-rich.

### a. Reaction with NaOH(aq)

Phenol reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide as a weak acid:

$C_6H_5OH + NaOH(aq) \longrightarrow C_6H_5O^-Na^+(aq) + H_2O$

Product: **sodium phenoxide** (sodium phenate)

### b. Reaction with Na(s)

Phenol reacts with sodium metal more vigorously than water:

$2\,C_6H_5OH + 2\,Na(s) \longrightarrow 2\,C_6H_5O^-Na^+ + H_2(g)$

Products: **sodium phenoxide** and **hydrogen gas**

### c. Reaction with Diazonium Salts in NaOH(aq), Azo Coupling

In alkaline solution, phenol exists as the phenoxide ion ($C_6H_5O^-$), which is an even stronger activator. It couples with a diazonium salt ($ArN_2^+$) at the para position to give a brightly coloured **azo compound**:

$C_6H_5O^- + ArN_2^+ \longrightarrow Ar-N=N-C_6H_4-OH + \text{(coloured azo dye)}$

Azo compounds contain the $-N=N-$ chromophore and are used as dyes.

### d. Nitration with Dilute HNO₃ at Room Temperature

Unlike benzene (which requires concentrated $HNO_3$ / concentrated $H_2SO_4$ at 55 °C), phenol reacts with **dilute $HNO_3$ at room temperature** to give a mixture of:

- **2-nitrophenol** (ortho product)
- **4-nitrophenol** (para product)

$C_6H_5OH + HNO_3(\text{dil}) \xrightarrow{\text{r.t.}} \text{2-nitrophenol} + \text{4-nitrophenol}$

The milder conditions are needed because the $-OH$ group makes the ring far more reactive than benzene.

### e. Bromination with Br₂(aq), No Catalyst Required

Phenol reacts instantly with bromine water at room temperature **without any catalyst** (unlike benzene which needs $FeBr_3$):

$C_6H_5OH + 3\,Br_2(aq) \longrightarrow 2,4,6\text{-tribromophenol}\downarrow + 3\,HBr$

All three ortho and para positions are brominated. **2,4,6-tribromophenol** forms as a white precipitate. This reaction is used as a test for phenol.
